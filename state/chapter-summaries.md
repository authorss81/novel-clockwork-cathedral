# Chapter Summaries

Two to five sentences per chapter. Keep the newest two or three chapters fully available for voice continuity. A compact volume index is at the end; the fourteen-volume index is maintained in `state/continuity.md`.

## Volume 01, Batch 0001 — Chapters 1–10

**Fixed calendar established on the page** (batch 0001 must not contradict it): Chapter 1 is **Monday the 15th**; Chapter 2 the 16th; Chapters 3, 4 and 5 all the **17th** (the Reading is filed at ten past two and Perry Vance dies that night); Chapter 6 the 18th; Chapter 7 the 19th; Chapter 8 the 20th; Chapter 9 the night of the 20th into the 21st; Chapter 10 the 24th, one week after the death. The Cathedral certificate Orrin signs is dated **the 15th**, two days before the Reading. Orrin's class-three renewal is **about eight weeks** after the 15th and does not fall inside Volume 01.

## Chapter 1 — The Extra Beat

Orrin signs a Continuity Office hourline inspection authority left at his bench (Tobias Rill inspects, Halden Rusk's office appoints him), certifies the Cathedral's crown-case public clock for a clerk who will not take his name, and is refused nothing and offered no welcome. At the ropeworks a foreman takes a worker's minute and hands her an hour token, and Orrin says nothing because he is not her employer. He works a hospital ward clock, a school bell and a market scale, and in the Cathedral's clock — correct twice on his gauge — hears eight beats and then a ninth pulse arriving from below, like water returning to a pipe. He signs a clean certificate dated the 15th, tells nobody, and writes the ninth beat in his private grey ledger under a date.

## Chapter 2 — Non-Certified Standby

Orrin takes the anomaly to the guild hall hoping to file an ordinary defect note, and Gerren Hask never says no: he describes the fee, the letter, and the Quarter's reputation, and tells him the clock is right and that a building keeping its own accounts dislikes being told what it is carrying. Under Hask's elbow, open, the hall's labour book shows one line in a copying clerk's hand — *Weir Two, night pumping, non-certified standby, 1 crew, 3 nights* — and Orrin does not ask about it. Ivo Pell, a twelve-year operator at the licensing counter, calls a bench man's ear a trade habit, not an instrument, and leaves. On the wall is the Long Nine crew photograph, uncaptioned beyond the night and a shift number, with Edwin Vale somewhere in twenty-two faces and no one to say which. Hask produces a governor stopped mid-travel on purpose, with a bar's jaw marks on it, and asks Orrin to reduce it to unrecorded scrap; Orrin refuses to scrap a regulator with a cut pin and takes it home instead.

## Chapter 3 — The Hand Valve

Called out to Weir Two, Orrin finds the certified clock four minutes fast and a pump that is not failing: its stop logic assumes a hand valve is shut, and the valve is open because a man opened it in June and wrote nothing down. Bevin Sarr keeps the crew's own hours on a chalk board; the guild's line for that work is *non-certified standby*. Perry Vance, forty-one, twenty years on the nights, unlicensed and cheerfully unresentful, arrives, and Orrin tells him the stage will run past midnight if the head keeps rising. Vance goes down, shuts the valve in daylight, chalks *hand shut* on the board, and then points out that the board and the clock both say the valve was already shut, and the machine has been wrong about it for twenty years. The ninth beat arrives a second and last time through the pump house's own line: a shove with a count in it that keeps going past the tick and leans on something he cannot name or locate — no picture, no night, no word, and no cost — so that he understands he has had it twice in two days under two machines doing their jobs perfectly, and there is nothing to write down either time. It is not a prophecy and not a preview of the night, and it is never fused with the Aftershock that arrives for the first time in Chapter 5. Vance mentions his daughter's six o'clock tram and warns him not to write the night shift in.

## Chapter 4 — No Unseen Cost

At the Question Hall, Orrin files as a private citizen, names the paying district — the Clockwork Quarter, the district with the most reserve and the least right to be charged — and watches the spindle reject a moral question with *QUESTION NOT ACCEPTED. NO NAMED ACT.* Ivo Pell takes the operator station, Ostrand the record station, and the affected-district station is filled by **Alma Festinger**, 56, of Green Span, on a public-health rota, because the Weirs have no standing; she signs because she was asked, wearing a witness bond she does not understand. The single Reading notice of the batch appears: the trace holds Ferrywright Bridge, the pressure says the second stage will not stop before midnight and the hospital's night clock slips with it, the blind margin reads *unregistered night work is not represented* in a footer half the size of the signature, and the toll is three district minutes and a witness interval. The countersignature carries **Anja Voss's** name and nothing else. On the Toll Stair the chalk reads *3 — Clockwork Quarter*, and nobody can see from the notice who paid.

## Chapter 5 — The Pump That Should Have Stopped

The afternoon after the Reading is empty and Orrin tries to find what the Loom left out by listening at his own bench, and can hear everything within reach and nothing at all about the omitted shift, because his sense is blind to whatever the network does not carry. At dusk at Weir Two the hand valve is open again, driven open by the opened gate and the running second stage, and there is no water record of when. He puts a hand on the stage, asks the question in plain words, and gets Aftershock for the first time: a weight leaning, a whole night, a man going down a ladder — a load and not a beat, the first time he has asked about something that had not happened yet or about a man, and unlike the ninth beat it costs him. He tells the watchman and the watchman is right to refuse him — no licence for that machine, no proposal, no shift time, no name, no number — and he sends nine coppers to the Hall with a boy and the Hall is shut. The crew comes on at seven, four of them, and nobody is told anything. Vance goes down on his own initiative to shut the valve the model said was shut, and the pump that should have stopped does not stop. At dawn Bevin Sarr tells him the valve was open and the stop never came, and the fifth name on her board is a boy who was two streets over, which is what let four look like a miscount instead of a removal. A clerk pins the Directorate's notice on the door: every word of it is true. Orrin's account of his own guilt is that he had the information and no instrument that turns information into an action somebody else can take; he is not the cause and is not let off.

## Chapter 6 — The One Minute He Cannot Account For

Teo Arden, twenty, Vance's apprentice, up since Tuesday, has the better account of the night and the correct suspicion, and tells Orrin flatly that three minutes is the price of the bridge, against an hour a week that Perry was never paid for. Orrin admits he filed the question and keeps back the ninth tick and the regulator. At the bench, writing the evening down, Orrin finds a minute with clean edges and nothing in the middle, produces two complete and incompatible accounts of it, and writes that he cannot say whether he read the stage or imagined the reading. In a Green Span corridor Leda Vale shows him a health slip recording a tram driver's lost interval as *calibration strain* with no name, no duration and no date, and names the guild's separate phrase, *self-inflicted occupational strain*, for a clockmaker who listens without a witness. They have the book's first real fight, both quoting their mother, and he still does not tell her about the tick. Perry Vance goes into the private ledger as the first person in it. On the six o'clock tram, Ada Vance, thirteen, asks him one question through a closed window and he answers it uselessly, and lets the tram take her round the corner. A clerk from the Office of Public Reckoning arrives at ten past six with a toll receipt and a question about a street clock in his own district.

## Chapter 7 — Three Minutes

Mara Venn of the Office of Public Reckoning audits the Toll Stair and nothing else, and meets Orrin at a street clock on Halloway Street that is five minutes slow where the file says three, which she attributes to a hand correction, the way everybody does. In her office she has his clean certificate dated the 15th, his filed question, and the receipt. The deepest resistance is that nothing he felt at Weir Two is a document: his feeling has no paper because the paper requires him to certify a district toll he has no licence to certify. She names the rule that lets a Reading be charged to any district in the city, files an objection on the receipt on the ground that the paying district was neither affected nor consenting, and it is accepted for filing, which changes nothing. Orrin gives her the one checkable fact — the blind margin's exact wording and its size against the signature — and she has measured it at eleven to the point against twenty-two. She quotes the rule about *calibration strain* in a tone with a whole room behind it and refuses to explain. Then she notices he cannot account for a full minute of a conversation he clearly had, and her reaction is professional fear, not scepticism. The Weir Two night roster goes upstairs and comes back in one courteous line: *referred to the sealed archive.* The Archive of Unlisted is named.

## Chapter 8 — The Form Number

Sindri Lake, keeper of the public Archive room, is helpful, precise and immovable, and shows Orrin that Weir Two's public record holds a maintenance line, a period, a quantity of hours and no names, because the record's purpose is the hours. The public index also records every transfer *out* of the open record, because a hole must be distinguishable from a lie. The Weir Two batch: transferred eighteen years ago, forty-one entries, on guild request form GH-9, standing withdrawal, maintenance-deferred class, countersigned for the district by the pumping warden of the Lower Weirs, deputy, **S. Kade** — a name Orrin has never heard. A guild price schedule prices the removal at two shillings the head for a year, which is what Perry Vance's night work cost the hall, and the routing card copies went to the hall, the ward, the Continuity Office inspection file, and a Green Span public-health rota, where the attendance line reads *FESTINGER, A.*, and nobody in the room connects it. Lake will not hold testimony. In Wenna Pruitt's kitchen over a chandler's shop, the tenant-council organiser who has kept the pumping district's shift book for eleven years states the case plainly: Orrin has the paper and not the person, and a district that produces the hours and cannot produce a signature is not going to be believed this century. She asks the question he cannot answer — whose name goes on the form that says there was no crew. On the way out the Weirs' clocks slip out of phase, the slip crosses the Green Span hospital line, and the only reserve that could absorb it is not there.

## Chapter 9 — What the Ward Can Absorb

The Weirs' clocks repeat and skip and the hospital line goes with them, and the cause is not a retroactive toll out of the Reading — three minutes and a witness interval is all that Reading charged — but the ordinary late arrival of a district that has been running twenty years on hours it does not have. Orrin states the three reasons there is no Reading tonight, out loud, in order, with Sel Kade and Teo and Mara in the room: a model of conditions that change by the minute, a witness rail booked a day ahead with no district resident to fill the third station, and a fee that would come out of the reserve that has failed. Sel Kade defends the pump reserve she moved to Carrow Street in the spring: seventy-one people, and it is true, and it is exactly the moral shape the book is about. Leda Vale brings the ward's arithmetic — forty minutes of slack, six hours of free-running ward clocks, two hours of it unbudgeted — and makes it a method condition: if her ward is going to be the number he spend, say so in a room before you spend it, in front of the people whose hours they are. Orrin names Aftershock aloud, a tape measure confirms him, Teo's crew and Marta Dey and Bevin Sarr wind and hold the rate all night, and a sacrificial plate goes on the bracket with a name on it because a ward that names its plate can find out later who owns it. The clocks hold; the trestle's failing load is exposed in daylight on the back of a shutter; a second, larger keeper's toll, taken without warning and unasked for, takes his right hand for two days and leaves the ringing in his right ear as a condition. Teo does not thank him and calls it an audit.

## Chapter 10 — The First Demand

Mara drafts a two-clause demand in the Office: first, that every Reading's toll be entered in the Assembly's record with the paying district named, because the Toll Stair is chalk scrubbed every morning and is not a record; second, that body tolls be recorded with a name and a duration, which is the Compact's fourth safeguard read literally and has never been done. She warns him that a published ledger will panic four Green Span wards, that some of the people in it did not ask, and she gives him the sentence that will kill it: *who pays for the record.* In the Assembly's public-health committee, Councilor Imani Rook blocks both clauses with real arithmetic — sixty-one calibration-strain entries in one committee's files in a year, scaled to the low thousands, and no estimate for the transcription — and Orrin has no answer and is not equal in the room: he has no seat, the affected district sent nobody, and the paying district sent nobody because why would it. The demand is referred to committee with a date in the spring, read twice, entered in the minutes, discharged. Alma Festinger is asked in the gallery by a clerk collecting names whether she understood what she signed at the rail, says no, means it, and has it written down; nobody follows it up. Sel Kade states on a public street that she moved the reserve in March, for seventy-one people, and would move it again. At the bench Orrin stops writing Vance as a shift and writes him as a man, copies the three minutes charged beside the five minutes the street clock was out, and writes that the two numbers do not agree and that he cannot say why. He writes the rule at the top of his own ledger: nothing of mine is used again without its blind margin and its toll named in public, in a room, to somebody who can object. On the way home he hears the trestle repair spoken of as Saturday night's work, and writes the ledger entry he made in the small hours of Sunday beside the numbers he cannot reconcile. The hall's reply to the Office arrives on the noon post, courteous and true: the hall keeps a certification ledger, which is a record of machines, and does not keep a record of hours for work that is not part of a certified shift, and form GH-9 is a request form and not a record, and the hall's price schedules are the hall's own property. He sets the form number down on a clean page in a hand that does not shake, because it is the first time in three days that his hand has been entirely steady.

## Volume 01, Batch 0002 — Chapters 11–20

Both dates are in batch 0003, and the licence is renewed rather than lost, in Chapter 27; no chapter may describe either as imminent before its own day.**

## Chapter 11 — The Honest Man With a Form

Tobias Rill, 44, Continuity Office inspector of hourlines, is halfway through his quarterly round in the Lower Weirs with a reserve gauge he cannot use his licence to open a case with, and Orrin discovers that the Office's entire authority is a four-inch column headed *within tolerance*: Rill certifies that a line agrees with its own record, not that the district's clocks show the right time, so the chandler's window at eleven minutes out is *correct* and the line is *consistent*. Asked for the certified complement of the 17th's shift, Rill can only write **four**, from the works' return, because a form that records a discrepancy has to weigh two things of the same kind, and a guild labour book and a painted chalk board are not two of a kind. The fifth name cannot go on the form at all: a non-certified boy has no line in a form that can only enter certified men, so the one name that proves the book is wrong is the one name Rill has no way of writing down. He offers the one honest door — an enclosure, which goes into a file nobody reads and is *furniture*, not a lie and not a record — and closes the file with a finding that is complete and useless, and tells Orrin that to get a record he needs a person who can sign one, and there is nobody in that district who can. Rill also mentions, without emphasis, that the Office files everything it does and the files are public on petition, a week, counted from the day after, the box emptied at noon and never on a Sunday. Orrin's own sense is not used: the second stage is sound, it says *it holds*, and he could have got that from the gauge, and it costs him nothing to learn that. Waiting at the bench under the brass bearing weight is the renewal card: a printed hall form, not a letter, with a fee, a date, a master's endorsement, and a standing request for the holder's work book.

## Chapter 12 — What the Form Wants

Hask offers no threat. He asks for the work book, explains that the hall *cannot* take a man's private book and would resign before signing that, and then explains the other half just as plainly: from today, the only record of what a maintainer has found in any office in the city is one the hall writes, and the hall form W-4 has three columns and has never had a fourth for *I am not sure*. He adds the one weapon nobody expects: an **optional** interview, Tuesday the 28th, at which nothing follows, and which Orrin cannot decline without giving a reason, because declining is itself a line in a file. Orrin gives the first lie of the batch without noticing he has given it — Hask asks whether he has taken work off the board, and Orrin says yes, the trestle, and certificates nothing, and the two words he does not say are the whole of his answer. In Mara's office he learns the Office's non-refusal — the certified shift returns came back *received and noted* in six days, pointing him at the hall — and that she lodged the Continuity Office petition on the 20th, the day of the Archive, without being asked, and that it takes eleven days and not a fortnight. He says *keep it* about the grey ledger, which is the decision the third rule in Chapter 20 exists to undo, and Mara puts the word *now* on it.

## Chapter 13 — Arithmetic

Imani Rook files her own demand on the Monday, three chapters after blocking one, and says in the chamber that she was shown arithmetic and not persuaded of anything. The arithmetic is the pumping district's own returns for the four months to the end of last quarter — 1,412 hours worked at a civic water station, kept by the district in a rent book and a chalk board, against a certified shift return of **nil** — which turns the low thousands she gave on the 24th from a rate into the bottom of a column with nothing under it. The chapter's structural rhyme is exact: the sentence that killed the demand on the 24th was *who pays for the record*, and Rook answers it by putting a ward clerk in for two days a week for six weeks at **£4 10s** out of the Green Span public-health clerical allocation, in an estimates line headed *district returns, exceptional*, where four of five Green Span delegates will vote against it. She keeps the name-and-duration clause **out**, because she does not know the size of the population the column would describe, and she says why in one sentence: a person's lost attention in a public book beside a district's return will be read as the cost of it, and four Green Span wards will be told what their bodies are worth by a chalk board. The demand is referred to committee with a return after the fourth sitting, the six returns are ordered printed in full with the minutes, it is read twice, entered, and discharged — forty-one minutes against nine. Mara speaks once from the gallery, is refused, and sits down on purpose so that Orrin will not try.

## Chapter 14 — Behind a Closed Door

Della Marsh, 61, thirty-one years as the guild's secretary, shuts the door of the eight-by-eleven series room, makes him state the number, and then does the thing nobody has asked her to do: she turns up the **copybook**, the hall's working duplicate of the form-number series, which is a list of *sites* and batches and head counts and has no column for a man and never intended one. It carries nine series, and the Weir Two line has been copied eleven times by a clerk who did not know what a standby was. She names four — Weir Two, a Northglass boardinghouse stair, a pumping station in the Sloughs, and a hospital generator she will not attribute — and then tells Orrin the sentence that turns discovery into dread: a hall with a form number and a price schedule and nine districts is not a crime, it is a *practice*, and practices do not get exposed, they get a column. He has just made eight districts' worth of people into people who are on the same page as a man who drowned. She names the fee — **four shillings in coin, no receipt**, on her own rule, a week after the asking, and neither of them will know the other's face by the year's end — and then, free, gives him three doors to the labour book: the Office requisitions it and waits fourteen months; a licensed maintainer may copy it but must sign the inside cover and be found in eleven weeks; and **a person with no licence, no form and no name to write on a line may read a book off an open shelf and declare nothing, because declaring requires a form.** She also tells him, in one flat sentence, that nobody has thanked her for anything in thirty-one years, and that if anything happens to him the only copy of a month of his district dies with his shop.

## Chapter 15 — Supper, and the Rule

The Green Span staff kitchen from four until five, the hour the day staff take, and the rule is priced rather than upheld. Leda says the trestle worked *because* nine people could object and four of them did, so the rule is not worthless — and then the true version: the rule puts the people who can object in the room and the people who pay outside it, and outside it is where everybody who cannot fight for themselves is, every time, and it is not a coincidence. The worth of a rule, she says, is the argument. Orrin can finally give her the copybook in a form she can hold — one form number, one price, one line, and four places on a page — and she does the thing he does not expect: she writes it on the back of a ward roster and posts it on the duty board in the nurses' room, unsigned and undated, where patients' families read, on the reasoning that with a name on it this is a complaint by a nurse and without one it is a notice that somebody can be asked a question. Then she names the lamp. She was nine, in the chapel school on Weir Lane, about sixty people, bells stopped, she counted, her aunt came at four with a basket, their mother three years dead. She does not know what their father did, has never pretended, and has spent eighteen years being carefully not asked — and tells Orrin that his not-telling is *not a kindness*, that she would have come, and that she cannot hold a lamp for something she does not know is happening. He gets it afterwards, in a tram at ten past five: the reason a private certainty is useless is not that it cannot be proved, it is that a person who is not told cannot refuse.

## Chapter 16 — Deferred, for Cost

The Continuity Office file on a table with no fire, and the volume's argument changes shape: the omission is not only a moral instrument, it is a **maintenance policy with a price schedule**, and the volume's climax can therefore be about money. The hospital trestle's conduit bracket was surveyed two years and two months ago — two of four fixings withdrawn, span dropped three-eighths, third bracket of an obsolete pattern, estimate **£2 14s 6d** including a new pattern off the original drawing — and deferred twice to *the district works programme, next triennium*, in a lighter hand, lawfully and correctly. Its routing card went to the same four places as the Weir Two batch, including a public-health rota, and nobody ever joins them because there is nothing to join. The same volume also holds batch 21/4 with **S. Kade's** countersignature, and Orrin has it in his hands and does not tell her, and gives himself a reason on the stair that Mara identifies as a good reason with a door in it — and that door is the third rule in Chapter 20. In the corridor, once, and unresolved: Mara wants the deferral, because a priced item in a budget is the only document in this case that can be attacked without attacking a man, and the copybook is nine districts on a form and asks a chamber to guess at a motive; Orrin wants the copybook, because the bracket is a piece of iron and Perry Vance is the only thing there is. They agree about the bracket. Mara's method is nine years of waiting and one sentence she is ashamed of, and her plan is three years, a bored chamber, and a clerk who asks what's the form number.

## Chapter 17 — A Dead Pump, a Timer, and a Joint

Practice, and the discovery is that a **narrow question is payable**. A seized second-stage cistern pump in a Northglass yard, asked one named question, answers the second-stage gland and costs ninety seconds with clean edges, which he enters in the book as a *reading cost* and not a keeper's toll, with the reason written under it: *a toll is what happens when you ask something you cannot pay for.* A hospital interval timer that is losing four and a half minutes a round and is not tired at all, whose click is set a quarter of an inch too deep, fixed with a thumbnail — and a ward sister who is owed the click at two o'clock and gets the second will be at twenty to twelve, which is the real price of asking too much. Then the third floor, the ward clock by the door, and a real corridor: a small true answer about a woman called by the pharmacy at about four, and behind it the weight of the whole floor, and Orrin **stops**, deliberately, having said in Chapter 19's terms that nobody would know, and it costs him eleven minutes with clean edges in a corridor he was standing in and a right hand that will not close properly until Monday. Ferrywright Bridge on the Sunday with no water, no load and nobody waiting, gives him **nothing at all**, and the reason is mechanical and not mystical: a joint with nothing resting on it has nothing to lean against. Ivo Pell comes to the bench about a shellac stop under a locking plate, finds it in four minutes, is right and contemptuous once — a bench man's ear is not an instrument, a man who practises on a dead pump is behind his trade and a man who practises on a live one is a public hazard — offers no lesson, takes no interest, and says *don't* on his way out. Orrin adds a second rule: *ask one thing, about a named load, and know before you ask what it will cost.*

## Chapter 18 — Rumour With Page Numbers

Eleven copies of the hall's labour book go out in a plain envelope on Monday morning and come back inside a day, and the volume's argument turns on its own pivot. Hanna Stroud, 54, nine blocks in the Weirs, pays her own renters on the first and has never been two months late, and is *not* a villain: she takes the paper to her insurer on Monday morning, is quoted about ninety pounds a year on a block on Carrow Street, and therefore takes a Michaelmas rise off that block only, on the strength of the hall's own labour book and nothing else. **Thirty-one households do not get the rise they were told they were getting**, and the district's own arithmetic is indistinguishable from a lie. Wenna Pruitt and Teo Arden are both right about their own half and both wrong about the other's, and Hester Lomas, the hall copyist who made the copies over three nights and lost nineteen years for it — and points out that no rule forbids any of it — puts the whole argument in one sentence and then names the one copy that went to the man who sells the Advertiser's front page. Teo does not apologise to Orrin because Orrin did not send it. Leda rings the shop at eleven at night, tells him about a discharge pressure on a third-floor pump, tells him she is not asking for anything, and makes him notice the difference. He ends on Ferrywright Bridge doing the sum: *a fact nobody can see is a rumour* and *a district that can prove its own labour is a district that can be invoiced for it*, both true, both eleven years old, and neither of them about the Weirs.

## Chapter 19 — The Pump He Could Not Certify

The batch climax, and the failure is quiet and complete. On a hospital landing Orrin does exactly what he did on the pump house floor in Chapter 9 — names what he knows, what he does not, which part is a guess, and what it costs — and this time nobody can take their coats off. The gland will go sideways at about two hours' head; the pump is on the Lower Weirs pumping main, so the working window is a **local toll**, and a class-three licence may certify the machine and not the window; Green Span cannot get a standing on the Weirs' main; the pumping ward's reserve was spent in March on Carrow Street; the rail is booked a day ahead and the Cathedral does not sit one on a Sunday; the Office writes back in four lines with *no further correspondence on this matter* in the corner in small type. The only instrument that would hold the main open in time is certifying a local toll he has no standing to certify, and he says out loud, before he feels like deciding it, that he is not going to do it. He writes the five items down, gives the page to Mara, certifies the pump — nine minutes for a class-three certificate, plus nine minutes on the *reverse*, which nobody reads, which is the deal and not the reason — and Ellen Reeve packs the gland properly and the ward runs on three clocks that all disagree. Teo shims the gland on Sunday morning with a length of tram-line bond in eight courses, painted, signed underneath the eighth, illegally, and correctly, and it holds for five weeks; the ward still has a morning in which two medicines arrive four minutes apart. Leda's corridor scene is the hardest thing she says in the volume: he kept his rule, the rule worked, and it took eleven weeks and four letters and an inquiry to fail, while a girl of nineteen on nights does the ward clock's work by hand — *don't you dare do it on your own* — and he understands that the third keeper's toll was taken because he wanted to act and did not: thirty-eight minutes gone, and the permanent loss of the thing that made him his trade: the judgement, not the hearing. He can still hear a watch at thirty feet in a still room and has tested it twice since the Tuesday; what he cannot any longer do is tell by the sound alone whether a movement is running true, and nothing had been strained, so it is not coming back. This is a precision loss and is deliberately not a range loss, so that Volume 08 and Volume 14 both keep their costs. He certifies nothing, commits no offence, and the last line is that nothing, anywhere, in any place anybody could read.

## Chapter 20 — What the Ledger Says About Him

Recovery, and the reading-back. Four ordinary days, and he makes the sum properly for the first time: **three minutes charged and displayed, five found on a street, two accounted for in no document in the city**, and now two of his own — eleven minutes on a lane in the Weirs and eight at the Ferrywright corner, both with a bar and no receipt and no form, and a district that cannot prove either of them. The hospital's porter's hall clock is the best movement in the district, is right, and is nobody's reference. Leda comes into the shop for the first time in her life, asks to see the book, and reads a hundred and nine pages in four weeks: three are a bad click, one a bracket, one a Northglass regulator, nine are the pillar clock on Halloway Street and the two minutes, and thirty-eight of them are about other people's hours and not about machines at all. **The book has stopped being about machines**, and he had not noticed. She finds the one entry in it that is his own fault — the Continuity Office page, where he had S. Kade's eighteen-year-old countersignature in his hands in a room and did not take it round to her — and closes the book and says the thing: *the rule I wrote is about using a result. ## Chapter 21 — Nine Copies and a Method

The counter-record becomes a method and the method is expensive in the only currency that has never had a column. Bevin Sarr refuses to let the board out of the pump house and gives the real reason: a document has to live somewhere, and the somewhere costs her, and a woman who lies about a shift board stops the board being evidence for everybody on it. Wenna Pruitt's kitchen holds the four ledgers and produces the method as four questions — who holds it, who copies it, who may be asked for it, who may not — and Bessie Crane, of the Bellweather Road laundry, states the bill before anybody has priced anything: a page is twenty minutes of her Tuesday, nobody pays for it, and if a district ever gets to the point where the record of its own hours is a document then somebody is paid to keep the document and that somebody is in it. The compromise is a ledger that goes round on Fridays to a building that has not held it, carried by a man going that way anyway, with no owner, and Wenna says out loud that it is going to fail and that she has watched two fail from the same table. ## Chapter 22 — A District Is Not a Bloc

The structural problem arrives as a row in a room over a chandler's shop, and the three parties are all right. Hanna Stroud wants the record kept and quiet and says in public, unprompted, that she took a rise off thirty-one households in the month before last and would take it off again, and that she would rather be a woman who keeps a good thing quiet than a woman who is right in a room and alone by the winter. Bessie Crane wants the district's hours entered **in a form**, not published, because she has thirty-one women on a wage return and no record at all, and a girl of fifty-eight who is going to find out what that is worth on the day she cannot stand up. Wenna Pruitt wants it in the open, because a printed page is the only lever a district with no standing has. Nobody leaves in a temper and the three of them walk out within an hour of each other. **Sel Kade is in the room and is not on anybody's side**: a pumping warden's business is the water and everybody else is talking about money, she has an interest in the record only in so far as it decides who gets four in the morning, and she is resented for it and says so first. On the Saturday it is tried again in a kitchen and Bevin Sarr tells them the number: a district is a place, eleven times in twenty years, and the eleventh was worse because the water was up. The disclosure argument between Teo and Wenna is had at last and is **not** resolved. ## Chapter 23 — The Guild's Four-Line Reply

Monday the twenty-seventh of the second month, and the speed is the point. Three requests go in before eleven — the Office of Public Reckoning, the tenant council, and Hanna Stroud in her own name about an uninsurable block — and the hall's answer is on the mat by a quarter past two, on three identical cards with a form number, because the answer is a **definition** and a definition is already written and has been in a drawer since before Orrin was apprenticed. The four lines are the hall's Chapter 10 answer, in the hall's own type, with the letter's own *is asked to note* and one connective with it left off to save a line, and every word of it is true, and a definition cannot be beaten by producing more evidence. ## Chapter 24 — The Twenty-Eighth

Tuesday the twenty-eighth, the last day of the month, at ten, in a warm room, with the same chipped cup, forty minutes, and no threat. Hask asks how the year went and has an answer ready for either one and gives whichever is offered without a sign of having prepared it; he lets Orrin say *my father — eighteen years ago, in the storm month* and closes the subject cleanly, and that is the kindness. He gives him the **second sheet** of the W-4, which nobody asks for, and tells him the counter is open on Sundays, and both are gifts that are also the leash. He asks, once, pleasantly, about the thimble in the regulator's flank, and does not ask again, and the answer is four words and no expression. Then, unprompted and kindly, he says the escapement on his own bench wants oiling, and Orrin cannot hear it — the judgement, not the hearing, gone since Chapter 19 and not coming back — and understands inside ten seconds that he has been told the price of a fact. Orrin does not hand over the grey book, does not lie, and asks his own question: has the hall ever been asked for its labour ledger? *Yes.* *By whom?* *I could not tell you, and I have never once wanted to, and those are the same clause.* He comes away with a second sheet, a date, and no information at all, and nineteen days of margin spent in forty minutes, and a sleep lost on Monday night, and a hand that will not close on a bad week. The leash is never pulled. It is a form, a fee, a conversation and a wet stamp, and it is enough.

## Chapter 25 — The Water Schedule

The volume's midpoint, and it arrives cold: a printed notice on the ward board outside the Weir Two pump house at twenty past eight on the morning of Wednesday the eighth, posted there because the ordinance requires an affected district to be told, correctly, and it is an hour and forty minutes' notice. Question 4 112, filed on the second of the third month, six days earlier, by the **Water Users' Committee** of the pumping district — a body with a property qualification, nine members, no night worker on it and no form by which one could be. The Weirs send forty-one people and are not on the rail, because a standing is a written claim and the district has no standing in the model; the affected-district station is filled by a Green Span resident on the second-Wednesday rota who is lawful, correct, and the wrong person, and nobody in the building thinks this is a trick. Before the rail Orrin fixes a flickering lamp on the district indicator, which is a clockmaker being useful in a hall of clocks, and asks one question he can already price: if the front runs four cycles a day instead of two, what gives first. The Weir One gate's escapement governor, made for two, thirty years older than the works' standard, will hold, and is the only part nobody will ask about. It costs **a hundred and forty seconds, entered as a reading cost with the reason written under it**, and he cannot say it in the room, because Teo Arden refuses him on the correct ground: a true thing with no form under it in a room like that is the afternoon the district becomes a thing people are careful about. The notice comes up out of the spindle on a pressed strip and **the record keeper** reads all four lines aloud, twice, the way the form requires, with the blind margin set below the rule in small foot at half the size: **unregistered district labour** instead of *unregistered night work* — a category rather than a crew, and wider because the district learned to say *the pumping district's own returns* in a chamber. Twenty-six district minutes, **the Clockwork Quarter** — the paying district on the filed question and on the Toll Stair and nowhere else. After Chapter 25 the volume's failure is repetition, not ignorance.

## Chapter 26 — What the Second One Cost

The bill, compiled the tenth to the seventeenth by people going and asking, in seven currencies and with no column. Twenty-six minutes off the Clockwork Quarter, whose public clock is the city's reference and was out for an hour with no record anywhere. **An interval out of a named person:** Mrs Enid Skellern, 61, the rota resident, who has lost the afternoon of the eighth and whose name has been put beside an undated *calibration strain* entry in a Green Span health file for the first time by these people, with no duration beside it and none invented. **A shift worked late:** the Weir One crew off at twenty past two on the board, two o'clock on the works' certified return, so the district's own return is now wrong on the one night in four months a district-scale Reading ran over its line. **A hospital:** the Green Span auxiliary main, off the Weirs line, and the ward's eleven minutes on the Thursday, because the sub-line has been correcting out of something for eleven years and this is the first week it gave any up. **A bridge:** the Ferrywright gate interlock releasing the approach early and a night porter walking round, brought back across at four in the morning by a man with a lamp, after an hour and fifty minutes of his own walking. **A ferry timetable** printed eleven weeks ago and pencilled *as printed — see notice* in eleven places. **A school signal** in a headmistress's book. Mara produces the list and cannot make it a record, and the Office's reply arrives in seven days with *no further correspondence on this matter*, worse than a refusal and one day slower than its own precedent. In the Water Users' Committee, Leda reads the blind margin aloud to a room that can write it down; the room writes it down and does not re-open the schedule, and Orrin is in it for four minutes being useful and gets *advice received from the hospital* in the minute. ## Chapter 27 — The Licence, Renewed

Saturday the eighteenth into Sunday the nineteenth, and the ordinariness is the chapter. The W-4 has four things printed at the head and a ruled table of **three** columns and forty lines, and the grey book has two thousand pages, and the recommendation column is a third of an inch wider than the others because the guild found out in 1908 that men will say anything at all if the box is big enough. The selection is the finding: he does the machine faults in fifty minutes and two entries take the whole morning. The Green Span entry is the honest one, and its cost is Ellen Reeve and a document. The Weir Two entry is the one that matters — *the works' certified shift return for this station is not consistent with the station's own board* — and putting it in hands eleven years of the pumping district's night work to an institution, of his own motion, with nobody in the room able to refuse it. He finds the hole in his own third rule (a form is not a room), writes it under the entry, and leaves it in. The counter takes renewals between ten and twelve; he fills the book in the shop on the Saturday the 18th, walks down at a quarter past two, and sits on a bench outside the hall from twenty-five to three on the eighteenth until ten to ten on the nineteenth — **nineteen hours, and the only thing in the chapter that measures what Chapter 23's notice did to him**. A porter unlocks the counter and goes home, a clerk takes the fee, and **Hask endorses it in four seconds**. The book goes to a copyist at fourpence a page and the hall will keep its copy. He is a licensed maintainer for a year from the date of lodgement. Nothing is suspended, nothing is threatened, no letter is written, and the finding is that the correct thing and the useful thing are two different documents and no form carries both — and that a fee is what a building charges you to let you keep something that is yours, and a price is what it charges you to be allowed to do it.

## Chapter 28 — The Ledger, Copied

The W-4 comes back two days into a promised fortnight, with an enclosure: a **true copy of the hall's labour book line** for Weir Two, eleven years, made by **Hester Lomas, the hall's copyist of nineteen years, signing the note as E.L.**, out of the back room since the sixth of the second month and working the hall's transcription as outwork in a front room three doors down Verge Lane, and she did not know what a station was and did only what she is taught, which is to go and get the source so the maintainer can check her work. Nobody requested it and nobody in the hall has heard of the Lower Weirs pumping district. The fair copy is perfect, signed, stamped and true, and it has taken the evidence out of the labour book: the original line is in a second, harder-pressed, wrongly slanting hand on a day when the ink above has a Sunday in it, and a fair copy cannot show a hand or carry the ink above, so **the Sunday is lost by making a better copy**. Bevin Sarr is vindicated in the exact way she predicted and does not enjoy it, and names what the board has got: two hands and a wet night, and you cannot file a window. Della Marsh explains what was not a conspiracy and is not thanked, and the not-thanking is the exchange. Hester Lomas, out of the hall since the copies and the hand that straightened the evidence out of the labour book, is working three doors down Verge Lane for a conveyancer at an insulting rate, copies the extract at the hall's own price so the district can hold its own copy, and asks for the one thing she wants — **a lease and a wage a month** from the man with the chandler's shop. The Kestrel-side boardinghouse gets the same fair copy, and it is worse than nothing, and Teo says so. Ends on the discovery the whole batch has been driving at: the district has to choose which of two true things it will walk into a room holding, and the choice is not about the hours.

## Chapter 29 — Recovery, With a Number In It

Four ordinary days and the shop shut on the Sunday. The ropeworks clock at Ombersley's, a fusee and a strike train, six hours, £4 11s, and the first line in six weeks he has not felt sick about afterwards. The chandler's clock on Weir Lane, four minutes slow, and the price of doing it right: the guild's out-of-shop tariff at **four shillings and sixpence the hour** — the same figure as the renewal fee — an estimate in duplicate, the bracket found out of true and re-bushed, six hours and £1 7s 6d, four minutes to write the certificate, and a hand correction would have been eleven minutes and nothing. The count of hand corrections stays at two because he does not do it. On the Sunday he writes up the interview and the month's arithmetic, including the number the batch exists for: **Weir Two's night pumping for the four months to the end of last quarter is 388 hours**, counted by the district, printed in the Assembly's minutes, sayable by him at a table for the first time — and what it costs, which is that a district proving it worked 388 unpaid hours has proved its rates were set on a nil return, and the bill falls on a ward whose reserve has been empty since March. He has become a person who cites and the citation is a bill. On the Monday he certifies the Quarter's public clock in two hours forty with a gauge, because he cannot tell by the sound any more, and stands afterwards working out that checking is a thing you do in the room. On the Tuesday, in a kitchen, three women disagree about what the eighth of March cost their street and all three are right — the schedule was a good thing, a day is not a night, and **the eighth cost the things nobody wrote down** — and he makes a fair copy of it and hands it to Wenna Pruitt, and tells her the truth about it, and she goes into the Assembly's own printing with it through a clerk she asks in a corridor.

## Chapter 30 — What a District Decides

Nineteen people in the tenant council's back room over the chandler's shop on Weir Lane decide what the Weirs are, and the answer is not a bloc, not a movement, and not what Orrin wanted. The tenant council votes seven to three to one **not to take a position on the water schedule**, because a position is a thing a district is held to and a district that is held to a position can be asked to give it up; Teo Arden votes against, says before the vote that he will not be bound by it, and does not go behind Wenna's back, and neither of them is sorry. **Sel Kade** says the hydraulics and is resented for it: a schedule is a machine and a claim on a machine is a thing the machine can be run without; and she puts her own unreviewed March diversion on the table in her own words and refuses to be asked about it in that room, which is the seeding of the documented diversion in batch 0004. Orrin offers the district his rule for nothing and Wenna tells him the district has outgrown it: nine people who can refuse is a room, and a room has no address and cannot be served. The costs are real and chosen: the Advertiser stops printing the argument, eleven shillings paid, a page about the front and nothing else instead; two hundred women in the district find out they were not asked; a Bellweather Road mutual phones to ask what they are to do now. And on the Thursday afternoon a rigger brings back the Assembly estimates appendix in which **Orrin is named in a footnote** — one clause about as long as a sentence, in a source note to a £4 10s estimates line, cross-referring his certificate and Mara's objection — and it is the second time he has ever been in somebody else's document, and Wenna says she would rather be a footnote in a document a woman paid four pounds ten for than a paragraph in a paper a man printed because he was angry on a Friday. He goes back to work, and a man brings in a carriage clock, and mentions that his whole street has been out since Tuesday, and so have Weir Lane, and nobody has come. **Clocks are slipping across the Quarter and the Weirs and the man with the gauge is the only instrument the city has.**

## Chapter 31 — The Shape of a Moving Error

A diagnosis chapter written as a craftsman measuring, and the measurements are the plot. Saturday the 1st, **the day after a Friday the 31st of the third month on which nothing was taken in**: the carriage clock brought over the counter on the Thursday evening is not three minutes slow and not four, and the reason is a **bent minute hand**, so it could never be read to better than about two minutes; trued, it reads one minute forty and holds it to a second on six readings. He then times four machines on one hourline against a bench watch set on the Quarter's public clock and finds the errors are **100, 300, 500 and 700 seconds** — 1:40, 5:00, 8:20, 11:40, **a whole multiple of a hundred seconds each, and the common difference of 200 is written down and then refused as a law** — and a signalman two districts away has been writing **fifty seconds a night** in a bound day book for sixteen days and has never been asked to see. The shape: the line divides by eight, takes its beat off the odd teeth, **was surveyed for two cycles a day and has been given four since the fifteenth of the third month**, the first night of the canal-front schedule. One Aftershock, at the district indicator, at a price he knew: the main is not tired, is not wrong, has no opinion, and cannot tell a rhythm from an instruction — **true, and there was nowhere in Morrowfen to put it.** A hundred and twenty seconds, entered as a reading cost. Four condition records, all true, all saying *within tolerance*, which together say the city is on time. **Ambrose** at the works' counter refuses it on the correct ground: *a line is not a machine, and there is no column for that, and I have looked.* And on Sunday evening at ten past six he and Teo wait for a fourth draw that does not come, which is the news: the line has stopped, the error is now fixed and permanent, and there is no fault in it to correct.

## Chapter 32 — The Schedule Has No Address

Monday the 3rd, the first working morning after a schedule that lapsed at midnight on the Thursday, **and Bevin Sarr at eight with the ward's night book, reporting the third consecutive night on the standing rotation** — the front gave up one go, the water is where it is supposed to be, and she stayed two hours watching it be correct because she had never stayed. The Water Users' Committee notes that its schedule has run its term, that the district reverts to the standing rotation, that **it has no power to continue a schedule and no power to suspend the standing rotation**, and resolves to post notice of a proposed schedule for a hearing on the 17th — and the notice carries a sentence in a clerk's own hand that no notice on that board has carried in eleven years: *the committee is not able to consult the district, the district having no standing under the water act.* The chapter's finding is that the Weirs' Chapter 30 decision to take no position has cost them the schedule and nobody is going to say so, because the only person with the standing to say it said it to herself first. In the chandler's back room, where **Orrin is not admitted and says so at the door**, Wenna Pruitt decides to spend the council's last **£4 10s** on laying the ward's returns before the committee as an appendix, which is the only door that is both addressed and free to be refused — and **the Advertiser page on the ninth is cancelled, which is the visible cost.** Teo wants the page, says he wants it, and does not pretend to have moved. Sel Kade comes at four to say she has thought about it since Saturday and does not stop Wenna. The chapter ends with a woman's arithmetic: *nine men, a property qualification, and a fire that smokes, and a room that holds thirty, and the district is a hundred and forty thousand, and there is no form for the difference.*

## Chapter 33 — A Diversion, With a Schedule

The pumping ward's March diversion is **documented**, by a district works accountant who is looking for a discrepancy in a rate base and has no interest in Sel Kade. A diversion note: from the ward's reserve to the Carrow Street main, **nine thousand gallons a day, three hundred and twenty-four thousand gallons in thirty-six days**, seventy-one households and a galvanising and wire works, commenced the second of the third month, in force; **reviewed at the next monthly return and thereafter at the discretion of the district works**; and **restored within one month of any variation of the rotation** — and the rotation was varied on the 8th and again on the 30th, so the clause is triggered and nobody will serve it. The ward's reserve alarm has been inhibited since March and no report has been made. The first ordinary job of the batch is a refusal: Orrin will not wind the Bellweather Road clock a hundred and forty seconds with a bar, because **the count of his own hand corrections is two and it stays two**, and he tells the owner so in plain words and charges him four shillings for looking. Wenna offers to have the beneficiary's name struck out; Sel Kade refuses, because **a rumour can be beaten by anybody who turns up with a document, and a diversion with a schedule and a named beneficiary and a review clause is a thing that can be ended** — and she says the clause is the only part of the document she can use, and that it will not be used. Then the arithmetic nobody wanted: a ward's rates are set on its certified return, the return said no variation, the water went out free, and **correcting the certified return raises the ward's rates**, so four pounds ten buys a document that will cost the district more than four pounds ten.

## Chapter 34 — A Defence of Jobs

Monday the 10th, in the back room over the chandler's shop, fifty minutes, nine men, no bar, no clerk's table, hats on knees, a fire that smokes. Sel Kade sets her own terms and is not examined. Her first eleven minutes are a defence of jobs and not of herself — the works' certificate carries ninety-one, of whom sixty-one are resident in the district, and the district's own gauge sheet is the evidence that the Carrow Street end has had no standing water since March. Then the reframe that is the chapter: **she did not divert the reserve to a works, she diverted it to a flooding street, and the works is the reason the street flooded, because the street floods because of a gate, and the gate is the ward's business, and I am this ward** — and she offers the gate's own log on a written request naming the week, which the committee cannot ask for, because it has no function in respect of the district's flood performance. **Bessie Crane and Ivy Pike disagree, thirty-one against ninety-one, and both are right, and neither will choose.** She gives a restoration date in her own words and says she has no authority to bind the district and the committee has none either. Four lines of minute: the employment finding in, the flood finding out, the restoration noted and not ordered, and **the review clause not in the minute at all, because the minute is about what the committee heard.** She asks for one correction, gets it in forty seconds, and says thank you to the clerk and not to the room. **She is not recruited, not converted, not softened, and the tenant review is still owed by about Chapter 46.**

## Chapter 35 — What the Loose Minutes Take Back

The Chapters 20 and 28 threads pay in hardware. Two section caps off a buried line under the towpath, a set of gate wheels, a pinion, a drum, and one **1904 standpipe bracket** out of the district works' store — and **Teo Arden signs for all five in his own name in the store's issue book**, and puts the address of a laundry that is not his, because the method is that a record is a thing with a person in it. The cost: the bracket was the only one of its kind in four districts and it came off the top of the standpipe riser of a block of twenty-two households, the pipe dropped a quarter of an inch in the ceiling void, two flats are wet, the fourth-floor landing is dark, and **the four clocks that floor's 66-year-old resident has wound up a stair every Monday for eleven years have stopped being wound** — and the only clock in that block that is on time is the one on the ground floor, out by eleven minutes and forty seconds. ## Chapter 36 — The Ledger, Wider

Hask's labour book is copied wider than eleven buildings — it is in twenty-two by the seventeenth — and the copying is now organised, and one of the copies is **the front page of the Advertiser**, paid for by eleven small trades out of a spare advertising block, set in type by Hester Lomas at the page rate and a signature, and true in every word. **The print run is the chapter's action:** four thousand and sixty sheets off a flatbed on the night of Wednesday the 12th, counted twice by the paper's own delivery man, fifty-one, nineteen years, who was asked for nothing, offered nothing, said he would not help set it, and counted them because a book that says four thousand when it was four thousand and sixty is not a lie by anybody. Then the chapter's turn: **every copy is true and every copy is now a list of addresses, and a list of addresses is the thing that turns a grievance into an organisation.** Hanna Stroud and Renn Corby disagree about it and both are right — a list of shops is a list of shops; a site is a street, a number goes to a door, and **you do not need a name to get to a man.** The **book of initials** in Wenna Pruitt's kitchen holds forty-one entries in twenty hands, each a set of initials and a building, and **nobody has written down the key, on purpose.** The Office of Public Reckoning is ignored and turned into an instrument: Bessie Crane sends a form the Office itself supplies, for nothing, and asks for one thing — *the document exists, I want the office to write down that it exists, I do not want you to do anything about it, and I want the date on it* — and the note comes back with a fifth line that is not on the form, and Mara Venn is rung up and thanked, for the first time in nine years, and says she is not going to say how it feels because the caller is on a telephone. ## Chapter 46 — The Review Is Not a Punishment

**The quietest chapter of the batch and not a tribunal.** A warden could be questioned by a committee and reviewed by nobody at all, and the process did not exist, and it is built on the Wednesday the twenty-third in a room over a chandler's shop with a fire that smokes, and **Sel Kade writes three of its four clauses herself on the back of a warden's return at eleven o'clock on the Tuesday night and says so, and brings the fourth clause in and says it is not hers.** Clause one: a variation entered and posted within one working day. The six hours are not a spectacle: twenty-seven clocks, four standpipes, fourteen houses, six men, one man in a boat, the times set by the front and not by him; **and before anything else happens he says the thing nobody else could say first — I do not live on the canal front, my rates are made in the Clockwork Quarter, I have never drawn a bucket on this street — and the district's own labour for the six hours is twenty-seven shillings and nobody is going to be paid it, and Bevin Sarr writes that on the night book with a note that it is a quarter of the price of one of the works' brackets and that a sheet does not know that and a stair does not know that and a room does not know that.**

## Chapter 48 — Four Lines

**Resolution items one, three and six, in the chapter where the guild's decision and the suspension are read out.** Hask uses a **determination and not a question**, and says why in the room before he reads it: a question is about returns, takes twenty-one days, cannot suspend anything, and is right for a book; a determination is about an act and is the only instrument that can reach a holder for certifying a thing his class does not permit, and **I did not have to find a way round a rule, I used the rule, and the rule did exactly what it has been put there to do since before I was a master, and the fact that it worked on a good man in front of a good crowd at a cost to the good man is not a defect in the rule.** Four lines, read twice, in front of thirty people, all true — and in the fourth of them, **the term of the licence is not altered and the licence expires on the nineteenth of the third month next year: nobody shortened his clock and a master moved four months before he had to.** Then the guild's own standing orders operating correctly on a guild master, because *where a master declines a certification the ordinance permits him to make, the master's class is subject to review*: his class under review, **no certificate alone from this date, every one countersigned by a first-class holder and a monthly list published**; **the master's right to certify a shift in the maintenance-deferred class withdrawn and the class itself to be abolished within twelve months**; **the labour book, series 1 to 9, eighteen years, published in full at the counter and for sale at a price that covers paper, with a hall's annex naming the night of the Long Nine and the figure of two hundred and twelve, with the master's own instruction printed at the head of the annex that the figure is not to be added to any other one.** It is a punishment and it is the correct use of a well-run system and he says so without irony, and **he is a repairer under public oversight for the rest of his life and is not destroyed, does not confess, is not softened, and is not shown enjoying it.** Then item one, both halves: **Weir Two's second stage brought forward in about nine minutes on a variation, the bearing out of a box in the pump house, fitted on the Thursday and Friday, four hundred and twelve pounds and six shillings, the first work out of that schedule** — and **maintained by name, in the works' own hand, in a four-inch column, for the first time anything under it has been a person: B. Sarr; A. Dench, known as Amos, to be entered as a full name and not as an initial; T. Arden, licensed: no; R. Morrow; and Vance, P., the seventeenth, night shift 4, carried on the line because the line does not begin where a man begins.** And the **public toll ledger** in a glass case in the pumping ward, key on a string, chair and pencil on the ledge, **two sets of columns, MINUTES and DISTRICT and NAME and DURATION**, the first entry under the new ones **VANCE, P.**, and **no figure at all in the duration column**, with a five-line headnote in the Office's type saying that an empty column is a statement that the interval was not recorded, that it is not an omission of the Office, that it is not a mystery, that it is not to be filled in by inference, and that any person who fills one in by inference commits an offence against the Office and against the person whose name is beside it. **The shop stays open on a lease and a reputation, and nobody asks him to leave the bench.**

## Chapter 49 — Standing Protection, for a Stated Period

**Resolution items two and five, and item four's consequences in the ordinary world, in rooms and minutes.** The public-health committee sits on a Saturday, which it may do; **the pumping district has no delegate and what there is instead is a member of the public who may be seated and may not speak to a motion**, and there are two of them and one is twenty years old with a paper. **The standing protection, moved by the chair, carried without a division in eleven minutes: no shift certified in the maintenance-deferred class until the ledger and annex are published and received; temporary; expiring at the first sitting after the report, not before; the committee has not undertaken to renew it and has no power to promise it will** — three months against an audit that takes about fourteen months, **carried anyway, and everybody in that room knew the arithmetic**, and the chair says she cannot tell them what the committee will do and says so anyway so nobody can go home and tell a district it was safe. And **Imani Rook files the toll-ledger demand herself**, the clause she left out of her own demand of the thirteenth of the fourth, on arithmetic and not persuaded of anything: **an audit is a thing that is done and an office closes it and a drawer holds a card about nine hundred times; a demand is a standing thing, read twice, entered on the minutes, discharged, and read again next year, and it is the only instrument in that building which survives a change of clerk.** Carried nine to four, the four not the clerk, read twice, entered, **and the chair did not thank the four and did not thank anybody.** She also entered, unasked, that she had read the public index entry and the countersignature line and had known since the ninth of April and had said nothing in that room. The Teo–Wenna disclosure argument is had in a doorway in four minutes, **both halves, by a third person, because the two of them have not spoken since the ninth of the fourth**, and it is not resolved. **The Office of Public Reckoning, Monday the sixth, and the first *received and noted* in nine years that is the right answer to the question asked**: it will not supply a duration derived from a health record after the fact, because the column of a public ledger does not take an estimate, and it asks the branch to read the note, and it would rather have an empty column than a comfortable one; **the drawer holds thirty-one cards**, her mother's name is still not said, she writes a note she does not send, and then closes the file, because the file was about a document. And in the ordinary world: **the galvanising and wire works restarted on the fifth on a reduced draw and fifty-eight of ninety-one are back** and thirty-three are not, and nine of their houses have had a shilling and a penny in the pound added, and the finding is still in chalk on a board, and the review could not have kept the water on and does not claim to have. Wenna does the September arithmetic on the Sunday. It is the same September.

## Chapter 50 — The Column With Nothing In It

**The final image, and it belongs to this chapter alone.** Tuesday the seventh, the Toll Stair, washed twice by the rain, chalked at six by a man of fifty-four who has done it for nineteen years, **with the ledger's two sets of columns chalked out in full for the first time in the life of the stair** — done on the third inside two feet measured with a folding rule on the Monday, so a stranger can see they were done by somebody who measured. The minutes column is filled the way it always has been: **3, and Clockwork Quarter**, the first line of the stair since the eighteenth of the first month, with a clockfitter's initials beside it **because the custom of the stair is that a district's own figure is put up by a person of that district**; and below it 26 and Clockwork Quarter from the ninth of the third month. And in the new name column, in the same hand, **VANCE, P.**, and in the new duration column **nothing at all** — and Ostrow puts the chamois against the wall below it and takes it away again and leaves it, and says the only thing he says about it, to the woman from the Office: *then that is a thing that is on the wall and is empty on purpose, and in nineteen years that is the first one of anything I have chalked that I can say what it is for.* **A stranger stops on the stair, because the Toll Stair is the one place in Morrowfen where people stop to read things.** He is nobody and is not a journalist and will not come back. Orrin says *three minutes*. The stranger asks **from whom** — and for the first time in fifty chapters there is a name to give, and it is two words long: **Perry Vance.** Then he asks the question the book has spent fifty chapters refusing: **"How many more?"** — and Orrin could have said four documents and says **I don't know**, because every one of them is a document and not a number and there is no column anywhere in this city for the size of a thing that nobody has counted. And the man who could have written the number beside Vance's name stands on the other side of the stair with a chalk in his hand and nothing to put there, and leaves it empty, and goes down to a shop with a lease. **And on Wednesday the eighth, at a quarter to twelve, on the second step of the Cathedral, he counts the hour out of nineteen years' habit, and hears eight and then one over, and it leans on a weight somewhere he cannot name and cannot go and find, and it gives him no night and no face and not one word, and it costs him nothing, and it is not a warning and it is not an answer and it is not Aftershock, and it is the ninth thing in a machine that does eight, and the eight is the time and the ninth is not. Three times in five months. The third and last entry. He is going to go on not finding it, and it is still, exactly, unexplained.**
