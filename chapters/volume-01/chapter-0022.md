# Chapter 22 — A District Is Not a Bloc

The room was over a chandler's shop on Weir Lane and it had been a meeting room for about eleven years, and the reason was that the chandler had a back room and a bench and a great deal of unpaid goodwill, and the tenant council had a membership of people who could not meet in a licensed hall because they could not pay the door.

Bevin Sarr had got there first and had put the chairs out, and there were fourteen of them, and eleven people came, and three of the eleven arrived in the last minute in a way that told you what the evening was going to be.

---

The landlord came at eight and sat down before anybody had spoken. She was Hanna Stroud, fifty-four, nine blocks in the Weirs, and she had come in a good coat with a leather case, and she had a sheet of paper in it which she did not take out for the first forty minutes.

The employer came at eight as well and sat at the other end of the long table, away from the landlord, which was the first thing the room noticed and neither of them acknowledged. She was Bessie Crane, who ran the Bellweather Road laundry and paid thirty-one women on the Friday.

The tenant council came as a delegation, which was three women, and one of them was Wenna Pruitt, and the delegation was not permitted to speak first because it was a delegation, and that is the sort of thing that decides an evening.

And there was a chair at the end that nobody had been supposed to sit in.

---

"I want to say what I want before anybody says what they want," said Hanna Stroud, "so that nobody has to guess at me for an hour, and I want to say it flatly because I am not going to enjoy this evening and neither is anybody else and we may as well start with the part I am least proud of.

"I want the record kept. I do not want it published. I have nine buildings and I have been a landlord for thirty-one years and I have never been two months late with anybody, and in the second week of this month an insurer read a piece of paper in a hall and decided that one block of mine, on Carrow Street, was uninsurable on the present schedule, and I went into a room in this month with my own twelve years of rent book in a column and came out with the block on the present schedule and a letter saying the district returns were insufficient. That letter is in my file. I have read it about forty times.

"So: kept. In the district. And used when there is a form to fill in and somebody in an insurer's office is holding a piece of paper in the other hand. And not in a newspaper, and not on a board in a public hall, and not by a young man with a list." She looked at Teo Arden for exactly as long as it took. "I am not saying you are wrong about anything. I am saying that in this month thirty-one households did not get a rise they had been told about, and that I was the one who took it off, and that I would take it off again this month, and that I would rather be a woman who keeps a good thing quiet than a woman who is right in a room and is on her own by the winter."

The room was very quiet.

"That is the whole of what I came for," she said. "Now somebody else can tell me why it is the least sensible thing anybody has said in this room, and I will sit here and take it, and Mr Vale, if you tell me the district needs a voice I shall believe you and I shall go home and think about it and I shall think about it for a long time, because you have never once given me a reason that was not arithmetic."

---

Bessie Crane was not interested in the district having a voice.

"I have got a laundry on Bellweather Road and I pay thirty-one women on the Friday," she said, "and every one of those women has got hours, and every one of those hours is on a return, and the returns are checked twice a year by a man from an office in Bellweather who does not know me and has never once asked me for anything, and if he asked me for anything I would give it to him and it would be true.

"That is what I have got. I have got the hours of thirty-one women in a form that a man's office can open. And I have never once been asked to produce it by anybody in this room, and I have thought about that, and I have come to the conclusion that you are all talking about a different thing and that I am the only person at this table who has ever been made to fill one in.

"So I want one thing and I am not going to be dressed up. I want the district's hours entered in the same way. On a return. In a form, with a batch and a head count and a date, so that it is the same *kind of thing* as mine and not a chalk board. Not for the paper. For the girl who is fifty-eight and cannot lift her arms above her head, and who at present has a rent book and a wage envelope and no record that she was ever at work, and who is going to find out what that is worth on the day she cannot stand up." She squared her handbag. "You can print it, council, and I do not care. Print it tomorrow. I am not here for the printing. I am here because I have watched four of my women go the way that woman is going and I have never been able to say a single word about it, and it is because there is no form."

---

"And the council," said Wenna Pruitt, "wants it in the open, and the council wants it in the open because eleven years ago I said in this district that a district that produces hours and cannot produce a signature will not be believed this century, and I have been right, and a man took a copy of the hall's book and put it through nine doors in one morning and I told him in my own kitchen that he had proved it, and I have never been more right about anything in my life.

"It is in the Advertiser. It has been in the Advertiser for three weeks. Not a copy — a *column*, a page of it, a woman in a printing shop setting type off a page of a hall's labour book with her name at the bottom of it, and a paragraph underneath that says what it means, and a picture of a pump house. And the Advertiser is the only thing in this city that the three offices we care about actually read, and it has cost us nothing, and it is the only door in this city that is not shut. And on Monday morning I am going to put a question in writing, through a proper form, with a name on it, and it will be the correct instrument, and it will cost four pounds ten that I have not got, because that is what a request costs.

"So: open. This week. All of it. Every hour we have counted, in every building, on a plate, and somebody from this room standing next to it who will say what it is and will put their name to it and will lose whatever the putting costs them."

---

They went at it for two hours and made no progress and it was not a fight, and that was the worst of it.

Bessie Crane wanted it in a form and did not care about the printing. Wenna Pruitt wanted it in the open and knew that the printing was the only lever she had. Hanna Stroud wanted it quiet and had just told the room, in public, on her own initiative, that a rise on thirty-one households had been taken off and put on again, and that she would take it off again this month, and every person at that table understood that she was the only one of the three who had ever been made to carry the cost of the record on her own balance.

The argument that should have finished it — that Stroud's position was the position of a woman who had been on the wrong end of an insurer and did not want it again — was never said, because everybody in the room had worked it out within about a minute and nobody said it, because saying it would have been unkind and because it was true, and those two facts were the reason the meeting went on for two hours and produced nothing.

At about half past nine, Wenna Pruitt asked the one question that everybody had been waiting four weeks for, which she had told the council she was going to ask and she asked it the way she had said she would, flat, without sitting down.

"Which of us is speaking for the Weirs?"

There is nothing in Morrowfen that answers that, and the silence after it lasted about eleven seconds, and then a man near the window said, "Well," and a woman said, "That's the third time this month," and the room came apart in a way that had nothing to do with raised voices.

---

The chair at the end that nobody was supposed to sit in had been occupied since a quarter past eight by a woman of forty-four in a wet coat who had come in late off a pump round and had not announced herself, and who had not said anything for an hour and forty minutes, and who now said the only sentence in the room that anybody was going to remember in a week's time.

Sel Kade put both hands on the table.

"I am going to say a thing and I am going to say it once and then I am going to be quiet for the rest of the evening, and I would like it minuted if anybody here knows what minuted means.

"Every one of you has been talking about the record. You have been talking about where it goes, who holds it, who can be asked for it, what it costs, whether it goes in a paper. Nobody has asked me a single question about any of those things, and I have sat here for an hour and forty minutes and I have not been asked one, and I want to say why, and it is not modesty.

"It is that a pumping warden's business is the water." She said it without any heat. "That is all it is. It is the whole of the post. When the pumping ward has a reserve, the water is where it should be and the canal front does not go under and a hospital on the Green Span line does not draw its auxiliary off a main that is somebody else's problem at four in the morning. When the ward has no reserve, I am the woman who goes to seventy-one households on a street and says the reserve has gone, and I say it out loud, on a public street, because that is the honest version, and then I do the allocation, and then I do it again in the next month, which is the word for it in that office, and which is the same month every year.

"I do not have an interest in your record. I have an interest in the water at four in the morning. I have been sitting here for an hour and forty minutes listening to three very good arguments about who gets to be believed, and I want to say that I do not know who is right, and that I am not going to pretend to, because the one thing I have learned in this post is that a person who starts pretending to know is how you get a district hurt.

"And if you take that as me not being on your side, that is what it is, and you are entitled to it. I am not on your side. I am on the side of the water, and the water does not have an opinion about the record, and when it goes wrong the people who are in it are not the people who were in the room."

Bevin Sarr said, from the doorway, "That is the fairest thing anybody has said in this room in two years, and I would like everybody to notice that I said so about the woman who has the most to lose by saying it."

"Somebody noticed," said Sel Kade. "Now, the chair at that end of the table is not empty. That is a licensed clockmaker from the Quarter and he has been in a pump house at eight in the morning and a shop all day and a city all month and he has not said one word in an hour and forty minutes, and if anybody wants to know why, it is because there is nothing in this room he can certify, and I have watched a good deal of this in my time, and I would rather it were said by him than found out in the spring."

---

Nobody left in a temper. Orrin had wanted them to, and had a whole speech ready about what happens when a district cannot hold a position, and it stayed in his pocket.

Hanna Stroud put her case on the table and said, "I am going home, and I shall think about what was said, and I shall be honest with you about the thinking. I am not a woman who says yes in a room and means it in a letter, and I am not a woman who means it in a letter and does not do it."

Bessie Crane was out of the chair about four minutes later, and she said, to nobody, "Thirty-one women, on the Friday," and went down the outside stairs with her handbag and did not look back and was not angry with anybody, which was somehow the worst thing that happened all evening.

Wenna Pruitt stayed, because she had not come for a chair, and she sat with her hands round a cup she had not filled, and at about twenty past ten she said to Orrin:

"You have been sitting there for an hour and forty minutes."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because there is nothing I can certify," he said, "and because a clockmaker in a room like this is a man who has come to fix something, and if I had said a single sentence in the last two hours somebody would have written *the clockmaker says* and it would have gone into a newspaper that is printed on a Friday."

Wenna Pruitt looked up.

"That is a very good reason and it is the second best reason," she said. "The first best reason is that you are not of this district and you have a lease, and if you are wrong about us then there is one white man in the Quarter who can be got hold of, and that is worth more to us than anything you could have said."

---

They did it again on the Saturday, in a kitchen, for fifty minutes, and it was worse.

The reason it was worse was that Bevin Sarr was in it, and the reason Bevin Sarr was in it was that Orrin had asked her to come, and the reason he had asked her to come was that he had realised overnight that he had been in three rooms in four days in which he had spoken to people who could be asked about something, and had not once been in a room in which anybody could be asked about *him*.

She sat down and put two fingers on the table and said it.

"That is the eleventh time."

"Eleventh?"

"Eleventh in twenty years that I have sat in a room where three people who want the same thing from a pump house want it in three ways, and the eleventh was in nineteen-oh-nine and it was worse because the water was up." She did not raise her voice. "I have watched you people spend two days discovering that you are a place and not a person. Eleven times. I have never once had to explain it to anybody because you always work it out, and it always takes about two days, and it always costs about the same, and the cost is that somebody who needed the room has to do without it while you find out what you are."

Wenna Pruitt said, "Are you saying we should not have met?"

"I am saying you should have met on Thursday, and you did, and it was a good meeting, and it was eleven times eleven." Bevin Sarr put her hand on the board-hook that she had brought with her, for no reason anybody could see. "I am saying that a district is a place, and things happen in places, and a place does not want anything, and the moment you go looking for a place that wants something you will find one inside a week, and it will want what it has always wanted, which is to be left alone with its own arrangements until somebody from outside needs it to be angry."

"That is a bleak position from a woman who keeps a shift board."

"It is not bleak," said Bevin Sarr. "It is twenty years of the best decision I have ever made, and I made it on my own, in a pump house, with a hook, and nobody has ever once come out here and thanked me for it, and I have never wanted to be thanked for it, and I am telling you three things about myself in a kitchen and I am telling them because the two of you have spent two days learning them and I have had them for free."

---

The argument that was not resolved, and was not going to be resolved on a Saturday, was about disclosure, and it was between Teo Arden and Wenna Pruitt, and it had been running underneath everything since Monday the sixth, in this month and had never once been had in a room.

"Three weeks in the Advertiser," said Teo. "Nine addresses. And a printing shop that will take anything anybody brings it, on a Friday, for a page, and it is the only door in this city that is not shut."

"It is the only door in this city that is not shut *at you*," said Wenna Pruitt. "The Advertiser takes a page from nine doors and prints what the nine doors agree on, and a woman in there sets type off whatever arrives in the middle of the week, and on the Friday you are reading something that four people argued about and two of them did not know about. That is not publishing. That is a letter to a stranger in a hurry."

"Say what you would do instead."

"I would wait until there is a table to put it on, and I have been looking for a table for eleven years, and this is the first one anybody has put in front of me." She put her cup down. "And I will tell you what the table is, and you will not like it either, and you have not thought about it because you are twenty. The table is a *request*. You do not get believed by printing. You get believed by asking, in a form, in a name, and getting an answer that somebody in an office has to write their name to. And you cannot ask, because there is nothing to ask as. So you print, and printing is what you do when you cannot ask, and it works about one time in three, and the other two times it is what people mean when they say a thing has got a bad name."

"So the bad name is ours now."

"The bad name is ours now," said Wenna Pruitt, "and it is the cheapest thing we have ever bought, and I would not give it back, and I am not going to pretend to you that I have not enjoyed it. Enjoy it. It is the first time in twenty years that anybody in this district has been angry about something at the same time as everyone else."

Teo Arden looked at her for a long moment.

"No," he said. "No, and I am not going to agree to that this month either, and you are not going to get me to agree to it, and I am not going to go and do it behind your back, and I want that written down as well, and the reason I am not going behind your back is not respect, it is arithmetic, because if I go behind your back I lose the one door I have got and I get a bad name and a public row in the same week."

"That is nearly respect."

"It is nearly arithmetic," said Teo Arden, "and it is the same thing this month and it will not be the same thing in a year, and I am telling the room so that neither of us is surprised in a year."

---

Nobody met on the Sunday, because nothing met on a Sunday, and because there was nothing on the Sunday, and Orrin said so out loud on the Saturday night in a shop in Verge Lane to two people who had not asked him.

"You cannot put a Sunday in it. You cannot get a clerk, you cannot get a hall, you cannot get an insurer, and there is no version of this where a room on a Sunday is a room anybody has to attend. If we ever get to the point where we are holding meetings on Sundays to keep this alive, then what we have built is a thing with a rota in it, and a thing with a rota in it has an owner, and we have spent two days agreeing that it has no owner."

Teo Arden said, "Then what happens on the Sunday."

"Nothing happens on the Sunday," said Orrin. "That is what a place is. I am told it is very restful."

---

He worked the limit out on the Sunday night, in the flat above the bathhouse, at about eleven, with the ledger open on the table and the lamp pulled over because his ear was doing the thing where turning his head further than he needed was cheaper than the alternative.

It came out of the Saturday and it was not a moral discovery. It was a form.

*You cannot ask, because there is nothing to ask as.*

That was Wenna Pruitt's sentence and it had been sitting in him for a day and a half like a stone in a boot, and on the Sunday night he took it apart and found that it was not one problem. It was two, and the two had been sitting on top of each other for a week, and when he separated them they were both enormous and neither of them was about the water or the pumping or the hours.

The first was: **a request has to be made by somebody with standing, and the only three bodies in this district that have standing are a landlord, an employer and a tenant council, and none of the three will sit in a room with the other two.** A petition could go in with any of their names and it would be answered, and it would be answered on the standing of the name, and the name would be the whole of what was read.

The second was: **the only thing the district actually has that anybody in this city has ever asked for is the guild's own labour book.** Not a copy. Not a board. The book, the bound volume, out of the licensing floor on Verge Lane, where it sat on an open shelf in a hall that anybody in the public might walk up to and read, and where a woman had told him there were three doors to it and that the third door was a man with no licence and no form and no name to write on a line.

And the two sat together and made a shape, and the shape was this: they could not get anybody to ask the hall for the book, because the only people who could ask are the three who will not meet, and if one of the three did ask, then that one of the three owned the record, and the record would be the record of whoever asked, and the district would have a position.

And Orrin Vale sat in a cold room above a bathhouse and understood, with the particular clarity that comes to a man who has spent a month being wrong in expensive ways, that a district's best evidence is a thing that gets used against the district.

He wrote it out.

*The record is now good enough to be used against us. That is not a thing that has gone wrong. That is what a record is for.*

*Mrs Stroud's insurer used a copy of it in this month and thirty-one households lost a rise for a month. Miss Crane's thirty-one women would lose their return if the same page said they were the same kind of thing as ours. My own district's clocks are the only ones in this city with a certificate anybody can point at, and the certificate is mine, and the certificate is the reason a school bell on Weir Lane is a different argument from a pump house.*

*Every one of those is true and every one of those is a cost and not one of them is a reason to have less of it. That is the thing I did not understand on the twenty-first of last month. I thought a record was protection.*

*And the Weirs are not a thing that wants anything. Eleven times, says Bevin Sarr. So we cannot be asked. And the three who can be asked will not meet. And the only door in the city that opens without somebody's standing on it is the Advertiser, and that is the door we have used, and it is one door in three, and the other two are what we are now living on.*

*Which means the next move is not a meeting. The next move is a form, with a name on it, in an office, asking the hall of the Guild of Horologists for its labour book for the pumping district, fourteen months, and the name has to belong to somebody with standing, and the three who have standing cannot be got into a room, and I have got a name and no standing, which is exactly the wrong way round.*

He read it back.

Then he added, at the bottom, because it had been in his head since the Wednesday and he wanted it in ink:

*And I have been in three rooms this week in which people talked to each other and not to me, and that was a favour done to me and I did not notice it for two days, and the not-noticing is the thing I have actually learned this month. In the spring the man from the Office was the only person in the room who could ask for anything and I have never once asked myself what that has done to every room I have walked into since. That is not a fault in anybody else's arithmetic.*

He shut the book, and got four hours' sleep, and was up at six on the twenty-seventh with a Monday in front of him and a decision to make about who put his name on a piece of paper.
