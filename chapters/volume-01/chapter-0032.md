# Chapter 32 — The Schedule Has No Address

The canal-front schedule lapsed at midnight on Thursday the thirtieth of the third month, and nothing happened at midnight, and nothing happened on the Friday or the Saturday or the Sunday either, and by eight o'clock on Monday morning the pumping district's front was back on the standing rotation and had been for three nights, drawing at night the way it had drawn for eleven years before anybody thought there was a question to ask.

Bevin Sarr came down the slipway at about eight on the Monday morning with the ward's own night book under her arm, and she put it on the counter of Vale's Bench without opening it, which she had never done before.

"Last night," she said. "One go. The front gave up one go and went back to drawing at eleven at night like a clock, and the water is where it is supposed to be, and I have been down there since half past ten watching it be correct."

"How long were you down there?"

"Two hours. And I want you to understand that I went down there because I did not trust it, and I stayed because it was right, and I have been doing this for twenty years and I have never stayed." She put her hand flat on the counter, which she did instead of touching the book. "So you can have your arithmetic. It stopped. Half the city is wrong by a fixed amount and the reason has gone out like a lamp and there is nothing to wind it against."

"Have you been up to the corner since?"

"Twice. It has not moved. It is not going to move." She looked at him. "You have got the whole of today to use that before somebody comes and asks you what it means, and they will not like the answer, and neither will I, and neither of us is going to be in a position to say anything the district can do about it."

---

The Water Users' Committee of the pumping district sat at ten on Monday the third of the fourth month in the back room over the chandler's shop, and there were nine members of it, and the chairman was a chandler, and the room was the same room it had been in twice already, and the fire was lit because the chimney in that room smokes.

Orrin Vale was not in it. He was in the passage outside it with his case at his feet, because the room over a chandler's shop is reached by a passage and the passage has a window in it that looks on the yard, and nobody had thought to close the door, and from where he stood he could hear the chairman say *minutes` three weeks* and a chair go back and somebody turn a page.

He was not a member and could not be. Membership of that body was by property qualification — the freehold or the lease of a property that drew on the common main — and there were about ninety people in the pumping district who qualified and not one of them had ever been awake at two in the morning on the Bellweather front. Orrin owned two benches in Verge Lane in the Clockwork district, four miles away, on a main of his own, and drew nothing off the Lower Weirs at all, and would have qualified for nothing.

The thing that came out of the room at half past eleven, in the voice of the clerk, and was repeated on the stair and in the yard and in the pump house and in a kitchen off Weir Lane and in four shops, was this:

That the committee noted that the schedule made on the second inst. and effective from the fifteenth to the thirtieth had run its term and was at an end.

That in the absence of any further schedule the district would revert to the standing rotation from the first inst., and that the committee had no power to continue a schedule and no power to suspend the standing rotation, and that the standing rotation was the position of the district before the question was ever filed and was in that sense the district's own arrangement with itself.

That the committee had resolved to give notice of a proposed schedule in the same terms, to be posted on the ward board outside the Weir Two pump house in accordance with the ordinance, and that the matter would be heard at the sitting of the seventeenth, and that anybody might be heard at that hearing on giving notice to the clerk.

And that was the whole of it. Forty minutes, and there was no argument, because there was nothing to argue about: a schedule is a thing a committee makes and a term is a thing a schedule has, and the term had ended, and nine men had gone back to their own properties.

The only person in the yard who asked a question was a boy of about eleven, and he asked it to nobody, and it was: "Where's the man that's off?"

---

The part nobody had thought about until the middle of the following week was not the lapse. It was the notice.

A schedule is made by a committee. A committee is a body of people who draw on a common main. And the pumping district — a hundred and forty thousand people, eleven hundred of them on the canal front, a night crew that had stood on Weir Two for eleven years without appearing on a single page of anything — is not a party to any of it, has never been a party to any of it, and cannot be made a party by anybody who is not the Assembly, and has not been the subject of an Assembly sitting since the returns were printed.

There is no form on which a district can be asked whether it consents to a schedule about its own water. A man can be asked. A ratepayer can be summoned. A hundred and forty thousand people who are not ratepayers at the address where the water is cannot be sent anything, served with anything, or written to about anything, and a notice on a board outside a pump house is a notice to whoever happens to walk past it, and the ordinance calls that telling an affected district, and it is the truth, and it has been the truth for as long as the ordinance has existed.

The Weirs had voted on the Wednesday of the twenty-ninth of the third month, seven to three to one, not to take a position on the water schedule, because a position is a thing a district is held to.

The Weirs were now living inside the consequence of that vote with a standing rotation and eleven hundred people on a front that had gone under in the spring of last year.

Nobody said *you did this.* Orrin looked for somebody to say it for two days, in a shop and on a slipway and in a passage, and it did not come, and he understood that it was not going to, because the only person in the district with the standing to say it was the woman who had moved the motion, and she had said it to herself, in her own voice, at her own table, on the Wednesday morning, out loud, before the vote was taken, in the past tense, which was how a woman of thirty-eight tells a room she has already done the sum.

---

On the Monday afternoon the tenant council met again in the back room over the chandler's shop, and it was a smaller meeting than the one on the Wednesday of the twenty-ninth, and it was not a meeting anybody had called.

Orrin Vale was not in it.

He was told that at ten past two, in the yard, by Wenna Pruitt, in about nine words.

"You are not coming in."

"I am not a member of anything you have ever done."

"You are a class-three maintainer of Verge Lane on a different main, and the moment you are in that room every decision we take in it is a decision somebody can afterwards say was taken with the district's clockmaker sitting there, and this council has spent eleven years not having a person like that in it, and I am not having one now, and it is not a slight and it is not because you are from the Quarter, it is because of what you are, and the thing you are is a man with a rule who put eleven years of our night work into a guild form without asking us."

"That was the W-4 and I have never been sorry of it and I would do it again."

"I know you would. That is why." She shut the yard gate behind her. "Go and do your work. If you are in the building when I come out I shall say something I have not planned."

He went and did his work, and what he did with it was a thing that took the whole afternoon, which is the sort of thing that happens to a man who is sent out of a room.

He went to the chapel school on Weir Lane at half past two and asked to see the headmistress's book, the one that said *Fri 10th, 4 mins. Mon 13th, 7. — and the notice says eleven more.* He asked for the fortnight before that as well, and she gave it to him, because she had been keeping it since the first month of the year and had never had anybody ask for the whole of it.

*Sixth, 3 mins. Ninth, 2. Tenth, 4. Eleventh, 3. Twelfth, 5. Thirteenth, 7. Fourteenth, 4. Fifteenth, 2. Sixteenth, 1.*

Then nothing at all.

Nothing at all from the fifteenth of the third month to the twenty-eighth, and the notice had said four to eleven minutes on eleven nights and eleven nights had come and gone and the school's front had gone from four to eleven minutes late to dead on time, and eighty-one children had walked to school on a canal front at exactly the hour they walked to school in the previous year.

He stood in a school corridor and read that twice, and it was not a small thing, and it was not a good thing either, and it was not a story. The fourth draw had stopped. That was all it was. The school was on time because the water was coming back in one go at night instead of four, and the water coming back in one go at night is the reason thirty-one houses went under in the spring, and the school had got its eleven minutes back, and the front had got its flooding back, and both of those were true and neither of them was anybody's fault and there was no form anywhere in Morrowfen on which a person could write down *the school is on time and the canal is going to flood* and be believed about the second half of it.

He copied the fortnight out into the shop book and put the original back in the headmistress's hands himself, and did not tell her why.

---

Inside the room there were five people and they had known each other for a long time, which is the only condition under which an argument like that can be had.

Wenna Pruitt had the Advertiser's bill on the table and the council's bank book beside it, and the bank book said what it said.

"Four pounds ten," she said. "That is what is in it. That is the whole of what is in it. The printer wants four pounds ten for a fortnight and the second page on the ninth of the month, and I have eleven shillings owed to a copyist for the last fortnight, which I have paid, and after the ninth there is nothing, and after nothing there is no Advertiser page and no printing in this district."

"I want to keep the page," said Teo Arden.

"So do I."

"Then we keep the page." He put his hands flat. "Mrs Pruitt, the page on the ninth is the only door in this city that is not shut. It went out seven times. The page on the ninth goes out about the front and the standing water and it will be read by the eleven hundred people who live on it, and those are the people whose water it is, and if you spend the last four pounds ten of this council getting a document into a minute on the Rise then the ninth of the month is a piece of paper about nothing that four hundred people on the front never see, and we will have exchanged a door that is open for a door that opens on Wednesdays."

"You have been in a room with a printed page and a served document and you have come out with a preference for the page," said Wenna Pruitt, "and I have told you for five weeks that the page has no address, and you have not listened, and I am not going to win this argument by saying it again."

"Then say it by arithmetic."

"All right." She turned the bank book round. "Here is the arithmetic. The returns of the Lower Weirs pumping district, six of them, printed in full with the Assembly's minutes, on a line of the estimates at four pounds ten out of a Green Span ward's clerical allocation. That is a document. It has a form number. It is in a binder on Cathedral Rise and anybody may read it, and it is on the table of a water users' committee, and the four hundred pounds ten in this bank book is the same sum by accident and I have never once pretended it was not.

"Now. That document is the only arithmetic anybody in the Weirs has ever got into a room. And a room does one thing at a time. If I put the returns in front of those nine men, they will read them, because they are the strongest thing on the table and because the six returns are the only paper in this district that says a district worked hours at a civic machine. And when they read them they will see what the returns are, and what the returns are, is a list of what this pumping ward did and did not do with its water, and there is a quarter in there with a reserve that is not where the ward's certified return says it is."

Bevin Sarr said, "Say the rest of it."

"There is no rest of it. There is a diversion in the third month and it is mine, and I did it in March, and I said it out loud on a street in the first month and I said it again at this table on the Wednesday of the twenty-ninth, and I have never had it in a document, and I have never been asked about it, and no office in this city has ever written it down."

"Sel," said Teo Arden.

Wenna Pruitt did not look at her. "And if I spend the last money this council has, I will get a clerk to put the ward's returns before that committee as an appendix to their own minutes, and an appendix to minutes is an address, and an address can be served, and served means somebody has to answer it, and the first person who will have to answer it is a woman of forty-four who moved a reserve to seventy-one houses in a month before any of you were arguing with me about anything."

She put her hand on the bank book.

"That is the price of the page. That is what four pounds ten buys in this district, and I have known it since about eleven o'clock this morning and I have been walking round the yard with it since eleven o'clock, and I would like somebody to tell me that I have not thought of it, and I do not expect anybody to."

---

Sel Kade had been standing at the window with her coat over her arm since a quarter past two, and she came and sat down, which she had not done at this table in two months.

"You have thought of it," she said. "I have come at four to tell you that I have thought of it, and I have thought of it since Saturday morning, when a woman at a pump house told me that the front had gone back to one go at night, and I have been three days deciding whether to come here and say anything at all, and I have come, and I am going to say it in about four sentences and then I am going to let you get on."

She squared her hands on the table.

"Number one. If you lay those returns before that committee you will get a quarter examined in a room with nine men in it, and you will get me examined in the same room, and neither of those is the same thing and only one of them is about water. You have understood that. You have said it out loud in a room of five people instead of nine, and that was decent of you, and it changes nothing.

"Number two. It is a good trade. I have had the diversion public in my own words for a month and reviewed by nobody, which means it is a rumour with my name on it, and a rumour can be beaten by anybody who turns up with a document. I would rather be beaten by a document." She said it without any weight on it at all. "Number three, and this is the one I would like written down, because it is the only one I am actually here about.

"You will be buying the right to be counted. That is what the money is. It is not for us. It is not for the district, and it is not for the schools, and it is certainly not for the canal front, and it is not going to change one drop of water before the tides. What it will do is put a hundred and forty thousand people, and a night crew, and a whole quarter of the ward's own returns into a document in a building with a clerk, where a person can be made to answer for it in their own name, and that is the whole of what anybody in this district has ever been able to buy in this city, and I have been trying to buy it with my own two hands since I was twenty-six and I have never once had the money.

"So spend it. And do not spend it on a page, because a page cannot be served, and I have said that once in my life in a room where I was invited to say it and I am not saying it again."

"The committee sits on the sixth for the returns and again on the tenth," said Ivy Pike, from the doorway, with her coat on and her hat in her hand and the air of a woman who has a gate shift at seven. "The schedule's own hearing is the seventeenth, and that is the one with the room in it." She looked at the table. "I have a pay book at home with ninety-one names in it and I am going to go and open it, and I would like somebody to tell me which of those three sittings I should be at."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Right," said Wenna Pruitt. "Teo."

"I am not going to be a spoil for it," said Teo Arden. "I said in this room on the Wednesday that I would not be the same arithmetic as you in a year, and I meant it as a thing I would say and not a thing I would believe, and I have been careful about which of those I did. I want the page. I think the page is worth more than the minute. And I am not going to pretend that I have moved."

"Then we have a decision." She did not ask for a vote, because a vote is a thing a district is held to. "The page on the ninth is off. I will write to the printer today and tell him why and I will pay him nothing and he will print somebody else's argument instead, and he will be within his rights, and I shall have to read it. And the four pounds ten goes to a form for laying the ward's returns before that committee as an appendix, and the form carries a fee because every form in this city carries a fee, and we will be four pounds ten poorer, and I will put it in the council's book on the same line as the other one so that whoever comes after me can see the two sums next to each other and know they are equal by accident and not by anything anybody intended."

"I will type it," said Teo Arden.

"You will not type it, because you cannot spell *maintenance-deferred* and neither can I, and that is the entire reason Bessie Crane is going to do it and I am going to ask her and she is going to say yes for the laundry's page and not for us, and that is also the price."

---

He was still in the yard at six, and it had been dark since half past four, and Wenna Pruitt came out and shut the gate and gave him about four seconds of a face, which was not unkind and was not sorry.

"You were a long way from the building," she said.

"I was reading a schoolmistress's book."

"Of course you were." She put the strap of her bag over her shoulder. "I want to say one thing about the money and then I am going to go and put a kettle on, because you will do something stupid with it and I have known you for five weeks and I know exactly what you are going to do."

"Which is what?"

"It is going to sit in your head all evening and it is going to be about four pounds ten." She was already walking. "And you are not going to put four pounds ten into that, and you know that you are not, and I have watched you not do it about nine times, and every one of those nine times was a time when you could have bought a page or a shilling for a leaf and you did not, and I have never once known why and I have stopped asking, because I have three ledgers on a table and I have run out of years for other people's reasons."

She got six paces away and stopped.

"There is one thing in that building I want to say and then I am going. Nine men, a property qualification, and a fire that smokes. They have a power to make a schedule about this district's water and not one power to ask this district anything. And they have spent a morning being told, by a clerk and by a man in a passage, that a hundred and forty thousand people would very much like a say. And on the seventeenth of this month they are going to hear notice of a proposed schedule, and the notice will say that anybody may be heard, and the hearing will be held at ten in a room over a shop, and the room will hold thirty and the district is a hundred and forty thousand, and there is no form in that building for the difference." She turned round. "That is the piece of arithmetic that is worth four pounds ten and it is not mine and it is not yours and I did not find it, and I am going to bed."

He stood in the yard. Inside, somebody was already arguing about a bus route. On the canal the front was giving up its water for the first time in seventeen days in one go, at eleven at night, the way it had done every night for eleven years, and there was a smell off it of low tide and cold iron, and somewhere under the water a machine that had been asked four times a day for sixteen days had done nothing at all, which was the correct behaviour, and which meant that the error in it was now fixed, and permanent, and had nowhere to go.

He had four clocks' worth of a shape and a signalman's day book and a schoolmistress's fortnight, and a room on the Rise where nine men would read a return on the tenth of the month and one woman would be examined with it, and he had nowhere whatever to put any of it, and on the evidence of the last three weeks it was not going to be worth anything in a room either.
