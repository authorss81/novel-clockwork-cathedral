# Chapter 6 — The One Minute He Cannot Account For

The young man by the pump house had been awake for two nights and had better information about the death than Orrin did, and did not appear to have the slightest intention of giving it to him.

He was standing with his back to a wall and his hands in the pockets of a coat that had been let out at the elbows twice, and he had the narrow, pinched look of somebody who has been measuring adults against a standard they do not meet. When Orrin came up the slipway he did not move, and when Orrin stopped he said, without preamble, "You were the crown case man."

"I was."

"Certify a bell. That's what the crew said you were doing, up the hill, the Monday before last." He took his hands out of his pockets. They were filthy to the wrist and one of the nails was split. "Perry told me about you. He said the clock man was a decent sort who came down on a bad job because they were cheap. He said that about you at about two in the morning on the night of the seventeenth, and he was pleased about it, which I thought was a strange thing to be pleased about, and now I know it was the last time he was going to be pleased about anything."

"I wasn't inside."

"I know. The watchman told me. He told me a great deal and he's a good man and I would take his account over anybody's in the city, including mine." The boy's voice did not rise and did not shake. "I know you weren't inside. I want you to tell me what you told him at the door, because he says he will swear to it, and if it's the wrong thing I will go up the hill to the Cathedral myself and I will not be as careful with them as he is."

Orrin told him. He told him the whole of it, the stage, the count, the valve, the boy on the tram with nine coppers, the closed counter, the fact that he had no licence for that machine and no proposal and no shift time and no name to give.

The boy listened to all of it.

"Right," he said. "So you knew."

"I knew it wouldn't stop."

"And you had no way to do anything about it, because you're a clockmaker and a clockmaker doesn't have a form for stopping a pump at night, and nobody does, and that's not your fault." He said it flatly, and it was not kindness, and Orrin had the distinct feeling of being handed something by somebody who intended to keep it. "Perry had no way either. That's what I can't get round. He was the best there is and he went down a ladder into eight inches of water to shut a valve the clock said was shut, and there is nobody in this district who can be angry at him for that, and I have tried, for two nights, and I cannot, and I would like somebody to come and be angry at so that I can."

Nobody came.

"You filed a question," the boy said. "At the Cathedral. That's what the notice on the door says. *The question had been answered and the gate opened in good time.*"

"Yes."

"You filed it."

"I filed it. At ten past two yesterday. I paid for it out of my own district's clocks, which I had no right to do and no way not to do, because the sheet asks you to name a district and my district was the one that could afford it."

"How much?"

"Three minutes."

"Three minutes." The boy laughed once, with no amusement in it at all, in the way you laugh at a man who has measured a flood. "Perry worked an hour a week he wasn't paid for, and nobody has ever once read that out. Three minutes. That's the price of the bridge. I want you to understand that I am not saying you're a murderer, because everybody's already decided you're a murderer down at the shed, and if I start there then we're two people shouting. I'm saying that the number is on the door, and the number is three, and the man is Perry, and the machine that put one of them there is the machine you went and asked."

"Yes."

"Who was in the room with you?"

"An operator, a record keeper, and a woman from Green Span who was on a public-health rota and had to be asked because the Weirs have no standing."

The boy was quiet for a moment. "No standing," he said. "Right. That's it, then, isn't it. There's no standing, so they got somebody from Green Span. Which means the bridge was saved by a district that doesn't live on it, on a question paid for by a district that doesn't live on it, using a crew that doesn't legally exist, and there's a number on the door and it's three."

"Yes," Orrin said.

"Right." The boy pulled his cuffs down over his hands. "I'm Teo. Arden. Perry was teaching me and I'm not going to be a tuner now, because there's nothing to tune to, because nobody's on the books and the waterworks is falling apart. I've been up since Tuesday. If you've got something for me, say it now, and if you haven't, don't come and find me, because I'll be busy and I'll be angry and I'm not going to be careful."

"I've got nothing," said Orrin, which was the first completely true sentence he had said in two days, and which was also a choice, and which he was going to think about a great deal in the next week.

He stood on the wet slipway and watched the boy go, and then he went in and asked Bevin Sarr for an hour, and she gave him four, and she made him tea, and she did not leave him alone.

---

He wrote it down at the bench at half past seven in the morning with the shop shut and the door shut and the grey ledger open at a clean page, because he was a man who wrote things down, and because the only thing he had was in him.

He put in the date. He set down *Weir Two, 17th, dusk* and then he made a list, the way he had made a list on the same day of what the Loom knew, in order, with times.

*18.30 down the slipway. Nine people outside. Watchman, upturned bucket, stove.*
*19.00 second stage on. Forebay over fourth mark. Lamp in doorway. Ten minutes, I said ten minutes and I had forty.*
*Hand on the bedplate. Asked my question. — here his pencil stopped.*

He read what he had written and could not go on, because the next line was not there, and he knew the next line ought to be *Answer* and had it at the time, and there was nothing between the question and being sick over the floor.

He tried the second time, slowly, building up to it from the beginning with his hands flat on the bench and the stove going, and he got as far as the doorway, and the wet floor, and his hand on the rail, and the high thin ringing in the right ear, and then he had a hundred per cent genuine memory of the boy at the kiosk, saying *a question about Weir Two*, and counting nine coppers out of his own pocket into the boy's hand.

Both accounts were complete. Both were plausible. He had the coins in his coat to this day and there were nine of them, and he had no memory whatever of having put them there, and the second account was better than the first because it explained the nine coppers.

The first account was better because it explained why the boy had come back.

There was a minute in there, and he could put its edges on either side, and there was nothing at all in the middle of it. Not a blur. A blank with clean edges, the way a worn tooth has clean edges, and every fact on either side of it was a thing he had inferred since rather than a thing he had witnessed.

It was not tiredness. He was tired; he had not slept. He was the fittest he had been in years, his hands were steady, and he could hear a watch thirty feet away, and what he could not do was account for a minute of a wet evening in a pump house that he had been in the whole time.

He wrote, in the middle of the page, in a hand that he did not like:

*I do not know whether I read that stage or invented it. Everything after the ladder is reconstruction.*

And then, because he was his father's son and could not leave a machine alone with an unexplained mark on it, he set out the two accounts underneath it, one after the other, in full, and he could see, printed side by side, that they could not both be true, and that there was no instrument in Morrowfen that could decide between them, and that if a man had come to him at the bench that morning and said *I heard the water come up through a pipe under a floor in the Cathedral and I do not know what it was*, Orrin would have taken his gauge out of its cradle and looked at him seriously and asked him what else he had noticed.

He would have believed him. That was the discovery. He would have believed him, because a man who comes to a bench and says *I heard something and I cannot explain it* is a man with a fault in a machine, and that is how the world had taught him to hear it, and the world was wrong, and he was going to have to unlearn the whole of it, slowly, in a room by himself, over years.

---

Green Span at two in the afternoon, on the hospital line, with a card for a corridor clock that was not on his list and had been on the list for nine days.

Leda Vale was in a corridor with a ward clerk and a clipboard when he came up the stairs, and she had been on a night shift and had not gone home, and she looked at him for one second and said, "You look like a man who has been to a coroner."

"I have been to a coroner."

"Whose?"

"Perry Vance. The Weirs, Weir Two. You won't have had a name for him. He wasn't on anything."

His sister put the clipboard down on the ledge under the window, which was the thing she did instead of putting her arms around a person, and which he had learned to be grateful for.

"Go on," she said.

He told her, standing up, in three minutes, in the plainest words he had, because she was the only person in Morrowfen he would tell the whole of anything to. He told her about the note Hask had talked him out of and the boy with nine coppers and the counter closing at four. He told her the night. He did not tell her about the shove under the cathedral floor, and he did not tell her about the regulator in the case on the shelf, and he noticed both omissions the way you notice a missing stair.

Leda listened, and then she went very quiet, in the way their mother had gone very quiet.

"Say that bit again," she said. "The part about the night crew."

"The night crew is four men in a book and five on a board."

"Yes. Say the rest." She turned to the ward clerk, who had stopped pretending to write. "And there is a man in bay six who is not in that list. He is a tram driver, forty-one years old, and in the spring there was a Reading about the Green Span water schedule and he was one of the six people who stood at a rail, and he has been on the ward list since and nobody can tell me why. He is on a drip and he is talking and he is going to be all right. Do you know what is in his notes?"

"Calibration strain," said Orrin.

"Calibration strain." She said it perfectly level, and then, to the ward clerk, "He does not know what that means either, do you. Neither do I, and I have looked."

She took a folded slip out of her apron pocket and gave it to him. It was a health-office form, a carbon, the sort that came in triplicate, and the second line of the observations column read:

*Pt. reports loss of interval c. 11.40–12.05, subjective, no witness. Attrib. calibration strain. No interval recorded. Returned to ward. Adv. fluids.*

The observation had no name on it at all, because the observation was not a person, it was a category. No duration. No date of occurrence, only the fact of a day, because the phrase had no fields for any of it, and the phrase had been in the forms since before she qualified, and the phrase was about forty years old and had been bureaucratic in the days when the men who used it believed that a person's lost attention was not a municipal asset.

"Twenty-five minutes," said Leda. "He was in the corridor and then he was on the ward and he cannot tell you what he did, and he is a man who has driven the canal road for nineteen years and he can tell you the time to the minute from the bells at St Jude's, and there is no column on that form for how long it lasted, and the column for the name of the thing that took it has been filled in for forty years with a word that means *a machine adjusted itself, dear, next patient*."

"Calibration strain is the Directorate's phrase for a body toll," Orrin said. "The guild has a different one for a man who listens to a machine without a witness. Self-inflicted occupational strain. It's printed on the card by the hall door. Two phrases, one afternoon, and they don't mean each other, and if you ever hear a man in my trade use the first one for the second, he is telling you he has never once filed a question in his life."

"Both of them are a hand over somebody's mouth," said Leda, "and mine is signed by a doctor and yours is signed by nobody at all."

He could have argued. He had a genuine case: the district paid in sleep because somebody had to run the pumps at three in the morning, and the pumps were the only reason there was a district in the morning, and the hour Vance lost was an hour Vance had chosen with his own hands on a ladder he knew. He got as far as the words *somebody has to run the pumps* and stopped, because his sister was looking at him, and because it was their mother's sentence, said in this same corridor of this same hospital about the same subject in about the same weather, and because the sentence was true and also a wall.

"The pumps," Leda said. "Yes. And who asked the pumps? Who sent you up the hill to pay three minutes out of your own quarter for a bridge? Not Perry. Not the crew. The ward didn't ask. The men who drew the water didn't ask. The women whose ward clock is twenty minutes slow in a night because a stranger's question moved it, they didn't ask." She picked up her clipboard. "Perry went down that ladder for a valve because somebody had promised the city that the valve was shut. You know who did that. You've known since yesterday morning. It's not the water that did it to him. It's a sentence in a footer."

She went back down the corridor, and the ward clerk looked at the ceiling, and Orrin stood there with a carbon slip in his hand in a corridor in Green Span, and put it in his case, because a thing you have been handed is worth keeping whatever it costs to hold it.

---

At the bench, at four, he wrote a name in the grey book.

He had written hours in that book for six years. Dates and machines and what they actually did. It was a book about load and time, and everything in it was a noun.

He turned to a page and set down, in the space at the top where the description usually went:

*Perry Vance, 41. Twenty years, Weir Two nights. No licence. Shuts things by hand. Board says five, book says four. Daughter Ada, 13, tram at six.*

And then he read it, and shut the book, and sat in the cold with his hands flat on the bench, and understood that the first person he had ever written in that book was a man he had met once, for two hours, on a Wednesday, and that he had liked.

He put the kettle on at half past five, and while it came to the boil he looked out of the door at the light going off the canal, and the six o'clock tram came over the bridge at the pace of a thing that stops for everybody, and he saw her.

Thirteen, in a coat made for a smaller girl, with a satchel and her hands in the sleeves, sitting on the left-hand side of the carriage with her forehead against the glass. Nobody with her. She was watching the canal go past the way a child watches anything.

Orrin Vale stood in the doorway of a two-bench shop in the Clockwork Quarter with a cup of tea he did not want, and a street he could cross in nine seconds.

He did not count them. He watched her for a quarter of a minute, and he worked out, in the flat procedural way he worked out everything, that he had nothing to give a thirteen-year-old on a tram except a piece of information, and that the piece of information would have to be *yes, I filed the question that got your father killed*, and that there was no way on earth to say that to a child through a moving window, and that the reason he could not think of a way was that he had been waiting two days to have somebody make the way for him.

The tram slowed for the corner by the chandler's shop, which it always did, and the glass came level with the doorway, and she turned her head and looked straight at him, and he understood that she knew what he was, because in a district of four hundred thousand people a girl of thirteen had been told this week that the man who came off the hill on the Wednesday was the one who had asked the Cathedral the question, and everybody in the Weirs had something to say about that man and most of it had not been kind.

"Was it you," she said.

Not a question, quite. It came through a closed window at a walking pace and it was already going past, and he had about a second and a half in which to say four things, and he said the first one that came into his mouth, which was, "I'm sorry," which is useless, and she was gone, and the tram took her on around the corner into the Weirs.

He lifted a hand from the door frame after she had gone. A small thing, a piece of courtesy, the kind of thing a man does for a neighbour's child, and it was for nobody, and he knew that, and his arm was still up when the tram had gone round the corner into the Weirs.

He stood in the doorway with his hand still up.

That was the last small cowardice he was going to get away with in a week that had already cost a man his life, and he was not going to be able to account for that either.

---

The knock came at ten past six, and it was a girl of about nineteen in a coat two sizes too big for her, with rain in her hair and a folded receipt in her hand and the expression of somebody who has been sent to do something unpleasant and has decided to do it quickly.

"Mr Vale?"

"Yes."

"Office of Public Reckoning." She held out the receipt. "There's a clock on Halloway Street, in the Quarter, and it's five minutes slow, and the man who certified the Cathedral's clock this week is on the certificate, so they want to talk to him. The investigator's name is Venn. She's at the Rise. She's there until the last bell." The girl hesitated. "She said to say it's about a receipt, not about a clock."

Orrin took the receipt. It was a toll receipt from the Toll Stair, carbon, damp at the corners, and it had the seventeenth on it, and a district on it, and a number in a box.

He had seen the number on the stair, in chalk, in the rain, two days ago, and he had spent a day and a night being ashamed of it in six different ways.

He put the receipt down flat on the bench, very carefully, the way you put down a thing you are about to use a chisel on.
