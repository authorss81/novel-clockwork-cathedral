# Chapter 72 — The Ninth Day

Sel Kade stood down at eight in the morning on Thursday the first of the ninth month and four hundred men stood at a gate in Northglass at half past six, and nobody in the room where the first of those two things happened said one word about the second, and afterwards nobody could have said which of the two was the more extraordinary, and that is what a timetable is for.

---

**Monday the twenty-ninth, Tuesday the thirtieth and Wednesday the thirty-first of the eighth month, and the thing about those three days is that there was nothing in them to see.**

That is not a figure of speech. A foundry of four hundred men had a date, and there was no list, no rota, no branch, no deputation, no fund and no meeting, and what there was instead was the ordinary Tuesday of a works in the wrong week of the month, in which Idris Vane's own clerk had to be asked twice on the Monday whether anybody intended to do anything.

On the Tuesday morning, a man of twenty stood in the galvanising and wire works' yard on Bellweather Road and said four things to about thirty men, and they were these, and he said them in that order and did not give an order at the end of any of them.

**"I am not calling it and I am not organising it and there is nobody behind me and there is no meeting. I am going to be standing on a road on Thursday morning at about half past six and I am not going to speak to anybody. If you come out, come out because you have decided to and not because somebody told you, and if nobody comes out then it was a morning that nobody wanted and that is a complete thing that can happen.**"** 

Then he went and stood by a wall for about twenty minutes, and then he left, and about nine of the thirty followed him out of the yard, which is not a fraction anybody could do anything with.

On the Wednesday, a woman of thirty-three nailed a notice of twenty-four hours' standing to a board outside a pump house, and it was Sel Kade's notice, and it said what such a notice says, and the date on it was the Thursday, and the date on the thing Teo Arden had said in a room with a works clerk taking minutes was the Thursday, and **the two notices were nine miles apart and there was no form in this city on which anybody could have entered both of them in the same book, and nobody in this district connected them until the following Wednesday, and by then it did not matter, and that is the sentence.**

On the Wednesday evening Orrin Vale wrote in the grey book that a thing which lasts is not one morning, and that there would be a ninth day in it, and that he had no idea what a ninth day was, and that he was writing it down so that he could not afterwards pretend to have thought about the first one only.

---

**The first of the ninth, six twenty in the morning, and a gate.**

The Northglass works' gate is two leaves of steel and a guard's hut and a rail across the road in front of it, and the rail is there to stop a cart and not a man, and on Thursday morning it was in its housing, because somebody had thought about that in about 1948 and had thought about it correctly.

There were about four hundred men on the road outside it at about twenty to seven, in the rain, in wet coats, and the shift change is at half past six, and the early shift is two hundred and ten men, and the second shift comes in at two o'clock, and a foundry that stops its early shift on a Thursday morning does not stop on a Thursday morning, it stops on a Friday.

Nobody had called it. That is the thing to be exact about, because it has been repeated in this district for eighteen years in one form or another and it is not what happened. **There was no meeting, no committee, no membership, no minute book, no constituency, and no order given on a crossing, and at about half past twelve on the Monday before last a man of twenty said the date out loud in a room with a works clerk taking minutes, and asked somebody in the room to stop him, and nobody did.** The clerk wrote it down. That is all that happened on the Monday.

What happened on the Thursday is that two hundred and ten men read a works' whistle and did not go through a gate.

Idris Vane was in the hut at about ten past six. He is fifty-one and he manages four hundred men and he has nineteen years in the works and he is not a villain and he is not stupid and he is not a man who has been waiting for an occasion. He wrote a letter to a works counter four weeks ago because two people told him not to.

He did not lock the gate. He did not lock the gate and nobody has ever suggested that he should have and the reason nobody has suggested it is that a works' gate is the works' asset and a works' man has no standing to prevent his own men leaving a place of work, and there is a difference between that and the thing a person imagines a lock does.

What he did was: he came out into the road at about ten past six, on his own, without a coat, and he stood about ten feet off the gate, and he said one sentence, and then he went back into the hut and did not come out again until eleven.

**"The second shift comes in at two and I will be on this gate at two and the gate will be open at two, and I have not asked any of you for anything and I am not going to."**

That is what is on the record. It is on a works' day sheet because a foreman wrote it down, and it is not in a book in a shop, and Idris Vane has never been thanked for it and did not ask to be.

---

**Eight o'clock the same morning, four miles away, in an office ten feet by eight, and nobody said anything about either of those two things.**

She had written her notice of twenty-four hours' standing on the ward board outside on the Wednesday, and it said what such a notice says, and the two boxes were in the store against the wall with string on them.

There were six people: a clerk of the district works, a man from the pump ward's own staff, Ivy Pike, who signs for the Weir One gate and keeps a book of her own she has never shown anybody, two people from the tenant council, and a man from a letting agency on Ferrywright Row who had come about a lease and had been in the room for four minutes and understood about a tenth of it.

"I am not resigning," said Sel Kade, for the fourth time that week. "There is nobody here who can dismiss me. The district works did not appoint me and cannot, and the appointment is made under the water act by a committee that has not sat on this since 1953, and when it next sits it will find a post with a person in it and a set of keys and about nine inches of shelf."

"Then what is it," said the clerk of the district works.

"It is a woman of forty-four putting down a thing after nineteen years because **I have been the person who answers and I have never once been the person who pays, and a district where one person answers and nobody pays is not a district, it is a queue with a woman at the front of it, and there is no form in this country by which a district may be asked what it wants.**" She said it the fourth time in the fourth week and it did not improve and she did not improve it. "That is the whole of the reason. Put it in your minute in those words and do not put anything else in it."

The clerk of the district works wrote it in those words.

Then she went through it, and it took nine minutes, and it is the only part of the morning that is worth anybody's time:

**The gauge sheets for the three days before the third of the third month, in three copies, one of which goes in a box in the works' office and one of which is in a cabinet in a house on Ferrywright Row and has been in a drawer since 1951.**

**The tank's level at the end of every day since the second of the third month, in a column with no heading, in the warden's day book.**

**The variation of the thirtieth of the fifth, in a ward book, with quantity, beneficiary and date, posted within one working day, hers.**

**The review of the twenty-sixth of the fifth, four paragraphs in chalk, in a copyist's hand, with a copy on the works' desk, recording that the alarm was inhibited eleven weeks and no report was made and that no form requires one and that making one is a duty.**

**And the shelf above the barometer, taken down and put in the first box, on its edge, with the rest of it, and the line in the store's form filled in by hand: WHERE IT IS TO BE KEPT — THE STORE.**

"The shelf copy of the standing rotation is going into a store," she said. "It is the only copy in this district. It is a copy of a copy made in 1951 and it disagrees with the works' print by fifteen minutes, and there is no third copy and no run list and no accession number, and from this morning **the only place in this district in which a pumping district's water is written down is a room with a lock on it and no person in charge of it.**"

"Send it to the works," said the man from the letting agency.

"I did. On the nineteenth of the fifth. **I have got back four words and a signature and the four words are *received in your district*.**" She tied the string. "That is the whole of what a district is in this city. Received in your district."

She looked at the clock on the wall. It is a bracket clock, and it is on the district line, and it is **fifty-seven minutes and twenty seconds** slow of the reference that the city keeps its time in, and it was fifty-seven minutes and twenty seconds slow on the first of the eighth, and it is that figure now, because a step is a displacement and a displacement does not come back.

Nobody in the room looked at it. There were nine people in the district who had, at some point in the last ten weeks, stood in front of that figure.

"Same time in January," said Sel Kade, and picked the boxes up.

---

**And that is the morning, and neither half of it was said out loud in the other half, and the reason is not discretion. The reason is that a pumping district is not a ward, and there is no form on which one event in it may be minuted together with another, and by the middle of September nobody in this district will be able to find out which of the two caused which.**

---

**Friday the second, ten past two in the morning, and the tank.**

The alarm did not ring. It has not rung since the second of the third month, because the low-level alarm on the ward's reserve was inhibited on that date and no report of the inhibition was ever made and **no form in this city requires one**, and that is the sentence that is in a four-paragraph finding in chalk outside a pump house, and the finding binds nobody.

At ten past two the tank went under one day and stayed there for about one hour and fifty minutes, and in that hour and fifty minutes the hospital branch lost its pressure, and the branch is a mile and a quarter from the pumping station and it is 1 h 36 min 50 s out of the district reference, and the ward's eleven patients and three staff are on it.

It came back at about four o'clock.

There is no alarm and no report and no form. **What Green Span hospital knew at ten past two on the morning of Friday the second of the ninth month was nothing, and what it knew at four o'clock was that the clocks had come back, and the eleven patients and three staff on that branch had spent an hour and fifty-two minutes of an unregistered morning finding out by themselves.**

---

**Friday the second, half past two in the morning, at the works' gate, and a man of forty-one named Corvin Tole put his hand on a wheel.**

The boundary sluice is a 1911 clock-driven valve on the district main where it crosses into the works' boundary, and it is a works' asset, and a works' man opens it, and the men who open it are the men on the gate, and there are two of them on nights and one of them is Corvin Tole and the other is a man of nineteen called Petts who has been there eleven months.

It is not a handwheel on a pipe in a ditch. It is a gearbox the size of a small table with a reach of about four feet and a pointer and a plate, and it takes about eleven minutes to open from a cold start because the gate is a wedge and the seat has been closed since the second of the third month and there is a great deal of iron in the way, and there is an override on the side of the gearbox that is a square-headed screw and a man of forty-one can get a bar into it.

**There is a note in the warden's book, made in the fifth month, about this machine and this man.** It says that on a night in May a woman with a bad hip went up and down a ladder eleven times and that a man of about forty at the works' gate put his hand on a wheel for two hours, and it does not say his name because the warden did not know it, and it does not say why, and it is in a book in a store.

He is Corvin Tole. He is forty-one. He has been at that gate nineteen years and he came to the works at twenty-two off a canal boat and he can open that gearbox in about nine minutes once it is warm and he has never in nineteen years been thanked for anything and has never asked and is not a martyr and is not a hero and does not think about it.

He did it on his own initiative at about half past two on the Friday morning because he had watched a gauge on the district side of that sluice for nineteen years and the gauge does not lie and the gauge said the ward's tank was going down and the make-up had stopped, and the make-up comes through the wheel under his hand. He did not consult anybody because there was nobody to consult: the district works did not know it had stopped, the ward had no officer after Thursday, the hospital was two miles off and had a nurse's ward, and the foreman in the hut was twenty-six and asleep and had been for nine hours.

He opened it. The district main came up to about forty-one pounds behind it, which is seven pounds short of what it should be and has been short for eleven years, and the tank began to fill, and at about four o'clock the branch came back and Green Span's ward clocks came back with it, and the hospital did not know and has never known and will not know until somebody reads a gauge sheet.

It took him fifty-five minutes on the override and his own shoulder, and at about half past three in the morning the works' night foreman came out of the hut and stood behind him for eleven minutes and said nothing and then went back in.

**There is no column in this city for fifty-five minutes.**

---

**The hospital, in the nurses' room, from about ten past two, and the thing that happened there is the first thing of its kind in this book and it is not a miracle and it is not a reading.**

The ward's free master is a bracket clock of 1949 on the third-floor landing, about eleven inches high, and it is the clock the ward sets its own medicine intervals by hand from, and it is inside a second a day, and it is the only clock on the ward that was not on the branch.

Eleven patients. Three staff. The medicine intervals on that ward are worked off the line clocks, which is a thing a hospital does because a line clock is a reference and a reference is cheaper than a person, and at about ten past two the reference left.

**Leda Vale is a sister of twenty-seven and she has been in this city for four years and she is right about the hydraulic case in every room she has ever been in and she is on nobody's side.** She was in the nurses' room from about ten past two. What she did in the first four minutes was ordinary and it is what a nurse does: she got the master and the docket and two watches and she walked the ward, and she wrote the time of the failure and the time of the return on the ward card, and the ward card has a date column and had never before had a time in it, and there was no alarm and no report and therefore the times were the only record in the world and she wrote them on a card because a card was to hand.

**Elsie Cobb is nineteen and she is the night sister and she is paid at the ward rate and she has been named on a public sheet in this district once and has asked twice to be named and has not been taken off.**

She had the medicine docket in her apron. It is a folded card and on the back of it in pencil in a small hand there are about seventy columns of figures running down the sheet, one for nearly every night since the twenty-fourth of the fourth month, and the head line says: *Free v line. Worst gap. Who was waiting.* She has been comparing the free master with the line clocks with a watch and a piece of card every night since April, and she is not a statistician and has not been taught anything and is a night sister who can write.

What happened in that ward between ten past two and four o'clock on the morning of Friday the second of the ninth month was that eleven patients' medicine intervals were worked by hand off a bracket clock, one at a time, at the bedside, with two watches, and checked, and the free master was read against both watches at the end of it and was inside a second a day, and nobody's interval was missed.

That is all. **It is the ordinary work of a ward on a night when the reference leaves, and it has been done on this ward and on about nine hundred other wards in this city for a hundred and forty years, and it is not remarkable, and the only remarkable thing about it is that anybody timed it.**

And she timed it. Because a man who came in on the twelfth of the seventh and looked at that card for four minutes and gave it back into her apron had said one thing about it, and he had not said it as a favour, and she had not asked him to say it.

On the Saturday morning, in the nurses' room, with eleven people in it, and the date written at the top of a new column, and the column ruled with a pen that skipped:

> *New col. 2nd Sept. INTERVAL WORKED BY HAND. Night sister E. Cobb, ward rate.*
> *Line pressure lost 2.08 a.m. Restored 4.00 a.m.*
> *11 pts, 3 staff, 22 intervals worked off the free master at the bedside, each checked with two watches. Worst single interval 6 min 40 s.*
> *Total hand time for the round, 2.08 to 4.00 — **1 h 52 min.***

And she said the number out loud, in the room, because a number that is not said out loud in a room is a number in a drawer, and she had been on a card that went on four boards in June and had watched a man put his name under three hundred and sixty minutes at four minutes to ten past one in the morning, and she knew what the sentence was for.

"**One hour and fifty-two minutes,**" said Elsie Cobb. "Twenty-two intervals, eleven patients, three of us, off a clock eleven inches high on a landing, with two watches, and the worst one we had was six minutes and forty seconds and that was on the third bed and it was me and I would do it again."

Nobody argued with it. Leda Vale wrote the time and the date on the ward card and put her own name under Elsie Cobb's, which is the first time in eleven years that two names have been under a figure in that ward.

**And on the Sunday the fourth of the ninth month a clockmaker from the Clockwork Quarter read that card in about four minutes, and the number he stopped on was not the one hour and fifty-two minutes.**

It was the six minutes and forty seconds, on the third bed, with a name against it and a date and two witnesses and a watch.

**That is the first duration that has ever been written in a body column in this city with a name beside it, and it is not an inference, and it was not arrived at afterwards, and it was taken by the person who did the work while she was doing it, with two other people reading their own watches.** There is another column in a glass case in a pumping ward on the canal road, a mile and a quarter away, with a name in it and nothing at all beside it, and it is going to stay empty, and nobody may ever put a figure in it, and the two of these are not comparable and the Office's headnote in five lines says so in a sentence that took nine years to be written and one afternoon to be understood by a girl of nineteen.

He wrote that down on the Sunday night. He did not write it in a book that anybody else can see, because there is nobody to see it.

---

**The rest of it is a timetable, and this is the whole of what a timetable is.**

**Saturday the third.** The gate was shut and about three hundred and ten men were on the road, because the day shift did not come in either, and the second shift came in at two because Idris Vane was on the gate at two with the gate open, and the second shift is a hundred and forty men, and a hundred and forty men in a foundry of four hundred is about a third of it and about a third is enough to keep the furnaces and not enough to keep anything else.

**And the pattern shop came out on the Saturday.** Nobody ordered it. The pattern shop is eleven men and it is upstairs, and the eleven men came down and out through the same gate at about ten in the morning, and after that nothing in that works could be mended, because a new casting of a pattern not made in thirty years is not a thing you can buy at a price.

**Sunday the fourth.** The fettling shop. And about a hundred and forty men came back — not as an act of anybody's, but because a fettler is a man with nine years in a trade and there are four of them in Northglass and a furnace that goes cold costs a fortnight's order book, and three of the four came back at about six in the morning and the fourth did not.

**Nobody in this city can say when it ends, and that is not a failure of organisation. There is nothing to be an organiser of.** It is in stages, and it is not a strike — a strike is a thing two sides go to arbitration about, and there are no two sides, and there is no return date, and there is no fund, and there is no rule that says a man may come back on Tuesday without being asked, and about a hundred and forty men proved on the Sunday morning that they knew that.

**Nobody died. Nobody was brave. It was a foundry, on a road, in the rain, in the wrong week of the month, and the cost of it came out of a district of a hundred and forty thousand people in a currency that has no column.**

---

**And the cost, when it is written down, is water, and the number is in a warden's day book in a store.**

The gauge sheet says this, and these are the four lines and they are the whole of the cost of the first four days of it:

> *Thursday the first, at the close of the day: **1 day 14 hours.***
> *Friday the second: **1 day 6 hours at 2.08 a.m. 1 day 12 hours at 4.00.** The only thing that happened between those two figures is fifty-five minutes with a bar in an override on a gearbox at a gate.*
> *Saturday the third, at the close of the day: **1 day 4 hours.***
> *Sunday the fourth, at the close of the day: **1 day 20 hours.** Falling at about eight hours in every twenty-four with the make-up shut.*

And the alarm did not ring on any of the four nights, and no report was made, and no form requires one. The reserve has been under two days since the second of the third month, which is a standing condition and not an event, and on the fourth of the ninth there is about a day of it left.

**Those four lines are in a column with no heading in the warden's day book, and the warden's day book is in a box in the store at the pumping ward, and the warden stood down at eight o'clock on Thursday morning, and the store's form says where a thing is going to be kept, and what it says is THE STORE.**

Nobody in this city can be asked about the tank. Nobody can be shown the day book. The form that would put a pumping district into a question is a form that needs a body, and the only person who held the book is a woman who is on a bus.

---

**And at about eleven o'clock on Sunday night the works' night foreman came out of the hut and told Corvin Tole that the make-up was not to be put across the boundary again.**

It was not an instruction about the stoppage. It was an instruction about the works' own pump and the works' own electricity and the works' own boundary, and it was correct, and it is in the engineer's own hand, and it says this:

> *District make-up is not to be made across the boundary during the stoppage. The pumping is a works' asset and the works is not able to supply water across its boundary to a district having no standing under the water act, there being no form on which the supply could be entered and no person in the district who could refuse or accept it. Manual operation of the boundary sluice is a works' operation and is not to be undertaken without the engineer's written order.*

Which is true, and which is the whole of this district's condition in eleven lines typed by a man who did not have to type them, and **there is no form anywhere in this city on which a district can be given water, and so there is no form on which a district can be given nothing, and a works' man may therefore be told to do nothing by a person with a legitimate reason and be entirely right, and there is nobody at all who can be appealed to.**

He read it standing up. He read it twice. He had read it standing up, four days ago, in about eleven seconds, when a man he had not met had given him the price of a bus to come to a pump house at half past one in the morning.

Then he looked at the gauge on the district side of the sluice, which he has read every night for nineteen years and which says the ward's tank is at one day and nine hours and falling about two hours in every twenty-four, which is a figure he has never once written down and which he could not now if somebody paid him, because it is not a clock and there is no instrument for it and a number a man has in his head from looking at a dial is not a record.

**A man of forty-one, at a gearbox the size of a small table, with an override on the side of it and a bar in a locker behind him, and no order.**

He had done it for two hours in the fifth month of this year while a woman with a bad hip went up and down a ladder, and he has never mentioned that to anybody, and there is a note about it in a warden's book that does not have his name in it, and he did not put it there and he did not ask for it to be removed.

**He decided at about eleven o'clock on Sunday the fourth of the ninth month that he would open it again on Monday morning at the change of shift, and that he would do it on his own initiative, and that he would not tell the foreman, and that if anybody ever asked him he would say that he had opened the boundary because he was the man at the boundary and that was the job.**

He did not put it in a book. There was no book to put it in. The warden's day book was in a box in a store and the man who wrote in it had left, and there is no column in this city for fifty-five minutes or for four hours, and he is forty-one, and he has nineteen years in at that gate, and nobody has ever once asked him anything about it.
