# Chapter 29 — Recovery, With a Number In It

The ropeworks clock at Ombersley's is a three-dial wall piece with a fusee gone to slag and a strike train he had been promising himself for two years, and he went down on the Friday the twenty-fourth of the third month at eight in the morning and came off the wall at about two in the afternoon, and Ombersley paid him four pounds eleven and a cup of tea at half past four and said, "You are the only man I have ever had in this building who tells me the price before he starts."

"He did tell you the price before he started," said Orrin.

"He did not. He told me it would be four pounds and change and then it was four pounds eleven and change." Ombersley wiped his hands on his apron, which was what you did in a ropeworks. "Six years I have had men in here and they have all done the same thing, and you have never once done it, and I do not know whether that is because you are honest or because you are bad at asking. Go on, I do not care which."

There was nothing wrong with that clock that a good clean and a new strike spring would not have fixed, and it took six hours on the wall and every one of them was a trade he had been doing since he was fourteen, and at the end of it Orrin Vale sat on a sack in a ropeworks in the Clockwork Quarter with his back against a wall and his hand open on his knee, feeling the hand close and open, close and open, four or five times, like a man testing a hinge.

It did not close cleanly. The little finger still came late. It had been that way since the seventh of the second month and it was better on a good day's sleep and worse on a bad one, and there was no way on this earth to certify a thing with it, and a fusee and a strike train cannot be done with the side of a thumb.

He wrote in the shop book on the Friday night, in the ordinary book, not the grey one: *Ropeworks, Verge Lane side. Three-dial, fusee repinned, strike train cleaned, spring renewed. 6 hrs on the wall, 6½ in the shop. £4 11s incl. materials. Paid.*

He looked at that line for a while and found that it was the first line in that book in six weeks that he had not felt sick about afterwards, and that it was about a fusee.

---

The chandler's clock on Weir Lane was four minutes slow on the Saturday morning and he had it out of its bracket by half past ten.

He got the case off the wall, and the bracket, and then he stopped with the bracket in his hand and stood in the middle of the road, because he knew exactly what he was about to do and he knew exactly what he was about to do instead, and this was a small thing on a wet morning and it took him a full minute and a half.

He could have wound it forward four minutes with a bar in about eleven minutes. He had done it that way twice before, to a clock on Weir Lane and to a bracket clock at the Ferrywright corner, both of them inside six weeks — and both times it had been correct work, and both times it had cost nobody anything, and both times it had gone into a form in a hall on the nineteenth as a line in a recommendation column, because there is no other column in Morrowfen where a hand correction can sit, and because a man is not a fault. Two of them, both his own. He knew the count. It was two and it was going to stay two.

He put the bracket on the bench and went and got the guild tariff card down from the shelf where it had been since he was twenty-two, and it was a dirty card in a frame, and the out-of-shop rate was four shillings and sixpence the hour, which was also the renewal fee, which was a coincidence of institutional life that had never meant anything and that he had noticed on the Friday for no reason he could name.

*Item 1. Street or estate clock, not a civic machine. Attendant, at the customer's request, six hours. Shutter taken in and refitted. Rating of the bell-wire renewed, with materials. Total £1 7s 6d.*

He wrote the estimate out on a pad in duplicate, gave one to the chandler, kept one, and the chandler said, "For a clock?"

"For the clock, for the bracket, for the bell-wire, and for the certificate. I have to certify it if it is a civic machine and I have to write down what I found, and the writing down is four minutes of the six hours, and the four minutes is yours to pay for because the law says I have to do it in that form at that rate."

"The bracket's out of true."

"The bracket's out of true and it has been for four years, which is why the clock's slow, and I am not going to put a bar to a clock that's hanging on a bracket at an angle and call it a repair." He got the spirit level. "I have done that. I did it in the month of the second, on a lane in the Weirs, in about an hour, and it took eleven minutes and it is not in a receipt and it never will be."

He took the dial off and the bell-wire out and did the bracket true and re-bushed the steady pin and re-rigged the escapement, and it was six hours, and the invoice was one pound seven shillings and sixpence, and the chandler paid it in coin on the counter without arguing and said, "You are the only man I have ever had in this street who has told me the price of a thing before doing it and then charged exactly that."

Orrin wrote the certificate. It took four minutes and it is the shortest thing he has ever written: *Street clock, Weir Lane. Bracket found out of true, steadied and re-bushed. Escapement re-rigged. Bell-wire renewed. Within tolerance. No fault. No recommendation.*

Four minutes. He had spent eighteen minutes on one in the second month, on a certificate, reading the reverse twice before he pressed it, and the reverse of a certificate is not read by anybody, and he had done it anyway because the deal was the deal.

The whole job had cost one pound seven shillings and sixpence and six hours, and the useful part of it — the part that made the clock right — was about forty minutes, and the rest of it was paper and honesty and a bracket.

He wrote it up in the shop book. *Chandler's, Weir Lane. Clock 4 mins slow, cause bracket out of true. 6 hrs. £1 7s 6d. Certificate issued, 4 min.*

And then he wrote the second line underneath it, in the same book, which he had never done before, because he had never had a customer to whom he owed an explanation:

*A bar would have been eleven minutes and nothing. This is the same repair done the way a licensed man has to do it. I have charged £1 7s 6d and six hours for forty minutes of work, and every district in the Weirs has just been charged twenty-six minutes, and not one of them can put a name on the charge, and not one of them can invoice anybody for what the charge cost them, because there is no such thing as an invoice from a district.*

He read that back and did not know whether it belonged in a shop book, and left it in.

---

The shop was shut on the Sunday the twenty-sixth, as it always was, and he was in the flat above the bathhouse by nine with the grey book on the table and rain on the window, and the hand would not close properly, and the right ear had a hum in it that came and went with the weather.

He did not sleep well on the Saturday and he was tired on the Sunday and he knew the tiredness was not the thing it had been in the month of the second, when it came with clean edges and no residue and took something out of the middle. This was ordinary tiredness, in a man of thirty-two, at the end of a week of standing at other people's walls.

He did the entry for the twenty-eighth of the second month, which he had not done, because the whole of that day had gone on a warm room and a bad cup and a man who had told him the truth kindly, and the only part of it he could honestly put on paper was this:

> *Tuesday the 28th, the second month, ten o'clock, Hask's room. Forty minutes. The card carries four things and asks for four things and the expiry is the nineteenth of the third month. I did not lie to him and I did not tell him. I said there were four days in the month of the second when I was not on the board, and a hospital, a yard and a timer, and I left out a governor with a thimble in the flank that I made in the month of the first, and I left it out because he had brought it to me as scrap and I had refused, and I could not think of the sentence in which a man refuses a master and then says: by the way, I have been at it again since.*

> *He gave me the second sheet of the W-4, which nobody asks for, and told me the counter is open on Sundays, which is true and which I did not know, and told me the escapement on his own bench wants oiling, which I could not have heard in a room full of men, and which I would have found in nine minutes at nineteen years old.*

> *Cost of that morning: nineteen days of margin, spent in forty minutes. Nothing else. No fee moved, no date moved, no threat made. I have been leashed by a form and a conversation since the twenty-fifth of the first month and the leash has never once been pulled and it is not a rope and it is not going to be. I want that written down while I still feel it, because in six weeks the feeling will have gone and I shall think I imagined it.*

Then he turned to a clean page, because he had been working on something for a week and it wanted writing down and it was not a machine.

> *The pumping district's own count. Weir Two, night pumping, four months to the end of last quarter: three hundred and eighty-eight hours.*
>
> *Five men on a roster, one of them not certified and never enterable on any form, and the number is mine to say because a woman in Weir Lane counted it off a board in a pump house and a book kept on a table, and the Assembly's public-health committee ordered the returns printed in full with the minutes, and a ward clerk copied them out at a pound four and ten out of her own ward's allocation, and they are in a binder on Cathedral Rise and anybody may read them. I did not count it. I have not got the faintest claim to it. I can say three hundred and eighty-eight at a table in a room tonight, out loud, without checking, because a district counted it and a chamber printed it.*
>
> *So: three hundred and eighty-eight hours worked at a civic water station at night, and the certified return for those four months is nil, and the daytime maintenance line at the same station for the year is fourteen hundred and six hours, honest, certified, paid, and not in dispute.*
>
> *And the price. Two shillings the head for a year. Four men. Eight shillings. That is what the hall paid, in a year in which four men stood on a station at night for three hundred and eighty-eight hours, and the class rate has not moved in nine years, and the price schedule is the hall's own property and is not ashamed of itself.*
>
> *Eight shillings to not pay three hundred and eighty-eight hours. That is the whole of the eleven years. Not a corruption. A price. Somebody set a class for it, and a form for the class, and a rate for the form, and the rate is cheaper than the hours, and it is cheaper by about a factor of two hundred, and the arithmetic is so lopsided that it cannot be a mistake, and it is not a mistake, because a mistake would be somebody having no idea what a night's work costs, and the price schedule knows exactly what a night's work costs, because the price schedule is a page of a hall's book with hourly rates in it for a great many classes of work.*

He stopped there, and read it, and did not like where it went, and went and looked at where it went.

> *Which means that a printed return of three hundred and eighty-eight hours is a bill.*
>
> *Not a bill to the guild — the guild has paid its eight shillings and the eight shillings are two years old. A bill to the district. A district that has proved it worked three hundred and eighty-eight hours without being paid for them has proved that the rates of the pumping ward have been set on a return that was nil when it should not have been nil, and a ward's rates are set on a certified return, and the district is eleven years behind on a sum that nobody has counted, and every pound of it comes off a reserve that has been empty since the diversion, and the reserve is empty because seventy-one households on Carrow Street got water in March instead of a ward that had none.*
>
> *So the number I can now say out loud without checking is a number that, if it is believed, takes money from the poorest ward in this city to pay the city for a clock that has been wrong for four months. And I have wanted to be able to say that number since the seventh of the second month.*

He put the pencil down and worked out what to do about it, and there was nothing, and that was the finding.

Then he wrote one more line, because it was true and because he was tired and because it was the only reward available.

> *There has been no ninth beat since the seventeenth of the first month. I have not gone looking. I notice that I have not gone looking and I do not know what to do with that, and I have had ten Sundays to do something about it and I have not, and I have concluded that the honest thing is to record that the ninth tick has not come and that I stopped listening, and that those are two facts about a man and not about a machine, and that one day one of them will turn out to be the other.*

---

On the Monday the twenty-seventh he certified the Clockwork Quarter's public clock, which is the reference for every timetable in this city and the only movement in it that is held to nothing but itself.

It took two hours and forty minutes. He could not tell by the sound, and he had known he could not tell before he took the cover off, and he had brought the gauge anyway and the sounding board and the second watch, and he did the whole thing the long way, in silence, with a lead and a vernier, and it was correct to a tenth of a second on four readings, and it was the best movement in that district, and it had been out by twenty-six minutes for about an hour on the eighth of the month and had been wound back, and there is no record anywhere in Morrowfen that it happened.

He made the certificate, and it is a good certificate, and he stamped it, and the man who came for it was a clerk from a tram office who took it and said thank you and went out.

Then Orrin stood alone in the Quarter for about a minute with the gauge in his hand, and worked out, the way he worked everything out, that from the seventh of the second month to this Monday was six weeks and six days, and that in that time he had got faster at checking and no better at hearing, and that checking is a thing you do in the room, and that a man who has to be in the room cannot be in two rooms in a week, and that the room is the thing he has just lost, and that it is not coming back, and that nothing had been strained and nothing had broken and there was nothing to mend.

The renewal was over on the Sunday. Nothing had happened. He was a licensed maintainer with a receipt and a stamp and a wet day in a ledger, and there was no inquiry, and no letter, and no question, and the Archive of Unlisted under the Cathedral was exactly as shut as it had been on the afternoon a man wrote two words in chalk on a valve spindle in daylight, and he had not been near it and had not wanted to and had done nothing about it, which was a piece of discipline and not a piece of progress.

---

He went down to Weir Lane on the Tuesday the twenty-eighth at half past four in the afternoon, exactly four weeks after a warm room and a bad cup, and there were three women in Wenna Pruitt's kitchen and each of them had a different idea what the eighth had cost their street.

That is the part he had not been ready for, and it took him the best part of an hour to understand that it was not a disagreement. It was three correct answers.

Mrs Crane — Bessie Crane — had it in one line and she said it standing up, with her coat still on, because she had come from the laundry and was going back to it. "The Reading is a good thing. In the spring of last year the front went under at the Bellweather end and I lost eleven days' work and four women's hours in the wash and the whole of the standing water came into my back room, and if that front holds through the tides then I have had a good month for nothing, and I am not going to stand in this kitchen and be sorry about a schedule that stops that happening. I am sorry about the other thing. I want to be clear that I am not confusing them."

Ivy Pike, from the Weir One gate, had a factory clock and a pay book and a different complaint. "It is not a good thing and it is not a bad thing, it is a *thing*, and the trouble is that the day is not the night. Everybody is talking about the four to eleven minutes late on the front and the school and the ferry, and those are real, and those are on the notice, and I will tell you the truth: our men come in at ten and go off at two and the notice does not touch us at all. The notice touches the water. The water is the whole of what we are, and there is more of it in the night than there is in the day, and it is now going to be handed back in the day in four goes, and four goes is not the same as one go, and my men have to be standing at the gate for each of them, and that is a shift I cannot pay for and I am not going to pay for."

And Mrs Tallow, who was seventy-one and had been a sister in outpatients at the Green Span hospital for thirty years, and who had come along unasked because she was a neighbour of Mrs Pruitt's and had heard there was a man in the room who wrote things down.

"None of those," she said, "and I shall tell you what it is, because I have been the one who is wrong about it since the eighth and I am the only one here who is going to be honest."

They all looked at her.

"Four to eleven minutes late on eleven nights is an hour and a half of schooling, and that is a fact, and it is in a book in a school." Mrs Tallow put her hand flat on the table, which she had got from Leda and not from anybody and knew she had. "The ward on the third floor of that hospital lost eleven minutes on one Tuesday, and that is a fact, and it is in a sister's book. And a man walked round the Ferrywright approach in the dark at ten past two in the morning and was brought back at four, and that is a fact, and it is in nothing at all.

"So: here is what the eighth cost, and I have been getting it wrong for three weeks and I want it said that I have been getting it wrong. **It cost the things nobody wrote down.** Not the things that were written down. The eleven minutes and the hour and a half and the four to eleven minutes on the front are all written down, and every one of those is a *cost the machine admitted*, and the machine admits them because they belong to things that are in the model, and the school is in the model and the ward clock is in the model and the ferry is in the model. And the man with the lamp is not in the model, and his two hours are not a cost, and I have been counting the cost of the eighth in the wrong places for three weeks because those are the places there are numbers in."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That is the whole of it," said Mrs Tallow. "And it is not news. It has been the shape of this since the seventeenth of the month before last, and everybody in this room has known it since then, and I have been sitting on this floor for three weeks waiting for one of you to say it in a sentence, and none of you has, because every one of you is waiting for a number, and there is not going to be one, and the not-going-to-be-one is the cost."

---

He wrote it up that night at the bench, standing, with the rain on the shop window, and it took him an hour, and it went into the grey book, in his own hand, on a clean page, with the three women's names and the date of each and the fact that one of them had come unasked and one of them had come from a laundry and one of them was a different woman every time.

And then he did the thing that made the whole week different and that he had not planned and that he did not entirely know why he did.

He wrote it again, on a separate sheet, in a fair hand, because he has done a fair copy of a passage of a labour book correctly about nine hundred times and a fair copy is not a copy, it is a statement of what a page establishes.

And then he took it down to Weir Lane on the Wednesday morning and gave it to Wenna Pruitt, and told her what it was for, and told her the truth about it, which is that it was a fair copy of a page in a book nobody can read, made by a man with no standing, in the guild's sense of the word and in every other.

She read it at the table with the lamp on. It was a long sheet. It had three disagreements in it and it did not resolve them, and it was correct.

"That is the first thing anybody in this district has handed me that is any use at all," said Wenna Pruitt, "and I am not going to say thank you, because it is not yours, and because in about four months somebody is going to ask who wrote it, and I want the answer to be the three of them and a clockmaker and nobody else." She put it under the eleven years. "It goes in with the returns. The committee's next sitting is on the fourth of next month, and the returns go in with the minutes, and the printing is paid for until the end of the summer, and I shall put it in the appendix, and there is no form for an appendix, so the clerk will do it because I will ask her in a corridor, and that is the whole of the mechanism and it is a woman saying yes in a corridor."

She looked up.

"Three hundred and eighty-eight," she said. "You have been saying that number for a week. Where did you get it?"

"I counted it," said Wenna Pruitt. "In the kitchen. In the first month, on a Tuesday, off a board in a pump house and a book on this table, with a young man who cannot read a number aloud without wanting to be watched doing it. You have been able to say it since the first month, and you have had it in a book ever since, and it has been in the Assembly's own minutes since the twentieth of last month, and you did not have it on Tuesday because nobody ever said it to you out loud in a room, and I have been waiting nineteen years for somebody to need a number I had already got."

She shut the book on the lamp.

"You are getting better at this," she said, "and I want to be careful, because I do not know yet whether what you are getting better at is useful."
