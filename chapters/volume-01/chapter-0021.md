# Chapter 21 — Nine Copies and a Method

The pump house door was open when he got there at ten on Tuesday morning, and it was open because Bevin Sarr had it open, and that was the same sentence and she had never once pretended otherwise.

The rain had come back in the night and was coming off the slipway in sheets. Inside, the second stage was running and the place smelled of hot oil and wet iron and the tea that four men had been drinking in shifts for twenty years. Bevin Sarr was on the step in a coat that had been mended at the cuff four times, with the board-hook over her shoulder and chalk on the side of her right hand.

"You've come to look at the board," she said.

"I have."

"Then look at it and then tell me what you came for, because they're different."

The board was four feet by three and hung where the old plan used to hang, and there was a lamp on a bracket above it that had been added in about nineteen hundred and something and had never once been the right height. It was covered in chalk in two hands, and the top line, in different chalk, still said what it had said on the twenty-fourth of the first month: *board is ours. board is not the works'.*

He looked at it properly, because he had not looked at it since the seventh of last month.

"Three columns," said Bevin Sarr. "That's all it is. Name, station, hours. Every night, going back — " she tipped her head at the bottom third, which was the older chalk, the paler, " — back to before Perry."

"Nine years of it."

"Ten. The bottom of that is Amos's father's hand and Amos does not know it is up there, and I have told him, and he said leave it." She put the hook down against the wall. "Now. What do you want."

"I want it to be a thing that can be produced in a room."

"No."

She said it without any weight in it at all, the way she said everything.

---

"You can't take it out of here," she said. "Not because I won't. Because of what it does to me. You take that board out of this building, then it is a thing that was in a building, and then it is a thing that was somewhere else, and then somebody asks where it was in between, and I have got to have an answer, and the answer is going to be that it was in a shed on the Kestrel side with a lock on it, or under a bed, or in a hatbox in Slough Lane where they moved twice in the spring. And the first person they ask is me, because I have had the chalk on my hand every working day since the spring, and I will lie, and I will be a woman who lies about a shift board, and everything else on that board stops being worth anything to the people it belongs to."

"That is not nothing."

"It is the whole of it." She turned round and put her hand flat on the wall under the board, the way a person touches a thing to prove it is still there. "Mr Vale, I have watched this district lose every single thing it has ever been able to hold. Not to anybody. To rooms. A thing goes into a room and it comes out a matter that was before a room, and the room is the point of it after that, and the people it was about are a thing that was before. Eleven years my aunt kept a book in a kitchen and she was right and she told me so, and I have never once said she was wrong."

"Then where does it live?"

"Here." She knocked the wall with the back of her hand. "On a wall, where the men who wrote on it can get at it, and where nobody has to be trusted with it, and where a man from a hall or a committee has to come and stand in the cold and be introduced to it by somebody from here. That is what a place is. If you want it to be a document then you have to have a document, and a document has to have a place it lives, and I am telling you on a Tuesday morning that the place it lives costs me nothing and a document costs everybody something, and I do not know yet what and I am not going to find out on my own."

---

He wrote it down on the Thursday in the middle and found, when he read it back, that he had written down a *method*, and that the method was four questions, and that he had not gone looking for four questions and that it had arrived in a week because four women had refused him politely in three different rooms.

*Who holds it.*

*Who copies it.*

*Who may be asked for it.*

*Who may not.*

He had the first answer on the Tuesday from Bevin Sarr, standing up, without sitting down.

He had the second answer on the Wednesday morning in a kitchen over a chandler's shop in Weir Lane, and it took most of the morning.

---

Wenna Pruitt was thirty-eight in the way that a person is thirty-eight if they have been keeping four ledgers on one table for eleven years, which is to say that she looked about as old as the table.

She had put the kettle on before he came up the outside stair, which he noticed, and the room was two rooms: the kitchen, and a passage with four ledgers under a board and a bundle of blotters and a lamp with a bend in the flex that had been mended with tape three times.

"Sit," she said. "Don't say anything for a minute, and don't look at the table, because if you look at the table first you will look at the wrong one."

He sat.

"There are four," said Wenna Pruitt. "The district's own shift book, eleven years, and it begins in the autumn. My aunt's, going back twenty, in the same hand and a different ink, and I can vouch for every year of it because I was in the room for most of them. And the one that goes back forty, which is a different book and not a shift book, because the thing that happened in the first year of it is not a shift. And a rent book, which is a rent book, and which I keep because the rent book is how I know who is in these houses." She put her hand flat on the first one. "Fifty-one years of the family keeping something in this kitchen on this table, and I have reconciled all three of them twice and I will do it a third time if anybody asks me in a room, and I would like the asking to be in a room, and I would like it to be a question with a number in it."

"You're the only one who can do that."

"I am the only one who can do that, and that is the reason nobody will let me do it, and both of those are true at once." She took her hand off the book. "Now tell me what you have come to say, and I shall tell you whether it is the fourth time somebody has said it to me this year."

---

Orrin said it. He said it in the plain procedural voice he used for a fault, because he had learned from Leda that anything said in another voice in a kitchen becomes a speech.

"A counter-record that is a complaint is worth what a complaint is worth. It becomes a method or it stays an evening. And a method means somebody has to be asked for it, and if you cannot say who may be asked then anybody may ask and anybody may be refused, and the refusal is the thing that gets written about, not the hours."

"Somebody in this room has got a list of eleven addresses," said Wenna Pruitt. "And that is the most frightening object in this city and it is in this room and it is not mine, and I have known about it for three weeks, and I have not asked him for it, and I am not going to."

He looked.

"No," said Wenna Pruitt. "I am not going to. If I ask him for that list then the list is mine, and then I am a woman with a list of eleven addresses in a district where four men were in a well a month ago, and I have a table and a lamp and a lease on nothing, and the day anything happens to me the list goes the way your book goes if anything happens to you." She folded her arms. "And he is twenty. He is twenty and he has eleven addresses and no index, and an index is a thing with an owner, and the owner of the index is the person the index is about, and I have watched him not do that on purpose and I would rather it stayed not done on purpose than be fixed by a man of thirty-two from the Quarter who does not live here."

"I wasn't offering to take it."

"I know you weren't. That is what is nice about you and it is not the same as being safe." She got up and filled the kettle again, which was not necessary, and which he understood. "But you have made it into a method, and I want to say the thing about methods before I say the thing about my book, and the thing about methods is this and it is not a complaint."

"Go on."

"Every one of those four questions has an answer and every one of the answers is a bill, and the bill is not money, and it is time, and it is the same time every week, and it is the time of people who are not in the book." She set the kettle down. "The holding is free. The copying is not. And whoever does the copying is a person whose hours now exist in a second place that is not the hall's and is not the Council's and is not anybody's, and one day one of them is going to want paying for it, and the day that happens you have got an employer, and an employer is a man with a list."

There was a sound from the passage and neither of them had heard her come in.

Bessie Crane put her handbag on the table, took a folded apron out of it, and shook the apron out, and said, "I have been in the passage since you said *bill*."

She was about sixty. She ran the Bellweather Road laundry, which was the ninth series in the hall's copybook and the one that was not a pump or a stair or a night gate, and she employed thirty-one women, and she had come up the outside stair without knocking because she came up that stair twice a day.

"Say it again," said Bessie Crane. "Say the bill bit again and say it to me, because I have been standing in that passage working out that I am the person you are talking about, and I would rather be told than worked out."

---

"Every copy of that page needs making by somebody," said Wenna Pruitt, "and the somebody is not in it, and the hours of the somebody are not in it either, and if a district ever gets to the point where the record of its own hours is a document, then somebody will have to be paid to keep the document, and the somebody will be one of the people in it, and that is the sentence and I have been saying it for eleven years and nobody has wanted it said because it is the least interesting sentence in this city."

"Twenty minutes a page," said Bessie Crane. "For a page this size, done properly, in my own hand, and I know the proper way because I used to do the works' laundry sheets before I had the Bellweather Road and I did them for nineteen years. I can do a page in twenty minutes and I have done a page in a quarter of an hour when I was twenty-four and I have not tried in a long time."

Nobody said anything.

"And nobody pays for it," said Bessie Crane, "and nobody is going to, and I am not a woman who expects to be paid, and I want that understood before anybody starts thanking me. I have a laundry. I have thirty-one women in it and I pay them on the Friday. If I sit up on a Tuesday night copying a page so that a district can be believed, then the Tuesday is mine, and nobody is having it, and I have made my position perfectly clear in about four seconds and I would like the meeting to move on."

There was no meeting. Orrin became aware, standing in a kitchen with his hand on the back of a chair, that there had been a meeting for about nine minutes and that he had not started it and could not have stopped it.

---

The compromise was made on the Wednesday afternoon in Wenna Pruitt's kitchen and it took two hours and it was a compromise, and both of the people who made it said so at the time.

There is no owner. The ledger goes round. Nine buildings, nine holders, and the custody moves on a date that is written on the inside cover of nobody's book because the inside cover of nobody's book is a place a person could take a hall form off and read. It moves on a Friday. It moves to a building that has not held it, and it is carried by a person who is going that way anyway.

The copying is done by whoever is doing it, unpaid, and the fact of the copying is entered in the *recommendation* column of nothing, because there is no column, and the only record of it is a list of initials in a book in a kitchen, and Wenna Pruitt's aunt wrote a list of initials in a book in this kitchen for forty years and nobody ever asked to see it and it is the reason the forty years exist at all.

Who may be asked: anybody who can say what they want it for, to somebody in the district, in a room the district is in.

Who may not: the hall, the works, the Cathedral, the Office of Public Reckoning, and anybody acting for the insurance of a building in the district. And there was an argument about the last one that lasted forty minutes and that Wenna Pruitt won in a single sentence, which was that a landlord's insurer is not a person and the district is not a thing that is owed an answer by a company, and that Hanna Stroud would say the same and would be right, and that if anybody ever wanted Stroud's address they could have it off the top of the stair like anybody else.

"That is a compromise," said Wenna Pruitt, when it was done, "and I want it written down that I said so, because a compromise is going to fail, and I have watched two of them fail in this room and I have watched what happens. We are going to hold nine copies of a thing that nobody has asked for, and the day somebody asks for it — and somebody will, and it will be somebody we want to give it to — one of the nine is going to be in a kitchen in a bad mood, and one of the nine is going to be in a house where the woman is ashamed, and one of the nine will be gone, because one of the nine will have gone to the country or died or stopped speaking to the others, and that is not a reason not to do it. It is the reason to write down *where the copies are* and then to be extremely careful about who is allowed to know that."

"Then do not write down where the copies are," said Orrin.

"No," said Wenna Pruitt. "That is exactly the thing I have been arguing with you about for two hours, and you have not noticed, and I am going to let it stand because I am tired and because you will work it out in a week."

---

The Kestrel side of Northglass came on the Thursday, at about eleven, with a boy of fifteen holding the door for a woman of sixty-five who was wearing her good coat in a shop, which is a thing you do in Northglass and never do in the Quarter.

She was the landlady, and her name was Nell Arter, and she had come because Teo Arden had asked her to, and she said so before she sat down, and she said it in a way that made Orrin understand that Teo had told her exactly what she was walking into.

"Two floors of a house on Ashcuttle Lane," she said. "Nine women on that stair, and the stair light, and I have owned it nineteen years, and every one of them has been washing on that stair since before I bought it. One of them is fifty-eight. Her name is Constance Kear and she is a presser and she cannot lift her arms above her head on a Tuesday."

"What is the entry?"

"Batch twenty-two slash one. Maintenance-deferred. Nine entries, six years." Teo put the sheet on the counter. "One crew, three nights. Written the same way as yours, in the same class, on the same kind of line, and it is not a copy of yours, it is the same *form*, and the hall writes it the same way for a stair in Northglass that it writes it for a pump in the Weirs, which I did not know and which I have not stopped thinking about since the Wednesday before last."

"And what do you want?"

"I want her hours in a place where they exist," said Nell Arter. "Not my hours. Hers. I have a rent book and it is a rent book. I am not the woman anybody is asking about and I would be obliged if nobody wrote my name on anything this month."

---

It took until about two, and what it came down to was a thing Orrin had known since he was sixteen and had never once had to say out loud to a room.

A fair copy is not a copy. He did nine hundred a year for his own shop, in a hand that was his own, from a book he could not lose, and he had taught two apprentices the trick of it, and the trick is that you do not write down what the page says. You write down what the page *establishes*, and then you check that what you have written establishes the same thing. If the original has a fault in it, a fair copy has the same fault in it, in your hand, and your name at the bottom. If the original has a gap in it, a fair copy has a gap in it, and a gap is a thing you can only reproduce by leaving a space, and leaving a space is a decision and the decision is yours.

"You want to know how to enter a woman who is not in the record," he said. "That is not a copying problem. That is a forms problem. And I cannot solve a forms problem with a pen, and I want to say that now, before we spend a day on it, because the day is going to be worth about four hours of Bessie Crane's Tuesday and it is going to get us exactly nowhere and I would rather we spent it noticing that than not noticing it."

"Then what is it," said Nell Arter.

"It is the fact that the only way a person gets into a record of work is if the record was made by somebody who had to write something else at the same time." He turned the sheet round on the counter. "Look at this. The batch, the class, the head count. Somebody had to write the number of a batch. Somebody had to sign for a district. There is a person in that line somewhere, and they were not thinking about your women, and that is the only reason they are in it. The guild clerk who writes *one crew, three nights* is not a person who knows about a crew. She is a person filling a column. And there is no column anywhere in that hall for a woman who presses shirts on a stair on Ashcuttle Lane, and it is not because anybody decided there shouldn't be one. It is because a column is filled by somebody who is already writing something."

Nobody had anything to say for about a minute.

"Then it's the batch," said Teo Arden. "It has to be the batch. Get a different batch number in and she goes in with it."

"No," said Orrin. "She goes in as a *new batch*, which is a new withdrawal, which is a new price, and the price is two shillings the head for a year, and Mrs Arter's insurer will read the new batch and the district will be uninsurable on a new number, and the nine of them will be on a page in a hall saying they were withdrawn from work in the year they were working."

Teo Arden stood very still, which was the thing he did instead of swearing.

"Right," he said. "Right. Well. That is where that goes, then."

"That is where that goes," said Orrin Vale. "And I am sorry, and it is not a small sorry, because I have watched you spend three weeks on it and I have watched two women in good coats come across a city for it."

"Then say the next part."

"There is no next part. That is the next part. There is a hall form that will not hold a woman, and a price schedule that will hold a woman at two shillings, and a copyist who signed a true copy eleven times without knowing what a standby was, and I have got no instrument. I have got a bar and a book and a rule I wrote on a Sunday, and the rule says I do not get to be the one who decides."

"Mr Vale," said Nell Arter, from the front of the counter, in a good coat. "I did not come here to be entered. I came because the boy asked me to, and I would like to say one thing and then I will take my coat off this hook and go home. Nine of them on that stair, and not one of them has ever been asked whether she wants to be in a record. Have you."

"No," said Orrin. "No, I have not."

"Well," said Nell Arter. "Then you have noticed the thing in the last ten minutes that this whole trade has not noticed in twenty years, and you noticed it in a shop, and I hope you write it down."

She took her coat off the hook and put it on and went out, and Teo Arden stood in the middle of the shop looking at the door.

---

The eight of them turned up at the pump house on the Thursday evening at about seven, which was twenty minutes before anybody had arranged anything, and which Orrin was told about by a boy who came down the slipway at half past six to say that Mrs Pruitt wanted him because there was tea in the pump house and Bevin Sarr had put the kettle on.

There were eight. Bessie Crane, who had come up for her coat and stayed. Wenna Pruitt. A woman called Ivy Pike from the Weir One gate, who Orrin knew only as a signature. A man of about fifty called Renn Corby, who was a rigger and kept a copy because he had a strong hand and had been asked. A boy of nineteen called Ocky, who had brought it as far as the second step of the pump house and no further, on his own account. Two women from Carrow Street whose names Orrin did not get, and one man who turned out to be the chandler over whose shop the kitchen sat, and who had come because the chandler's shop was a building like any other and a copy in a shop was a copy in a business and a business is a thing that has premises.

The point of the eight of them being in a room at once, on a Thursday evening, in the twentieth year, was that it had never happened before and Orrin understood that inside about ninety seconds that it was not going to happen again, and that whatever the eight of them had come for, it was not tea.

Bevin Sarr did not come out of the pump house. She came to the door and looked at the eight of them on the wet slipway in the rain and said, "There is tea in there and it is bad, and I have made it for four, and you are all going to have to share the mugs."

She went back in.

Orrin stood at the top of the slipway out of the rain with his case in his hand and worked out the thing, the way he worked out things, by counting, and what he counted was not minutes.

Twenty years. Four ledgers in one kitchen. Nine buildings, nine copies, and no index. Eleven addresses in a list that was not in the room. And now eight people in one place, on a wet slipway, in the same hour, having agreed nothing.

The point was that the district now had a date in it.

---

He wrote it up in the grey book that night, on a clean page, standing up, in the shop, with the lathes cold.

*Tuesday the 21st to Thursday the 23rd, the second month. Four questions asked and four answers given, in a pump house and a kitchen and a shop, and none of them by me.*

*Holding is free. The copying is not. Bessie Crane, of the Bellweather Road, says she can do a page in twenty minutes and has not tried in a long time and will not be paid, and she said that in four seconds and asked us to move on, and we should have moved on and did not.*

*Compromise, and I said so in the room and I meant it: a ledger that goes round on Fridays to a building that has not held it, carried by a man who is going that way anyway. No owner. Nobody may be asked who cannot say what they want it for. The hall, the works, the Cathedral, the Office, the water users' committee, and the insurer of any building in the district, may not be asked.*

*It is going to fail. Wenna Pruitt says so and has watched two of them fail from the same table. I believe her, and I have said so, and I want it here that I believed her at the time, because in about four months one of the nine is going to be in a kitchen in a bad mood and I do not want to have been the man who said he knew.*

*And on the Thursday evening, without anybody arranging it, eight of the nine were in one place at one hour. That is the first time it has happened in twenty years. It is also the first time the district has a date in it, and a date is the only thing a record needs to become an event, and an event is a thing that can be described afterwards by a man in a good office who was not in the room. We have not been described yet. We will be.*

*I do not know what to do about that. I have written it down instead, which is the only tool I have ever had, and it is the same tool the shop has, and it is nine coppers, and it is not in any office in this city.*

---

He got the shop shut at about eleven, which was late, and he had not eaten, and he stood on the outside stair for a minute in the wet with his back against the door and worked out how much of the three weeks between now and the nineteenth was left.

Twenty-four days. A form, a fee, an interview on Tuesday, and an hour on a Sunday in which all of it had to happen at once, because the counter on the licensing floor did one thing on one day between ten and twelve, and he had read the standing notices on Monday and had not liked any of them.

The shop was warm behind him and the lathes were cold and there was a greenish smear on the bench where he had been working on a regulator that was sound and that he had been paid three shillings for.

Twenty-four days. And a district in the Weirs had a date in it and a boy of twenty had eleven addresses and a woman of sixty-one could make a page in twenty minutes and would not be paid, and a landlady had walked across a city in her good coat to be told that a hall's column would not hold a woman, and eight people had stood on a slipway in the rain because there was tea in a pump house.

He went in and shut the door and made tea he did not want and stood in the doorway with it, and did not listen for anything, because there was nothing to listen for, and because he had noticed on Sunday that he was no longer listening, and he had not written down what to do about that either.
