# Chapter 110 — A List of Doors

On Tuesday the fifteenth of the second month Hale opened at ten with the rate card fresh and the trays full, and Orrin was there to see what he had been walking toward since the thirteenth of the first: a broker's book, and a list of streets that is a list of doors.

Hale's day book was on the counter, bound, ruled, a buyer's street in it beside every sale over two tokens, in his own hand, with the date. He did not hide it. A broker stands between a thing and a buyer and takes a cut, and in this city the cut is a percentage of minutes, and the arithmetic of the whole trade is checkable by anybody with a beam and the price list. The tray was out at the price, the paper was out with the price on it, and the book was out, and there was nothing in the pitch at all that Orrin could call concealed, which was the most uncomfortable thing about it.

"I don't hide it because I can't," Hale said, before Orrin had asked. "And I don't keep it because I want it. I keep it because in about six weeks somebody is going to sell me a token that came out of a stall in Weir Lane at four and three, and I am going to want to be able to say whose brass that was, and a man who cannot say whose brass it was is a man who gets robbed by Friday."

Orrin asked to see it and Hale showed it, because the book was not the undertaking. The twenty-six addresses in a kitchen had a four-line undertaking signed by four people on the fourteenth of the tenth, not to be printed, copied, added to any list, used to plan any journey, ever, no person to go to any door, the households not to be told. And the market's day book was also a list of addresses, and nobody had taken an undertaking about it, and that was the same question coming back round, and not a new one, and the difference between the two pieces of paper was not the paper.

"One line," Hale said, turning the book. "Street, door, letter, quantity, price, date. Six lines over four trading days, and four of the six are one token and two are three. I'll show you the six and you may write the streets down if you've a pencil, because a street is a street. The doors you can read and you may not write."

The last entry was in that morning's ink, which meant it was a sale made on a Tuesday by a man who came early. It gave the street and the door and the district letter, Q, and the price, four shillings and sixpence, and a note in Hale's hand that was not part of the six lines and said *spent, same day, by the purchaser, on his own clock*.

"It went in this morning," Hale said. "Twelve days after I asked the council whether I might ask you, five days after you told me no, on a Tuesday, which is the day this place trades. He did not have to wait for me to open. He came at eight, when I was putting the trays out."

"You know it went into a clock."

"I know he told me he had been thinking about it since the sixteenth of the first month, a month ago, and had been to a chandler about a man to mend one." Hale closed the book. "I did not ask him what clock. If I ask him what clock then I have a second column, and if I have a second column then I am a man with a list of clocks instead of a man with a list of brass, and there is no door in the law between those two men and there is a very wide gap."

"A refusal binds the person who asked and nobody else," Orrin said.

"Your council refused me. I have not asked the council anything since. The row may refuse the council and the council may not ask the row. I am neither. Five days later brass is in a clock on that row. Nothing in your right anticipates that and nothing in it is contradicted by it." He said it without any apology in it at all. "I read your minute. All of it. It cost me a walk to the Weigh Lane end and seven days of a body's time, and I would like it on the record that I would not have spent the seven days if the answer had been yes."

Orrin stood there. "Why not?"

"Because a yes would have been the council asking the row through me, and then in three months there would be a form about it, and in three years there would be a column for the form, and a man in a pitch would be writing down what a committee wanted to know about a district's clocks instead of what a district's clocks are." He squared the tray. "You have spent a right to stop that, and it has cost you the thing you wanted and it has cost me seven days, and both of us would do it again, and the only person who has lost is the man who now has to buy his time in a market instead of getting it from a committee. And there are about two hundred and forty of him."

Orrin closed the book. "Whose minutes?"

"The clock's," Hale said, and then, before Orrin could ask where he had heard it: "That is the officer's sentence at the guild hall, and it is the only one anybody has ever given me about brass, and I have never once said it in this pitch, because saying it would be saying a thing I cannot make sound like mine. What I said to you on the seventeenth is what I have said to everybody in here for eleven weeks. A hundred minutes put into a clock in this Quarter comes out of this Quarter's margin and goes into the network, and the network gives it to the next clock behind, and there is no instrument in this city that can tell you which one. And you did that sum in your head then and did not say it out loud, and I have watched four men do it in a pitch and keep it in their head since."

"And the doors?"

"Are doors," Hale said. "A man of twenty says a list of addresses is a list of routes, and he is right, and a day book with a buyer's street in it is a list of doors, and I keep mine because a broker who does not know where his brass goes is a man who will be robbed by Friday. Nobody has taken an undertaking about it. If anybody wants one, let them bring four lines and four people and a date, and I will read it, and if the four lines are honest I will sign under them, and there is not one person in this row who would be surprised if they did."

He put his hand flat on the book for a moment, which was not sentiment. It was a man showing a thing he had decided was the correct thing to have.

"Whatever anybody decides about my book," he said, "the street is written in a book on a Tuesday morning, in ink, in my hand, in a city where a district cannot find out its own water. Whatever they decide, the row is now a page. It was not a page on the tenth of the second month. It is one now. And I did not do that, and I cannot be asked to stop it, and the only thing I can be asked is whether I would write it differently."

Orrin walked to Sluice Row in the afternoon and stood at the top of the street without knocking, because the twenty-six households were not to be told and the twenty-two were not the twenty-six and that had been said in December rather than assumed, and knocking now would have been a second asking without seven days.

It took him about four minutes to stand there and it was the longest four minutes of the week. There was a door number on a page in a book off Weigh Lane, and the page was under him, and Hale knew it too, because a broker who does not know where his brass goes gets robbed by Friday, and that was the only other man in the city who had both of those facts in his head, and he had not come up the row either.

He had the address. He could have been at the door in ninety seconds. A clock in a passage in a house on that row was a mantel clock and not a Quarter clock, and the brass had already gone in, and a woman who had had a hundred minutes bought for her without being asked would not be glad to be asked about it now by a man with a tool roll and a reason.

So he stood, and he counted the clocks from the pavement, which is a thing a clockmaker can do at forty feet. Eleven public faces: one on the gable, two over doorways, the rest in passages where the panes were frosted and the hands were a shape. Two standpipes, one of them dripping. The brick cistern under the street drawn down and refilled in December, five hours and three hundred minutes of it, hand-wound throughout, and the row book on the ledge in the pumping ward with the timetable in it.

A curtain moved in one house. A boy came round the side of another in a coat, with the collar up, and looked at him, and Orrin looked back, and the boy went in. Nobody asked him anything, and nobody thanked him for not asking, and a man standing at the top of a street in the afternoon with a tool roll and nowhere he was going is a thing this row had four hundred working people in it who are used to seeing.

He went on to the pumping ward and read the row book's timetable off the ledge where it lay, which is the only time in eleven years he has read it in that room, and he did not take it off the ledge and he did not copy it. The case stood on the wall to the left of the door with the glass on it and the empty column in it where a name ought to be, and he looked at that for as long as it took to understand that looking at it was the entire extent of what he had come there to do.

He went back to the bench and wrote the book in the grey ledger, doors not copied, streets named only as streets, because a number said in a room goes where the number goes and in December it had gone to the people the number was about, which was the one time anybody had wanted it to.

*Hale's day book seen 15th second, No. 14. Q100 into Sluice Row clock 5 days after the refusal, 12 after the asking, by the householder. Refusal binds asker only. Doors not copied. No undertaking taken. The same question twice, not a new one.*

On Wednesday the sixteenth and Thursday the seventeenth he worked ordinary bench work, beat two watches to the Quarter's public clock, the most accurate in the city, and wrote the rates on cards. The tank that week was about fourteen hours, exactly balanced, alarm inhibited since the second of the third month, no report made and no form requiring one, a level written on paper in a coat every afternoon by a man at the gate who has never been asked for it and has never offered it, and Mara knowing the figure on a printed four-line card, received and noted, not admissible. He did not go to the tank and did not ask for the sheet, because there was no form on which to ask and he was not going to manufacture one at the end of a fortnight in which he had learned that a manufactured form is a thing that outlives the man who made it.

He did write it in the grey ledger, though, on the Wednesday, the sixteenth, one line, with the date on it, in pencil, in a book nobody would ever be shown: *Tank, about fourteen hours, balanced, alarm inhibited, no form, no report. I have not been asked and I have not asked.*

On Friday the eighteenth he swept the step, locked up at noon, and sat at the bench with the grey ledger open at the week. The shop smelled of oil and cold iron. Outside, Verge Lane was quiet in the way a Quarter street is quiet on a Friday afternoon, with the shutters half down and the public clock showing the hour to nobody in particular.

He turned the pages back to the thirteenth of the first month and read forward, because five weeks of a man's own work is a thing he can check the way nobody else can check it. A printer's clock and brass sent back loose. An arcade on a Tuesday, and a board with four figures on it in chalk. A drawer with nine punches and a blank space. A beam on Weigh Lane that weighed free and had been asked nine times in nineteen years. A kitchen nine feet by eleven, and a brass disc worn bright on one edge from nine days in a pocket. A nurses' kitchen with four women in it and a name on a card that had not been taken off. A letter with a date on it, answered in a council's own words and published. A ward's forty minutes. A weight of one thousand eight hundred and sixty grains. And a day book on a counter with a buyer's street in it.

Ten brass tokens bought and paid for in that pitch in the four trading days since Hale wrote to the council, and not one of the doors written in Hale's book in anything but a broker's own hand, and not one of the persons who carried a token home named on any page that Orrin was permitted to copy, and a line of his own at the foot of it: *Doors not copied.*

The letter from the Cathedral was still face down on the shelf. He turned it over, looked at the direction in her hand, and put it back face down. Tuesday would come round again, and the north door would be open or not, and he had not decided, and not deciding was also a thing a man did with his hands.

He wrote the last line of the week under the date, in ink.

*Hale's book seen. Refusal holds to the man. Brass in row clock five days after. Doors not copied.*

He put the pencil down, left the letter where it was, and went up to the room above the bathhouse to sleep.
