# Chapter 119 — What Holds Without a Forecast

On Monday the twentieth of the third month Orrin Vale had no licence, and there were three things in the Weirs that had to be done that week, and none of them had been asked of him by anybody, and he found them himself, and the finding out that they did not need asking was the whole of what the week established and it was not nothing.

The first was a valve.

The boundary sluice at the Northglass works' gate is a 1911 clock-driven valve. Four feet of reach, a pointer and a plate, about eleven minutes from a cold start and about nine once it is warm, with a square-headed override on the side of it that a man can put his shoulder to. It is on the works' side of a boundary between a district that has no standing under the water act and a works that is not able to supply that district across it, and it had not been moved since the winter, and there had been a week of hand work on the canal front in the last month of the second, where somebody had wanted it moved and there was no form on which the somebody could ask for it to be moved, and the result was a week of hand work at a gate by about five men, which is a thing that happens in this city and is not written down.

He went out on the Monday with Jonas Bexley, thirty-one, of the district works', who had opened that gearbox in the cold four times in nineteen years and who said so before anybody asked him, and the square-headed override was cold and it took eleven minutes and four seconds between the two of them, and the pointer went over the plate, and nobody was hurt and nothing was certified and a line went in a log book in Bexley's hand, which is *J.B.*, with *O.V.* after it, because a log book wants two names on a line where two men are on a gearbox.

On the Tuesday the twenty-first he sat in a tram at the front and rode the Green Span and Weir Lane service from end to end with a notebook, and the woman driving it was thirty-three and had been doing it for twenty-one years and her name was Nita Bramhall and she did not know he was on it twice.

The tram signal at the Green Span end of that line carries a certificate of condition dated the third of the seventh, and the inspector's column on it says one hour, twelve minutes and forty seconds, and that figure is true, and it is not the certification column, and a great many people in this city have confused the two and got a great many things wrong, including, on the evidence of a wall, about six councillors.

Nita Bramhall did not run the service off the signal and she did not run it off a timetable she trusted either, and she said so before he asked, because she had been asked this before by two men from the works and had told them the same thing and had been right both times.

"The timetable is the contract," she said. "The signal is somebody's arithmetic, and the arithmetic on the certificate is an inspector's column and not the column anybody is supposed to be frightened of, and I could not tell you this morning whether the two of us are talking about the same hour or about the same minute, and I have not asked, because the answer does not change what I do at a stop." She put a hand on her coat pocket. "The one I have is my father's and it was stopped in 1948 and my mother had it mended and it loses about a minute a day and I put it right against the Quarter at the depot twice a week like everybody else, and it is a watch and it is in my pocket, and if it stops I have a spare in the other pocket because I have had this for twenty-one years."

"And the service runs on yours."

"The service runs on mine and the stops run on the timetable and the two of them are four minutes apart on a bad day," said Nita Bramhall, "and there is a certificate on the signal that says the signal is a minute and a half out, and I have never been able to work out whether that is the same four minutes and I have never asked, because if I ask and the answer is yes then the certificate is a lie about the timetable, and if the answer is no then the signal is lying about the timetable, and either way the driver's watch is the only one of the three I can be held to, and I have been held to it twice for two stops in eleven years and both times I was right and one of those times I was four minutes and the other I was two, and neither of them went in a book that anybody has ever read."

Later the same day she held the tram twice.

The first was at the Weir Lane stop at about four in the afternoon, a Tuesday-service stop with about nine people on it, and her watch said the stop was a minute and a half ahead of the timetable's idea of it, and she waited one and a half minutes at the stop with the door shut and nine people standing in a cold street, and the conductor came and said nothing because the conductor had been on that line for nineteen years and had watched her do it before.

The second was at Green Span at about five, and there she did not wait, and Orrin got off at the second stop and walked back to the hospital to find out why.

Because at Green Span the timetable and her watch had come back together, and the tram was four minutes early, and a hospital stop four minutes early is not a nicety, it is eleven people getting a lift they did not ask for and a driver who is now going to be four minutes late back, and she held it, and the reason she held it was that her watch said she was early and her watch was the one she could be held to.

"What does the timetable say?" he asked.

"The timetable says what the timetable says," said Nita Bramhall, "and the timetable was printed in 1941 and the street it counts to has had a new bus shelter and a new surface and about forty more people on it since, and the timetable is not wrong about anything it says, it is just not saying anything about this stop any more."

"You could be wrong."

"I have been wrong about a stop twice in twenty-one years," she said, "and both times the ward rang the works and both times it was me, and I would rather be wrong at a hospital than right at a factory, and that is not a rule and it is not in any book and it is the whole of what I have got."

She wrote both of the times on the back of a spoiled ticket roll in the cab in pencil, with the date and the stop and the number of minutes, and the roll went into her coat, and the coat went home, and there is no form in this city on which anybody may ask a conductor for a ticket roll.

He said thank you and she stopped writing.

"No," said Nita Bramhall. "You do not thank a driver for driving. You have nine people standing at a stop in the cold because she is holding it and if it goes in a book somewhere that a hold is a thing people are glad about then in a year I will hold one and think about the book instead of about the nine, and that is the day somebody gets hurt at a stop for the want of a quarter of a minute."

"You have taught me that twice today," Orrin said.

"You have been on the front twice," said Nita Bramhall, "and that is the only difference and I am not going to be thanked for it."

He was at the boundary gate on the Wednesday afternoon at about half past three, the day after the two holds, and the works' man had gone home, and the gearbox was warm, and the override was standing out at a right angle to the frame, and a man alone in a concrete box with a 1911 clock-driven valve in it is a man with a machine in front of him that nobody has asked him anything about, and it was, as far as he could tell standing there, the first time in his life that those two facts had been both true and both available at once.

The telephone was in a hut eleven yards away with a door that did not shut, and it rang at about half past four, and it was the ward.

Leda said one word.

"Do not."

"I was not going to."

"You were standing in a box," said Leda, "and I can hear you not having put your hand on it, and I want to tell you why I rang, and I have got about ninety seconds because I am on a corridor. I have eleven patients and two watches and the reason those two watches work is that there is no third instrument in the room. That is the whole method. It is not two watches instead of a machine. It is two watches *and nothing else in the room that is holding anything together*. If you put your hand on that gearbox this afternoon then the next time somebody needs a method they will hear that a man once did it alone at a gate and it worked, and it will not have worked, and it will have worked once, and once is a number that people remember."

"How did you know I was at a gate?"

"You did not tell me," said Leda Vale. "I have a ward with a line clock on the landing that is nearly four hours out and I have been reading that number every day for a month and I have had a fifth of a free minute of a second-hand regulator in my hands since Tuesday, and I have been reading *Valve, gate, override* underneath it, and I rang the works and a man at a counter told me which gate."

She put the phone down.

He stood in the box for about four minutes longer and then put the override back to neutral and locked the hut and signed nothing, because there is no column for *I did not do it*, and that is a thing the third month found out.

On the night of Wednesday the twenty-second into Thursday the twenty-third the ward was on two watches for the third time, and the second ward, which had come into it on the Monday evening after the fourth, was on it again, and the difference between the two was written down at every handover, and the total for that night across eleven patients was six hours and fifty-eight minutes, and the worst single interval was fourteen minutes and five seconds, and Cobb wrote it and the fourth line was hers and there was a column headed with the fourth of the third and the eleventh of the third and it was going to end in eight days whichever way the week went.

So: three things held, and none of them was asked for.

And the three records do not agree, and the reason they do not agree is that each one is a record of a different thing, and there is no column in any of them for the thing they share.

The gate's is a line in a log book in a works' hand and it says *override, cold, eleven minutes four seconds, J.B., O.V.* and it does not say what was behind the valve or whose water it was and could not, because the man who wrote it was not there to see it.

The tram's is a ticket roll in a coat in a cupboard in Bellweather Road with two lines in pencil and the dates, and it does not say how many people were standing at the Weir Lane stop, which was nine, and it does not say that the certificate on the signal says something different, which it does.

The ward's is a card on a board with the date, four names, a total, a worst figure, and a column with two watches' difference in it three times, and it does not say that the same free master has fouled twice since September, because the free master is on the other card and nobody joins the two cards.

Eleven minutes and four seconds in a log, five and a half minutes on a ticket roll, and a difference of eleven seconds at three in the morning, in three buildings, in one ordinary week, about three pieces of the same city, and not one of the three documents could be laid beside another without a person who knew all three, and there is one such person in Morrowfen and she is a ward sister of about fifty who read two hundred and twenty-five hours off a board in chalk in the third month and went back to work, and she was not asked and was not thanked and said so out loud to nobody in particular at about four o'clock on the Thursday, and the sentence was, "Nobody is thanked and I have stopped waiting, and I would like it on the record that the day somebody thanks me for a thing I have been doing unpaid for four years is the day somebody thinks I can be asked to do a fifth."

The best interval on that ward all night was under a minute and the worst was fourteen minutes and five seconds, and the ward's whole slack on the best night of the year is forty minutes, and a hundred minutes is two and a half times that, and the card does not say what the difference between the two is called, and the hospital branch is three hours and fifty-six minutes out and stepping fifty seconds a night, and a printed card with four streets on it has been read by about two hundred and forty households in four streets and there are ten brass in this city in a pocket or on a table or in a drawer, and not one of the three things that held that week would have failed if none of that were true, and none of them would have held if a man had put his hand on a gearbox at half past three on the Wednesday afternoon, and nobody knew that except the four people who had each independently written it down in a place nobody reads.

The ward sister of about fifty did the sum out loud on the Thursday afternoon, in the corridor, to nobody, and the sum was not a sum about hours and she said so before she started it.

A hold at Weir Lane is a hot afternoon in a bad coat, for one person and for nine others for eleven minutes. A cold override is a man of thirty-one with his shoulder against a square head for eleven minutes and four seconds, and a man of thirty-two beside him, and hands that have done it before and will do it again. Two watches is a pair of wrists for eight hours and a card written in pencil at ten to three in the morning, and a fourth line with a name on it.

That is the whole of what the day cost. One shoulder, one afternoon, one pair of wrists, and nothing that will be still wrong in September, and there is no form in this city on which a person may be given permanent hour-sickness for a Tuesday.

Which is the finding, and nobody in that corridor wanted it, and it is the true one. The day held because nothing was asked of any machine. Nothing was asked of any machine, so there was nothing for anybody to pay and nothing for anybody to learn, and a day that costs a shoulder is a day that can be done on a Tuesday by anybody at all. The day it cannot be done is the day somebody puts a question to a machine in a room, and that is not a moral observation, it is a bill, and it arrives in a different currency and it does not come back.

Orrin wrote the whole of it out in the grey ledger that night and left the last line without a conclusion under it, and the narrow book on the shelf behind the bench has a blank line under this one and has had since the first of the second month, and a blank line is not a conclusion and is not a failure and is the only honest thing a book that size has ever held.
