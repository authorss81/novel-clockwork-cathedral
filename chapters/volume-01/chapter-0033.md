# Chapter 33 — A Diversion, With a Schedule

Bevin Sarr took the notice off the ward board outside the Weir Two pump house on the Tuesday morning of the fourth of the fourth month, and folded it in four, because the pins are at the corners and the type is small and eleven hundred people live on that front and about ninety of them can read standing up in the rain.

She read it standing on the step with the pump house behind her and the water going out below.

> *PUBLIC NOTICE — PROPOSED SCHEDULE, LOWER WEIRS CANAL FRONT.*
> *Notice is given that the Water Users' Committee of the Lower Weirs pumping district will hear a proposed night-draw schedule for the canal front at its sitting on Monday the seventeenth inst. at ten of the clock, in the room over the chandler's shop in Weir Lane. The text of the proposed schedule may be read at the hearing. Any person may be heard who gives notice to the clerk. The committee is not able to consult the district, the district having no standing under the water act.*

That last sentence was not required by the ordinance and every other notice on that board for eleven years had ended at *any person may be heard*. It had been put there by a clerk who had read a good deal of correspondence that week and who was doing his best, in the way a man does his best inside a form that has one line for it.

Bevin Sarr read the whole thing twice and then said, out loud, to nobody, in the way of a woman who has been in a district for twenty years and has just been told something new about it in writing.

"They've put it in the notice."

---

The chandler's clock on Bellweather Road was a hundred seconds slow on the Wednesday morning of the fifth, and Orrin had known that since Saturday, and had left it alone since Saturday, and came back to it on the Wednesday because the man in the shop had left a note on the counter saying it had not been looked at.

He had the case open on the bench and the movement out and the hands off before he had decided, and then he stopped, and put his hand flat on the bench, and had the argument with himself for about ninety seconds, standing up, in an empty shop on a Wednesday morning.

There is no fault in that clock. The bracket is sound and the movement is sound and the rate is the rate it was made to and the movement is a hundred and forty seconds from the district reference because the line is a hundred seconds from the reference and the bracket takes forty of it back. If he wound it a hundred and forty seconds forward with a bar, it would be right. It would be right for about eleven weeks, which was how long the last one had lasted before the same thing happened again, and a hundred and forty seconds of a bar is eleven minutes' work, and there is no receipt and no form and no way for anybody in the Weirs to prove that a man came into a shop and moved a clock with a bar, and no district can be invoiced for it.

The count was two. Eleven minutes on a lane in the Weirs in about an hour, in the month of the second, and eight on a bracket clock at the Ferrywright corner in about eleven minutes. Two, both his, both in the hall's own form under the W-4's eighth line because there was no column anywhere else.

The count is two and it stays two.

He put the hands back where they were and shut the case and wrote the condition record.

> *Street clock, Bellweather Road. Within tolerance against the district reference. Error one minute forty seconds slow, cause not established. No fault. No work performed. No recommendation.*

Then he wrote, underneath, in the same book and in his own hand, which he was not going to do for a customer and did it anyway:

> *Not corrected. I could have done it in about eleven minutes with a bar. There is no fault in it, and a correction wants a fault, and I have found out this week that there is no way whatever to wind sixteen days out of a district's clocks unless somebody writes down what happened, and there is no form for what happened. So the clock is one minute forty seconds slow and the man who owns it has been told, in these words, and he will go to a shop and ask a man with a column, and the man with a column will ask him what the fault is.*

The man read it standing at the counter, and then he read the second paragraph, and then he said the thing that a man says.

"You've told me it's not your fault and you haven't fixed it."

"I have told you it is not a fault, which is not the same, and I have not fixed it, and I have charged you four shillings for looking at it, and if you want the other thing it is four pounds and eleven and there is a certificate and the certificate will say within tolerance, which is a lie you will be able to prove by looking at the clock."

The man thought about it for a while and then said, "No," and went to work, and the shop book has the entry and that is the whole of the transaction, and the count is two.

---

The clerk of the Water Users' Committee was a woman of about thirty-five called Nadia Erridge and she sent the notice of the appendix down to the chandler's shop on the Tuesday afternoon by a boy on a bicycle, and Bessie Crane set it in the Advertiser's type on Wednesday evening at the rate of fourpence a page, and it went up on the table at the sitting on the sixth in a bundle tied with string, and on the Friday morning of the seventh a copy of the whole bundle came back through the post, and there were nine pages in it and the ninth was a single sheet of a different paper with a different type on it, and it was headed *DIVERSION NOTE, LOWER WEIRS PUMPING WARD, THIRD MONTH*, and Wenna Pruitt telephoned Vale's Bench at twenty past eleven on the Friday to say that there was a page in it that had not been in it, and that a copy was going round to Weir Lane at noon, and that Mr Vale was welcome to come and read it but was not to bring anybody.

---

It is four paragraphs long and it is the plainest thing in this book.

> *District works accountancy. Reconciliation of the certified monthly return of the Lower Weirs pumping ward against the ward's own return for the same month, the ward's own return having been received in the appendix to the minutes of the Water Users' Committee of the fourteenth inst. and not previously before the district works.*
>
> *The certified return for the third month states that the ward's reserve was drawn in accordance with the standing rotation and that no variation was made. The ward's own return for the same month states a variation from the second inst. continued to the date of this note.*
>
> *The variation is: from the ward's reserve, to the Carrow Street main, nine thousand gallons a day, being three hundred and twenty-four thousand gallons in the thirty-six days from the second inst. to the date of this note. The Carrow Street main supplies seventy-one households and a galvanising and wire works. The diversion is required by the main, which draws on the ward's reserve and not on a separate main, and the works' certificate of the twenty-ninth of the second month states that the main is not to be shut.*
>
> *The diversion commenced on the second of the third month and is in force. It is reviewed at the next monthly return and thereafter at the discretion of the district works. Should the rotation of the district main be varied in any respect, the diverted volume shall be restored to the ward's reserve within one month of the variation.*
>
> *The condition of the ward's certified reserve at the first of the month was eleven days. Its condition at the date of this note is under two days, and the reserve alarm for the ward has been inhibited since the second of the third month, the inhibition being entered against this note. Under the works' tables an inhibited reserve alarm is a condition requiring a report, and no report has been made.*

He read it twice at the table in the back room over the chandler's shop and then a third time, standing up, because the first two times he had been reading it as a document and the third time he read it as a bill.

"It is correct," he said.

"I have not asked you," said Wenna Pruitt.

"It is correct in every line, including the line about the alarm, and the man who wrote it does not know the first of it from the last of it, and he has written it in the past tense in four places, and he has signed it and initialled the margin, and he is owed nothing by anybody and has been paid by somebody for it, and it is the first time in the whole of this business that anybody has written down what the pumping ward did in March, and I am including you, because you did not write it down either."

"I did not write it down," said Sel Kade, from the doorway.

She had come down the slipway in her coat and had not come in, and she was standing with her hand on the door frame the way she stood at windows, and she had not heard the document read twice and had not asked to hear it once.

"Will you come in?"

"I will read it standing where I am, because if I sit down in that room with a piece of paper about March in it, I am a person being examined, and I have not come to be examined, and you did not invite me to be examined, and you have never once invited anybody to be anything."

"Nobody is examining you."

"Somebody is." She came in and did not sit down. "Give me the page."

Wenna Pruitt gave her the page. Sel Kade read it standing, and she read it the way a hydraulic engineer reads a drawing — fast, in the middle, and then from the beginning — and she got to the second paragraph on the second time round and stopped for about four seconds, and then she finished it, and gave it back.

"Nine thousand a day," she said. "The figure is right. I have got a copy of the ward's own return in my own office and it is the same figure to the gallon and I have checked it every week since the second of the third month, and I have never had to check the same week twice, because I have not let anything else near it." She put her hand flat on the table, which she had got from Ivy Pike. "Nobody has ever had to check that figure in writing before. That is what is new. Not the water. The figure."

"You are not going to argue with the alarm line," said Wenna Pruitt.

"I did not put the diversion in to silence an alarm. I put it in because a works' certificate from a man who has never once lied to me said the main was not to be shut, and because that works has ninety-one people on its pay book and sixty-one of them live in this district, and because in the second week of March there was standing water at the Carrow Street end up to the second step of about forty houses, and I have the water board's own gauge sheet for it, and it is in my office, and it is the only document in this entire business that anybody in this district has ever produced about a flood, and it is a gauge sheet, because a district that has floods is a district with no form on which to say so."

"Then say the rest."

"The rest is that the alarm is right and the reserve is under two days and I have known that since the eleventh of March, and I did not report it, and the reason I did not report it is that a report is a thing that goes to the district works, and the district works would have come and looked at the reserve, and looking at the reserve means asking why it is empty, and it is empty because of a line on page four of that paper, and I would rather be asked about the line than about the reserve, and I have never had anybody to notice that trade until just now, and I have been making it for a month." She was still standing. "So put it in. I am not asking anybody to soften a word of it."

"I was going to ask Bessie to set the appendix again without the beneficiary's name on the fourth paragraph," said Wenna Pruitt, "because that is the only part of it that can be argued with, and nobody can argue with a line of a figure."

"I know you were. I would have said no." She looked at the ceiling for a second, the way a person does when they are looking for a room in a house they grew up in. "Mrs Pruitt, I have been a rumour with my own name on it for a month. A rumour can be beaten by anybody who turns up with a document, and about nine people have tried it, and I have not been able to answer any of them, because every one of them wanted something and I would not give it to them. That is the whole of what has happened to me since the eighth of the third month. And if you take the beneficiary's name off the fourth paragraph then in four years' time somebody comes with the same nine thousand gallons a day and no name on it, and I am a warden who moved water for a reason nobody will ever be able to hear. I would rather be beaten by a document with the works on it."

"Then it goes in as it is."

"It goes in as it is, and I will not have the review clause taken out either, and I know that is the sentence you would have struck if I had let you, so I will say the rest of it now and be finished." She put both hands on the back of a chair and not on the table. "The clause is the only part of that document I can use. Not the start, not the volume, not the main, not the works. The clause. It says the diverted volume shall be restored within one month of a variation of the rotation. The rotation was varied on the eighth of the third month and it was varied again on the thirtieth, when the schedule ran its term and the standing rotation came back. One month from the thirtieth is the thirtieth of this month. I shall do it before the twentieth. And the review is the same day, and after the review it is at the discretion of the district works, and discretion has no end to it, and I have been a warden for twenty-two years and I know exactly what discretion is: it is a thing with no date on it that a man is not obliged to use. And nobody will restore it in any case, because restoring it is a work on the ward's main, and a work on the ward's main is a work on a schedule, and the committee that makes the schedule has nine members and a property qualification and a hearing on the seventeenth, and there is nothing in that clause that anybody can serve."

"It is a discretionary review as well."

"It is a discretionary review as well, and that is the half of the clause that will do nothing, and I have known that since Saturday." She took her hands off the chair. "I am not asking anybody to be angry. I am telling you what is in a piece of paper, because in about a month I am going to be asked about it in a room and I would rather be asked about the clause than about the water, and there is one of those two things I can answer for."

---

Orrin did not sleep much on the Friday night, and it was not the ear and it was not the hand.

He got the ward's returns out of the bundle at about midnight in his own shop, with a lamp and the gauge out of the case on the bench because it made him feel that the bench was doing something, and he did the arithmetic twice, and the second time he did it out loud in an empty shop, and it came out the same both times.

*A ward's water rates are set on its certified return. That is in the water act and it is not a custom and nobody chose it. The ward's certified return for the third month said that the ward drew its reserve in accordance with the standing rotation and made no variation. The ward actually made a variation from the second of that month onward and gave away three hundred and twenty-four thousand gallons in thirty-six days.*

*Three hundred and twenty-four thousand gallons of water went out of the poorest ward in this city to a main with a works on it, and the ward charged nobody for it, because the ward's rates are set on the return, and the return said no.*

*So the ward's rates have been set for eleven years on a number that was wrong by every gallon the district ever gave away, and the district has been under-charged for its own water for as long as there has been a night crew to under-charge it for, and nobody in the Weirs has ever been able to prove that, because a district that has proved it has to produce the returns, and its returns were printed on the twentieth of the third month on a line in the estimates at four pounds ten out of a Green Span ward's clerical allowance.*

*And now a clerk in the district works' accountancy has written the wrong number down. And a ward's rates are set on its certified return, and the certified return is going to be corrected, because that is what happens when a reconciliation note goes into a file — not immediately, not this month, but the next time the ward is assessed, which is in the spring, on a cycle nobody argues about.*

*Which means that the district is going to be re-rated. Not because it has done anything wrong. Because it has proved what it did.*

He stopped there and sat with it, and then he wrote the line that made it a book entry and not a complaint.

> *So the last four pounds ten of the tenant council bought a document that is going to cost the Lower Weirs pumping ward more money than the document cost, and Mrs Pruitt knew the shape of it and not the size of it, and she paid it anyway, and she told a woman in a room before she paid it, which is more than I have ever done.*
>
> *And it is not a punishment and it is not an injustice and nobody in any office has behaved badly. There is a return. There is a number. The number was wrong. When the number is right the rates go up, because the rates are set on the number, and that is the correct use of a well-run system, and I have now watched it happen three times — to a bridge, to a category, and to a ward's water bill — and every time it has been the correct use of a well-run system, and every time the thing that was correct was somebody else's afternoon.*

He looked at that for a while and did not like it, and he did not soften it, and he shut the book.

The right hand closed, mostly, and the little finger was still late, and the ear had its hum with the weather, and outside the rain was coming up the street in the way it did when the tide was making and the low ground was answering, and in about nine hundred yards a woman of forty-four was standing in a room over a shop writing out a list of things she would be asked about on the tenth, in order, on a sheet of paper, so that she would have them in front of her in the order they would come.

He had not known she did that. He had known her for two months and he had watched her refuse a room, refuse a form, and refuse to be examined in an office, and he had never once thought to ask whether she prepared.

The count of his own hand corrections is two. It was two before the morning and it was two after it. The clock on Bellweather Road was one minute and forty seconds slow and would be for about eleven weeks, and there was a man in a shop who had been told so in plain words, and there was no form in this city on which any of it could be written, and on the Saturday morning Orrin Vale went down to the sloughs with a case and a bar and did not correct it.
