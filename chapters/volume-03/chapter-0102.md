# Chapter 102 — The Arcade on a Tuesday

Sunday the fifteenth of the first month Orrin did not go to Pindar Row, because Pindar Row is shut on a Sunday, and the reason it is shut on a Sunday is that a market which runs on a Sunday has no day to close on, and a day to close on is the only thing a market has that a criminal market does not.

He went instead to the bench and cleaned the oil off the test stand and read the narrow book through from September to January, which took an hour and told him what he already knew: every order of operations since the forty-one seconds, in pencil, in his own hand, and not one of them needed except the ones he would have got wrong without it. The letter was still on the shelf. He turned it face down so the direction did not show.

In the late afternoon he walked up Verge Lane as far as the corner where the lane stops being shops and starts being Cathedral Rise, and stood there, and looked up at the north door from the wrong side of the street, and then walked back down. It was not a decision. It was four hundred paces each way in the cold, and at the end of it the letter was still face down on the shelf and he was still the same man, and the shop was still shut, and nothing had been asked of anybody.

On Monday the sixteenth Teo Arden came in at ten with snow on his shoulders and a copy of the row book's fifth page in his pocket, which he did not take out.

"You've had brass," Teo said, without sitting. He never sat in the shop if he could stand; sitting was being a customer.

"One token. Q, a hundred, in a printer's office clock. Sent it back loose."

"Four and six?"

"That's the arcade price. Boy said so."

"Whose say?"

"Man in the arcade. He didn't give the boy a name, which is either honest or careful, and I did not ask, because asking a boy for a name in an arcade is asking him to go back and look at a face."

Teo put his hands in his pockets. He was twenty and looked nineteen in winter and thirty in summer, and he had the better of every argument they had had for a year and knew it and did not use it in the shop.

"Six pitches trading them now," he said. "Tuesdays and Fridays. No. 14 has the brass sign. Man called Corvin Hale. One pitch, one book, a rate card of districts ordered by how far behind they are. He is not a plant and he is not the Directorate. He is a broker with one pitch and he is competent and he is not going to be redeemed. That is the whole of him and it is enough."

"You've been."

"I've walked it. I don't buy and I don't sell." He shifted his weight. "Twice I stood in it for forty minutes with my hands in my pockets being a man in a coat, and a woman of about fifty came up beside me and asked whether they were the good ones, and I said I did not know, and she said that is the first straight answer anybody's given me here. Then she bought four."

"Four?"

"Four. Twenty-two minutes a night for a year and a bit, on a clock in Bellweather Road, and she wanted it for her mother, and she had thought about it for a fortnight, and she said so out loud, in a glass arcade, in front of eleven people."

"Wenna says a printed page has no address and a broker's book has," Teo said. "She is on the maintenance schedule and in the row book and she is right about this the way she is right about everything with a door in it."

"A list of addresses is a list of routes," Orrin said, because Teo had said it once on a landing six months ago and had sat on it for a month then, and nobody had thanked him for it and nobody had asked him where he had got it either.

Teo nodded once. "And a broker's day book with a buyer's street in it is a list of doors."

"What would you do with one?"

"I'd keep it in my pocket and I'd not spend it and I'd not tell anybody I'd got it, and in about a year I'd find out whether I'd kept it for a reason or because I was afraid of not having it." He said it without any weight at all, as a man reading out the shape of a thing he had already been inside. "You'd have sent it back. That's the only answer anybody in this city has got, and it's been a fortnight of Tuesdays."

He went out into the snow with nothing else said, and nobody thanked anybody, which was the second such morning in a row and would not be the last.

On Tuesday the seventeenth Orrin walked to Pindar Row at eleven in the morning, in daylight, in weather, with the token's figures in his head and no token in his pocket.

The arcade was a covered row of forty-one pitches off the Weigh Lane end of the Quarter, glass roof, iron columns, the floor worn in the middle where four generations had walked to look at other people's work. Six of the pitches had small trays of brass on velvet. The rest sold watch glasses, chains, tools, second-hand movements. The crowd was ordinary for a Tuesday: clerks, porters, two nurses off shift, a printer, a chandler's boy, women with shopping baskets who stopped to look because brass catches the eye.

No. 14 had the only brass sign in the row: HALE — MINUTES BOUGHT AND SOLD. Behind the counter a man of forty-one, broad, clean hands, a rate card on a board where anybody could read it without asking.

Orrin read it without asking. It was the City's own published daily numbers, arranged to answer one question: districts ordered by how far behind they were. Weir Two branch at the bottom, Green Span hospital branch beside it, Quarter hill at the top with the smallest figure. No comment, no advice, no promise. The arithmetic was checkable by anybody with a calendar and a price list, which was why it worked.

He stood in front of the board for a while. Under the figures was a line in Hale's own hand giving the price and the date, and under that, in smaller writing, the method: one token, one district letter, one hundred minutes, the drift taken from the City's sheet and not from any instrument of his own. It took a man about four minutes to read the whole board and it told you more than a printed minute from a hall had told this district in a year.

"A hundred minutes is four shillings and sixpence," Hale said, without looking up from the tray he was laying. "That is the price today. It was the price yesterday and it will be the price tomorrow, and I write tomorrow's on this board before I open, and if you come back in a fortnight expecting a number out of me instead of a date, you want a different man and there is one."

"And a night of drift?" Orrin said.

Hale looked up then. "Fifty seconds. A hundred minutes is a hundred and twenty nights, which is four months. A night at my price is about half a penny. You are the clockmaker from Verge Lane. You sent one back loose."

"I sent one back in paper. It wasn't mine to spend."

"It was the printer's. That is the same as not yours, and it is not the same as yours, and the difference is about four and six." Hale laid nine tokens in a row on the velvet, heads up, Q and W and G letters showing, each with 100 on the head. "They are lawful to hold, cut, sell and buy. What they are for is not in any form in this city. The form that would say was made in about 1883 and has no column in it. The whole of what the law has ever said about a token is what it is made of. Twenty-two millimetres, three thick, raised rim, hole, district letter, figure, year and die on the rim. That is the law."

"And the purpose?"

"The purpose is a hole in a form," Hale said, pleasantly. "A clock on a line shares its rotation with the rest of the line. A hundred minutes put into a clock in this Quarter comes out of this Quarter's margin and goes into the network, and the network gives it to the next clock that is behind. The market works in one direction, out of the most synchronized district in the city into the districts that have none of its margin. The Quarter is not being robbed and the Weirs is not stealing, and nobody breaks a rule, and the loss lands where it has landed for nineteen years, which is nowhere with a name on it."

He said the last part the way a man reads out a delivery address, and then he went back to his tray, because he had said it in this pitch twice a day for eleven weeks and it had never once changed the price.

Orrin did the sum in his head before he could stop himself. A year of drift on one clock at fifty seconds a night is five hours and four minutes and ten seconds, which is just over three tokens. A district's clocks two to four hours out is between a token and half of one and two and a bit. He did not say any of it out loud, because two figures in one sentence would have been a total, and he had promised the grey ledger he would not make one.

"How many cut?" he said instead.

Hale smiled. "Ask the hall. The die drawer has nine punches in it and blank space. The last return cut two hundred and sixty-one and took back two hundred and six and sold nine hundred and forty, and five hundred and sixteen are unaccounted for. Those are the hall's own numbers and they are on a plate with the cut figures on it. I did not make them."

"Do you believe them?"

"No," Hale said. "I believe they were written down on a Tuesday by a man who had cut two hundred and sixty-one of them and did not want a conversation. Five hundred and sixteen unaccounted for is a hundred and sixteen pounds of brass at my own price, and there is a great deal of difference between a hundred and sixteen pounds in a drawer and a number on a plate. I do not know which of those two the hall has. Neither does anybody else. The plate is not the brass, Mr Vale. Brass is heavy and dull and it does not need a plate."

Orrin wrote nothing down. He had no book with him and would not have used it in a man's pitch.

"Will you assay?" he said.

"There is a public counter on this lane that will weigh one free in about four minutes on standard weights to half a grain, and the keeper has weighed nine in nineteen years and every one was somebody bringing one back," Hale said. "The instrument that can tell a real token from a false one exists, costs nothing, and is not used. That is not my doing either."

"Somebody should tell the false ones apart."

"Somebody should have made a column for it in 1883." Hale wrapped a token in paper and wrote the price on the outside, and put it in a woman's hand. "That is not a thing a broker can do with a rule. It is a thing a hall does with a drawer."

The crowd moved round them, ordinary Tuesday business, money changing hands for glasses and springs. At the token pitches men bought and women bought and nobody hid it. Orrin watched a conductor buy two for nine shillings and put them in a purse with her fare money, and a night porter buy one and hold it to the light the way a man holds a coin he does not trust and wants to trust.

He watched a woman of about thirty with a laundress's basket look at the six trays for a long time and go out of the far end without buying, and he knew exactly which four shillings and sixpence she had and exactly which hundred minutes she wanted, and there was no one in that arcade whose job it was to stand between her and the four and six.

He left without buying and without selling, and Hale did not press him, and nobody thanked anybody, and the arcade bell rang twelve.

On Wednesday the eighteenth he went back to the bench and wrote the day in the grey ledger with the date on it, and under it the supply as Hale had given it, because a figure without a date is the whole of what the Weirs had been, and this one had a date.

*Two hundred and sixty-one cut on the last return, two hundred and six taken back at the counter, nine hundred and forty sold, and five hundred and sixteen unaccounted for. Hall's own numbers, on a plate. Said Tuesday seventeenth in Pindar Row, No. 14, by C. Hale, 41.*

He read it back twice. It was not a total for the omission and it was not a percentage or a ratio. It was a count of brass, and brass could be weighed, and a thing that can be weighed is a thing that a person can carry to a counter on Weigh Lane and ask about for nothing, and that was the whole of the difference between a number in a drawer and a number on a beam.
