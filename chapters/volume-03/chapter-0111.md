# Chapter 111 — A Question Dressed as a Letter

On Saturday the nineteenth of the second month Orrin Vale read Anja Voss's letter for the fifth time and put it face down on the shelf, and then read it a sixth time with the shelf open and the stair going down behind him, and it was thirty-eight days old, and the Tuesday it named had been the seventeenth of the first month, and that Tuesday had come and been gone a month ago and he had not gone to it.

Four lines on the Cathedral's own paper, and a direction in her hand, and one sentence in the middle of it that he had underlined twice in pencil and had not yet told anybody about, which was that *a room is a form and a form is a way of asking without the rule, and this is a door, and I cannot make anybody walk through it and neither can anybody in this building.*

He had read that sentence perhaps forty times since the twelfth of the first month and he had written it out once, in the narrow book, in his own hand, and he had then not known what to do with the writing, and the writing was still there on the page with nothing under it.

He had drafted four answers. The first was a refusal, and it was two lines, and both of them were true, and neither of them was why he was refusing, and a refusal whose reason is not in it is a notice dressed as a refusal and he had learned that in a kitchen in Bellweather Road with a piece of paper torn out of a book.

The second was an acceptance, and it was one line, and it was the line he had signed under a number in a glass case in the first year, and he had read it back and understood that it was the answer of a man who had been asked nothing.

The third was eleven lines and it was a list of what he would want, and he had got as far as the fourth item before he read it back and found that he had written *and a copy of the records for my own use*, and that a man who goes into a room and asks for a copy of the records for his own use has already decided what he is going to find.

The fourth was three questions and he had written it out twice and had not posted it, because three questions in a letter to the Director of the Cathedral is a form, and a form is a way of asking without the rule, and the sentence on the shelf says so in her own handwriting.

He stood at the bench for a while with that.

The shop smelled of oil and cold iron. Outside on Verge Lane the public clock showed the hour to nobody in particular, and the Quarter was doing what it does on a Saturday morning in February, which is nothing, and not quietly either. He had three weeks of electricity owing and four pounds two shillings of his own money in a form he had not filled in, and the postage of a letter was a penny, and the walk to the box at the end of the lane was a walk of six minutes each way, and the whole of what the morning was going to cost him was sevenpence and an hour.

He took the narrow book off the shelf and wrote the three questions out in it, in order, the way he wrote everything he was afraid of losing, and then he read them twice and did not improve them, because they were the questions and not the sentences, and a question that has been improved is a different question wearing the same clothes.

*1. If the north door is open and I go down, who may refuse me?*

*2. If nobody may refuse me, will you write on the door, in your own hand, with a date under it, that nobody may refuse me, so that a man who has gone down can be shown afterwards that there was nobody standing who could have stopped him.*

*3. If a person in that building may refuse me, what is that person's name, and will they be told that they may, before I go down and not after.*

He turned the questions over. The first was a plain question and it would get a plain answer. The second was the one he actually wanted, and he had put it second because a man who opens with the question he wants is a man who is asking a form to do something. The third was the one he had not known he wanted until it was on the page, and it was the only one of the three that was about anybody else.

He added a line at the top, and it took him longer than the three questions together, because it was the only sentence in the letter that was a decision:

*I am not accepting and I am not refusing. I am asking, because the second of these three is a question about a person who does not work in this building, and I would like it answered by the person it is about, and there is no address I have for her.*

Then a date at the top, his own name and trade at the foot, and a line saying that the copy of his four questions is in his own book with the date against it, which was a sentence he had learned in December and had never expected to use on anybody but himself.

He read the whole thing through once, folded it, put it in an envelope, wrote *A. Voss, Director, Cathedral of Consequence, Cathedral Rise* in the address because he did not know a better one and because the last four letters of a name are better than a wrong one, and sealed it, and did not sign it, and then signed it, because an unsigned letter to a Director of a public building is a thing a clerk can lose and he had made a clerk lose a thing in June and had heard the cost of it in somebody else's voice ever since.

He walked down to the box at the end of the lane. It was a Saturday and the post went at four. He put the letter in and heard it go down, which is a small sound, and stood there a moment longer than a man needs to stand at a box.

Sevenpence and an hour.

On Sunday the twentieth he went nowhere that had a counter behind it. He opened at nine, because a shop that opens on Sunday is a shop and a man who does not open is only telling himself something, and he did bench work with the door up and the blind half down, and he cleaned a carriage clock he had brought home on the Thursday and did not put it right, because the plate on the back wanted a piece he did not have, and a clock that is nearly right and knows it is nearly right is a clock somebody can use for a fortnight and somebody can be shown.

He said the day out loud before he wrote it down, because the last time he had a day wrong in his own head it had cost him forty-one seconds he had not known he had lost. The nineteenth was a Saturday. The twentieth was a Sunday. The twenty-first was a Monday. He said all three and then wrote them and the three agreed, and there was nothing in the shop but him and the sound of the street and a bench job that would take a week.

At about eleven he took the grey ledger off the shelf and did the arithmetic he did every few days and had never once been thanked for, and which nobody in the city would ever see. Since the seventeenth of the twelfth the branch had taken fifty seconds a night, and there had been sixty-three nights, which is fifty minutes and thirty seconds of drift, and the four clocks of the rehearsal were where the arithmetic said they were.

*Weir Two pump house, 2 h 41 min 20 s. Pumping ward outer room, 2 h 50 min 40 s. Quarter hill south, 3 h 42 min 30 s. Weir One gate, 4 h 22 min 40 s. Separations 9 min 20 s and 51 min 50 s, and they have not moved, and a step is a displacement and is permanent.*

He wrote that and then sat looking at it, because the two separations had not moved by a second in sixty-three nights, and the absolute figures had walked fifty-two and a half minutes in nine weeks, and he still did not entirely believe the second half of that sentence and would not have believed it in a room.

On the Monday the twenty-first he took a long-case in from Green Row that had been standing eleven weeks and beating a bad time, and it wanted a new pallet and half a day, and the man had brought it to the shop in a handcart and had asked, in the doorway, whether the man on Cathedral Rise was any good.

"He was," Orrin said.

"Was."

"He isn't any more," Orrin said. "I've not been up the steps in nineteen months. It's a public building and there is a woman in it who does not know my name and one who does and won't use it."

The man looked at him for slightly too long, the way people look at a tradesman who has said something about himself, and then he said, "Well," and asked what it would cost, and Orrin said four shillings and sixpence for the call and a shilling for the beat, five and six, and the man paid without argument, which was correct for a man who had come with a handcart.

At about four he did the thing he did about four times a year, which was to write down what he was not going to do. He was not going to accept the offer. He was not going to refuse it. He was going to post a question about it, and the posting was done, and the three questions were in the letter, and the copy was in the narrow book with the date against it, and there was no fourth question and he was not going to invent one at two o'clock on a Monday with a man of about fifty waiting to be told when his clock would be ready.

He also wrote, because it was a thing that happened about twice a year and he had never once been offered a third, that the count of his own hand corrections was two and had not moved since the storm year, and that nobody had put a third in front of him this month and that he would not have taken it.

Then he wrote the line he had written every week since the seventeenth of the twelfth, and had never once shown to anybody who had not already seen it.

*Answered 19th, by post, three questions, no acceptance and no refusal. A refusal is a fact and this is not a refusal. It is a question and it is going by post because a question is a thing I know how to send. Nobody thanked.*

On Tuesday the twenty-second the answer came.

It was four lines on the same paper, and it was dated the twenty-first, so it had come in a day, which he noted and did not think about. The hand was the same hand. The first line was a date and an hour: *Thursday the twenty-fourth, at ten in the morning, the north door. Come to the door and not to the steps.*

The second line was the answer to the first question, and it was the answer he had expected and had hoped would be longer: *Miss Alethea Cray, registrar, nineteen years in this building, may refuse you. I have told her so in writing and I have not asked her whether she is willing, because asking her whether she is willing would be a form.*

The third line was the answer to the third question: *Her name is Alethea Cray. She will be at the door at ten. She will have been told that she may refuse you and she will have been told nothing about what you asked me this week.*

The fourth line was the answer to the second, and it was a refusal, and it was the only one in the letter.

*I will not write on my door that nobody may refuse you. It would be true. In four years a person would read it as a permission, and I have spent four years learning the difference between those two, and you have not spent four, and the difference is a sentence you will learn inside the room.*

Orrin read the fourth line four times and then put the letter down on the bench with the palm of his hand flat on it, which was a thing his hands did, and which Leda had copied off him for eleven years and which he had never once been able to stop doing and had stopped noticing.

Then he took his hand off it and read it a fifth time, because the first four had been a man being pleased that he had been right, and the fifth was the reading, and the reading was that she had answered two of his three questions and had refused the third in one sentence, and that the sentence was better than the answer he had wanted, and that **a door is not a form and a form is not a door, and the reason one of his three questions had come back as a no was that it was the only one of the three that was asking a document to do a person's work.**

He wrote that down in the narrow book in the words he actually had, and then he did something he had not done since the June of the first year, which was to leave a blank line under it. A blank line in that book meant that he was not going to put a conclusion under a thing and had not yet earned one, and there were four of them in the book in a year, and two of them had been left for eighteen months and would probably be left for ever.

He had two days. He used about half of one.

He told two people where he was going and at what hour, and he wrote it out twice with the date, and he gave a copy to Bevin Sarr at the pumping ward and one to Wenna Pruitt at the council rooms, and the reason he did it was not that he was afraid and it was that a man who goes into a room and cannot afterwards say where he went and who was standing in it is a man whose account of it is worth nothing at all, and the only reason anybody had ever believed him about a glass case in the first year was that four other people had been in the room.

Bevin Sarr read the slip and said, "I'll put it in the ward book, because the ward book is where things go that somebody has to find."

"Do not read it out."

"I'm not going to read it out," she said, "and that is exactly the reason it should go in the ward book, and you have never once in your life worked out that those two facts go together."

She was right and he did not say so, and she was not thanked for it and did not look for it, and neither of them said the word.

On the Wednesday evening he laid out on the bench the things he was going to take and then took fewer than he had laid out, which is the ordinary discipline and is the only discipline he has. A lamp with a dry cell. The narrow book. A pencil. A rule. No tools, because there was nothing in a sealed room that a tool of his could improve, and no token, because there is no instrument in this city that can say what a token is for, and because a man who carries a hundred minutes into a room he has not been asked about has made himself the reason the room was opened.

He wrote that last sentence out twice on a scrap and then threw the scrap away, because a thing he had to write twice was a thing he was not yet sure of, and he had four of those in the narrow book and no room in it for a fifth.

Then he wrote the line for the twenty-second, with the arithmetic in it, and the count of hand corrections, and the tank, which was about fourteen hours and exactly balanced, alarm inhibited since the second of the third month, no report made, no form requiring one, and a man at a gate writing the level on a sheet of paper in his coat every afternoon whom he had still not asked and did not know the name of.

*22nd second. Answer received, four lines, dated the 21st. Thursday, ten, the north door. Two questions answered and one refused in one sentence, and the sentence is better than the answer I wanted. Going. Two people told where. Copy on the ledge. Nobody thanked.*
