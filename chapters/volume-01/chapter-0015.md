# Chapter 15 — Supper, and the Rule

The Green Span hospital staff kitchen was one room off the corridor behind the nurses' office, and it had a gas range at one end that was always too hot and a scrubbed table at the other, and it was the only warm room on that floor with a door that locked.

Leda Vale had it from four until five because the day staff came in at five and the two of them had an arrangement about it that had been running for two years, which was that Leda got the room and did not have to explain why.

He found her at the table with her shoes off.

That was the thing he had not been ready for. She was on her feet for nine hours of every working day and she had come from a ward where somebody's father had been dying slowly since Tuesday, and she had taken her shoes off under the table, and there were two plates out, and she had put the second plate down before he came in, which she had told him once was a professional judgement and not a sentiment.

"Sit down," she said. "You've eaten today."

"I've—"

"You've had tea. There's bread and there's the soup, and the soup is the same soup we had on Tuesday because I made too much on Tuesday, and if you tell me you can't eat at five in the morning I will put this spoon through your hand." She pushed the plate across. "Eat that and then tell me why you're stood there in your coat at ten to five in the morning when you could have written it down."

He sat down. He had walked to Green Span at half past three because he had tried to sleep and could not, and he had been outside the hospital for twenty minutes, and then gone round to the tram and back, and the whole of the twenty minutes had been an argument he had been having with himself about whether to knock.

He ate the soup. It was good. He had not eaten anything since a market pastry on Monday morning and had not noticed.

Leda watched him eat the first half of it, and then she took her feet off the chair opposite and turned round to face him and folded her arms, and said: "Right. You've come with something."

"I have."

"And you haven't got it out, and you did the thing on the Friday, the thing where you arrive in a room with a piece of information and then you talk about the machine for twenty minutes." She had her chin on her hand. "You've been doing it for a week and I have watched you do it and you did not think I could tell, and I want to say before we go any further that I can always tell, and that I have watched our father do exactly it, and that watching you do it is a thing I have not got the patience for this month."

"I know."

"Do you. Because there are two reasons a man does that, and one of them is that he is protecting the other person, and the other one is that he cannot yet say the sentence out loud, and both of them are the same as far as the other person is concerned, and both of them are your business and not mine." She took her hand off her chin. "So. Which."

"Both," said Orrin.

"Right," said Leda. "Well, that is the honest answer, and it is the first one you have given me since the seventeenth, so we are going to have the conversation in the correct order, which is that you are going to say the rule out loud first, and then you are going to tell me the thing, and then I am going to tell you what your rule is worth, and then you are going to eat the rest of that soup."

---

He said the rule out loud.

He said it standing up, at the scrubbed table, with his hands at his sides, in the flat procedural voice he used for reading a fault off a machine, because if he said it in any other voice it would be a speech.

"Nothing of mine is used again without its blind margin and its toll named in public, in a room, to somebody who can object."

Leda did not move while he said it. When he had finished she sat back and looked at the gas range for a while.

"That's good," she said. "That is genuinely a good rule and I have never heard a man say one better than it in my life, and I mean that as a nurse and not as your sister. Now sit down, because you want to be standing when you say the rest of it and you don't, and I have watched two men in this hospital do that and one of them was a patient and the other one was our father."

He sat down.

"Eleven days ago," she said, "you stood in a pump house and read out four things. What you knew, what you didn't know, which part was a guess, and what it would cost. And then nine men put their coats off. And I was in that room. Do you remember who objected?"

"Bevin Sarr. She wanted the number written on the shutter."

"Bevin Sarr, and the Reckoning woman, and the boy, and me, and that was four objectors in a room of nine, and every one of them objected in a way that made the thing better." She was not letting go of it, and she was doing it gently, which was how she did the difficult ones. "That rule is why that room worked. Do you understand that? Not because you were right. Because you stood in a room full of people whose hours those were, and let them argue with you, and they did, and what came out of it had a tape measure and two names on the back of a shutter. That is the whole of it. That is what a rule is for."

"Yes."

"So now tell me what it is worth when the person who needs it is a patient."

He said: "Leda—"

"No, that is the question, and you have to answer it, because you have written a rule that you intend to keep for the rest of your life and I am the only person alive who is going to make you say what it is for." She put her hands flat on the table, and the gas range ticked. "The rule says the toll gets named in a room, to somebody who can object. Fine. Beautiful. Now: the ward on the third floor has eleven patients and three staff and forty minutes of slack. The people whose forty minutes they are are asleep, and eight of the eleven cannot be asked, and the two that could be asked are on a trolley going to theatre. And the three staff are the ones who will do the extra, and they can object, and if they object, nothing happens — and that is your rule working exactly as written, and the two medicines still arrive four minutes apart. So the rule puts the people who can object in the room and the people who pay outside it. And outside it is where everybody who cannot fight for themselves is, and they are outside it every single time, and it is not a coincidence, because the whole reason you cannot ask them is the reason they cannot fight."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere down the corridor a lift went and stopped.

"I know," Orrin said.

"I know you know. I have watched you work it out at about half past four this morning while a man in bay two was dying, which is a thing I would have preferred you did somewhere else." She picked up her spoon and turned it over. "It is a good rule. It is the best rule anybody in my trade has and I would put it on the wall of that ward. And its value goes to exactly nothing the moment the person who needs it is a patient and not a witness, and the number of those moments in Morrowfen is the number of moments in the last forty years that somebody has not written down, and there is no instrument for any of them and there is not going to be one in either of our lifetimes."

"So what do I do?"

"Nothing," said Leda Vale. "You do the thing. You do it every time. Because it is right, and because the trestle would have gone into the canal otherwise, and because a rule that you only keep when it works is not a rule, it is a preference. And then you accept that on the day it is tested on a ward it is going to come out worth precisely nothing, and that this is not the machine beating you, it is the machine being an accurate description of the room." She put the spoon down. "That is what a rule is worth, Orrin. It is worth the argument. The argument is all of it."

---

"Tell me the thing," she said, at five minutes to five, when the day staff were starting to come in and were starting to look at the door.

He gave her the form number. He said it in the four pieces she could hold: one form number, one price, one line in a book, and four places.

"There is a form. It's the guild's own, and its number is GH-nine, and it is a request to record work as not having been done. And it has a price, and the price is two shillings the head for a year. And there is a working book in the guild hall on Verge Lane with a line in it that says Weir Two, night pumping, one crew, three nights, and it isn't a copy, it's the original working, and the hand changes in the middle of the page because the man who wrote that line wasn't copying anything out of the shift book, he was inventing it." His hands were flat on the table. "And the book has eleven years of the same line, and when I looked at the book this morning it wasn't the only one."

"Show me the four."

"Four places, on the page, in the same book. The Weirs, Weir Two. Northglass, a boardinghouse stair. The Sloughs, a pumping station out past the last house. And a hospital generator." He stopped. "Nine series in the book, and four of them I could name, and eight of the nine are a pump or a stair or a night gate, and one of them isn't, and I have not asked, and I am telling you the four because four is a thing you can write on a thing."

Leda Vale sat quite still with her hands around her cup.

"Two shillings the head," she said. "For a year. And you said four, and you said it slowly, and Orrin — that last one is a hospital."

"Yes."

"Do you know what hospital?"

"No. And she won't tell me, and she said she has never been asked and has written that page out about nine hundred times."

Leda put her cup down. Then she got up, went out into the corridor, and came back with a ward roster, which was a printed sheet of a page with a grid on it and the ward's name at the top and twenty-four rows of initials down the side and a column for the hour the medicine round was due.

She turned it over, took a pencil out of her apron, and wrote on the back of it, in a hand that was very small and very fast because she had been writing drug charts standing up for six years:

*There is a form. Number GH-9. It costs 2 shillings for a man for a year to say his work was not done. It has been used for: Weir Two, Lower Weirs. A boardinghouse stair, Northglass. A pumping station, the Sloughs. A hospital something - a generator, or a laundry; nobody has ever seen the page whole.*

*Ask any of us for the form number. Four of us will give it to you. It is the same form. There is a book.*

Then she put the pencil down, and looked at it, and said: "That goes up in the nurses' room, on the board by the duty list, because that is where families read, and because if it goes in a drawer it is a thing about the Weirs, and if it goes on that board it is a thing about a hospital."

"Leda — the second line. A hospital generator, and you don't know which. Somebody will read that on that board and think it means a ward clock, and somebody will think it means the resuscitation clock, and somebody will take it to their sister on the third floor, and by Thursday every nurse on this floor will be answering a question they cannot answer."

"Yes," she said. "That is what I have thought about, and I have decided that it is better than the other version, and I have also decided that I am the one who gets asked, not you, and that I have got a shift pattern and I can say *I am not on until Friday* and that is a true sentence." She squared the roster on the table. "You will notice that I have not put your name on it and I have not put the investigator's name on it and I have not put a date on it. If I put a name on it, this is a complaint by a nurse about a hall. If I leave the names off, it is a notice that somebody can be asked a question."

"That is genuinely the most sophisticated thing you have ever done," said Orrin.

"It is the third most sophisticated thing I have ever done," said Leda Vale. "Mrs Tallow in outpatients did the first before either of us was born and I have never found out what it was."

---

The day staff came in at five and the room changed temperature and the arrangement was over, and Leda put her shoes back on under the table and stood up and rolled her neck, and then she sat down again, which she had not been going to do.

"There's one more," she said. "And it's not about the form, and I have thought about whether to do it and I am going to, because you will not ask me and I have noticed that you have not asked me, and I want to see whether you can sit still while somebody else does it."

He sat still.

"I was nine," said Leda Vale. "The night of the Long Nine. I was nine years old and I was in the chapel school on Weir Lane with about sixty other people, because it was the dry hall and it had a floor and our mother was three years dead and there was nobody in our house at all, and my aunt came for us at about four in the morning with a lamp and a basket." She was looking at the gas range and not at him. "And the bells went and stopped. And I remember that I counted, because I was nine and I had decided that the bells were a thing you counted, and I got to something and then the gas lamps came up because somebody had lit them, and the bells started again, and I asked my aunt how long it had been and she said nine minutes, and I said that was a stupid number for it to stop for, and she said it was, and she was right, it is a stupid number, and that is the whole of what I know about the night the city stopped for nine minutes and killed two hundred and twelve people, and I have never once told anybody a version of it with a reason in it."

"Leda—"

"I do not know what our father did." She said it flatly, looking at him. "I do not know whether he was in it, or near it, or in a different part of the city doing a different job with his hands. I know he was a regulator and I know he went to work that night because our mother made him go and I was in the yard in my coat watching the car. I have never once pretended to anybody that I know more than that, and if you ever hear me pretend, take it away from me."

"I'm not going to ask you," said Orrin.

"I know you are not, and I have watched you not, for eighteen years, and it has never once been a kindness." She stood up. It was five o'clock and the day staff were at the range. "It is a thing you do with a mouth, Orrin, and I have watched you get very good at it. It is on the same list as the thing you did in this corridor a fortnight and a day ago with a man in bay six, and the thing you did in the flat with the grey book the same week, and the thing you did on the tram with a girl of thirteen that same Thursday. I have watched you not ask me for eighteen years and I have watched you not ask me very carefully, and it has cost me more than asking would have, and I want that on the table between us and I am not going to pretend I am being hard on you."

She put the roster in her hand and held it against her chest.

"You've been hearing things," she said. "For years. I worked that out about four days ago and I have not had the chance to be angry about it because my ward has been busy, and I have been sitting on it. You have been hearing things and you have been not-telling me things and you have been telling me about machines, and that is a way of telling somebody you love that you are near a cliff without making them come and stand at the edge, and it is a kindness, and it is the worst thing anybody has ever done to me, because I would have come. I would have come and I would have held the lamp. And I cannot hold a lamp for something I do not know is happening."

He did not say anything at all.

"That is all of it," said Leda Vale. "The lamp. Now go and do whatever it is you have to do, and come back on Tuesday, and if you turn up with a book under your arm I shall put you out in the corridor."

---

He was on the tram at ten past five with the sky going grey over the market gardens, and the city was coming up on time on all three of its districts, and he sat with his hands on a bar that was colder than it should have been and worked his way round to the thing she had actually said, which he had not been able to hold while she was in the room.

It was not the nine minutes. Everybody in Morrowfen had the nine minutes and the two hundred and twelve, and he had been carrying his own private copy of the same two numbers since he was ten.

It was the paragraph about the lamp.

He had kept the ninth beat to himself since the Monday because there was no defect to report. He had kept the regulator on his shelf because Hask had asked him to. He had kept hour-sickness out of a conversation with his sister for eighteen years because there was no form for it and nobody would have believed him. And every single one of those was a decision he had made *for her*, out of an accurate calculation about what a person can carry, and the calculation had been correct every time, and the total of it was that the people who loved him had been managing him.

And the sentence at the end of it, which was the one he would be turning over for the rest of the month, was this: that the reason a private certainty is useless is not that it cannot be proved. It is that a person who is not told cannot refuse. And that a rule about being used in a room only governs the rooms he is in.

He got off at the Quarter at half past five and walked down Verge Lane in the grey light past a shop that was not his own with a card in the window reading *licensed repairs — clocks, watches, instruments*, and thought: *there are nine districts in a book in a room on this lane, and I have told the only person in my life about four of them, over soup, at five in the morning, and she is going to put it on a board.*

He went up the outside stairs and sat on the edge of his bed with his coat on, and then got up and made tea he did not want and stood in the doorway with it, and listened for the ninth beat in the middle of the Quarter's ordinary perfect hour, and it did not come, and he noticed that he had been listening for it, and he went to bed at ten past six and slept for four hours.
