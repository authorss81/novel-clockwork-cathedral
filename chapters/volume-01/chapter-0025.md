# Chapter 25 — The Water Schedule

The notice went up on the ward board outside the Weir Two pump house at about twenty past eight in the morning on the eighth day of the third month, on a sheet of cartridge paper with four drawing pins, and it was the reason it was lawful, and it was eight and a half hours' notice.

Orrin came down the slipway at half past eight with his case in his hand and found six people standing in the rain reading it.

> *PUBLIC NOTICE — QUESTION TO BE READ.*
> *Question 4 112, Lower Weirs canal front, night draw. Submitted 2nd inst. by the Water Users' Committee, Lower Weirs pumping district. To be read in the Question Hall, Cathedral Rise, Wednesday the 8th inst., at ten of the clock. Three witnesses to be identified at the rail. The text of the question may be read at the rail on the day.*

"That is this morning," said Bevin Sarr.

"That is this morning."

"Nobody came up the slipway last night."

"No."

"Nobody told Mrs Pruitt."

"No." He turned the sheet round on the board, because the pins were at the corners and the print was small and half the people standing there could not read it at eight in the morning in the rain. "Bevin, go and get her. Do not send a boy. Go yourself, and take this off the board, and do not fold it."

It was the correct thing to do and it was not enough and everybody on that slipway understood both of those things at once, in the way that people in the Weirs have understood things for twenty years.

---

They got to the Rise at ten past nine. The Question Hall was full by ten, and full meant a hundred and thirty people in a room built for ninety, standing in the aisles, which is a thing that has not happened in that room since the Long Nine and the word was not said out loud by anybody while Orrin was in it.

The hall had nine benches of seating in rows and a rail at the front of it in brass, three stations on the rail, and behind the rail the question spindle on a pillar, and to the left of that, behind a screen of pierced brass, the edge of the Loom — water columns, weighted arms, copper paths, and a row of pressure drums along the bottom, all of it moving very slowly, all of it making a noise like a building breathing.

The gallery was up a step at the left-hand end, with nine chairs in it, and six of the nine were taken before Orrin came in, and by ten past ten there were forty people standing in the space behind the benches who had no business being in a Question Hall at all and were in it because the district had come.

Wenna Pruitt was in the fourth bench. Teo Arden was standing against the wall at the end of it with his arms folded. Bevin Sarr was in the third bench. Hester — no. Bessie Crane had not come, and had sent a message, and Orrin did not know yet what the message was. Hanna Stroud was in the front row, on the end, in her good coat, and she was not there as a witness and everybody in the room could see that she was not there as a witness.

At the very back, on a chair against the wall, Mara Venn, with a notebook open on her knees and the cap off her pen.

---

There is a list on the second-floor board in that building, and it is public, and it is the same list the district had walked past a hundred times without reading it, because it is a list of questions and not a list of answers, and a district that has spent twenty years being the answer to somebody else's question had not had a great deal of reason to go and look at a list of other people's questions.

*4 112. Lower Weirs canal front, night draw. Water Users' Committee, Lower Weirs pumping district. Second inst.*

Nobody in the Weirs knew what a water users' committee was. It is a body the water act makes for a district where more than a certain number of properties draw on a common main, and its membership is by property, and property in the pumping district is held by about ninety people, and it has a chairman who is a chandler, and it has met eleven times in four years, and it has never once had a night worker on it, and there is no form on which one could be put on it, because the qualification is the freehold or the lease of a property that draws on the main, and a man who is not certified and is not a ratepayer at that address has neither.

Orrin had worked that out in about four seconds in a wet corridor at ten past nine, standing up, with the crowd going past him, and then it had taken him the rest of the morning to work out why it was true, which is a different and longer thing.

---

The three stations on the rail were filled at twenty-five minutes past nine, while the room was still coming in and before the first of the arguments started, and it took ninety seconds and nobody argued about any of it.

The operator was at the left station, a man in his forties with a green loupe on a chain who put his own name in and the hour in and signed for the spindle, and who had done it a thousand times.

The public record keeper was in the middle. Ostrand, who had taken down the notice in the first month and had not wished to be asked a single question then and did not wish to be asked a single question now, with the ledger the size of a paving stone on a small wheeled table in front of him.

The affected-district resident station was filled on the right by a woman from Green Span.

She was about sixty. She had a Green Span public-health badge on her coat and a list of dates written inside the front cover of a small book, and she was on the second-Wednesday rota, and she had come on a tram and she was going to be back on it. The form in front of her had a column for the district she was a resident of, and it had *Green Span* in it, and she was a resident of Green Span, and she was signing the station of the affected district for a question about the water of a district four miles and a floodgate away from her.

"Nobody asked her anything," said Teo Arden, at the wall. "I want that on the record. I looked at her for a minute and a half and I did not ask her anything, because there is nothing I could have asked her that was not an insult, and because she came here on a tram."

"She has done it before," said Wenna Pruitt, without turning round.

"I know she has done it before."

There is no form on which a district stands. A standing is a written claim, and a written claim requires a body that a room will accept as the party, and the pumping district has population and a poor rate and eleven buildings with a man on the ridge of every one of them, and no standing of any kind in the civic model, and has not had since before anybody in the room was born. So the Weirs' delegates were in a room, in a hundred and thirty people, in the same building, and they were in the gallery, and a standing is a written claim, and a written claim is the only thing that gets a person onto a rail.

Nobody in that building thought this was a trick. That is the part Orrin could not get past for the whole morning. The record keeper did not think it was a trick. The operator at the left station, who had signed a hundred and forty of these, did not think it was a trick. The woman from Green Span with the rota inside her coat did not think it was a trick, and would have said so to anybody who had put it to her, and would have been telling the truth.

The Compact had been amended about twenty years ago to permit a written district standing to be substituted for a resident of the affected district, so that a Reading about a district with a large population could be witnessed without finding a person in it who was not a ratepayer, a householder, or a person otherwise competent to be asked to sign for a place with a hundred and forty thousand people in it. It had been amended for a good reason. There had been a difficulty in a district in the north about a Reading that could not be witnessed at all. The amendment had done exactly what it was for.

---

There were three quarters of an hour of waiting and Orrin spent eleven minutes of it on a lamp, between half past nine and twenty to ten, because a man in a hall of clocks is the man they fetch when a lamp on an indicator is flickering, and because there was nothing else to do with a quarter of an hour and nothing in the book to write in it.

The district indicator stands on the wall at the left of the hall, a brass case the size of a biscuit tin with eleven small windows in it, one for each district, and a needle in each window showing how much stored rotation that district was holding. Three of the windows were showing a fault lamp and the hall's own men were somewhere at the back with a board, because a lamp that is out is not a fault and a lamp that is *flickering* is a lamp on the end of its life, and a flickering lamp on an indicator in a Question Hall is a thing somebody will be asked about in an inquiry in about four years.

"I can do that," said Orrin, before the hall's man had got a hand near it. "It'll be a filament or a contact. If it's a contact I can clean it in a minute and you can sign it off, and if it's a filament I'll want to fetch a lamp and you'll have to stand there and hold the cover."

The hall's man was grateful in the way that men are grateful to anybody who can help in a corridor, and Orrin had his hand on the brass case with the indicator windows in it, eleven small dials with a district's reserve in each, and a hand going into the frame, and the housing was cold and the resonance in it was the resonance of eleven district reserves and a spindle behind a wall.

He knew what it would cost before he asked. That was the whole of the change and it had taken him a month: not that he could not ask, but that he could tell. He had a price in the front of his mind like a man who has learned the price of a loaf.

*One question. One named load. The night draw on the Lower Weirs main, and the fourth daily cycle of the front's storage that the question will cause. What gives first.*

It came up through the brass in about two seconds and it was a lean and a weight and not a picture and not a word, the way it always was.

*The Weir One gate's escapement governor. It is the oldest thing on that main by about thirty years and it was made for two cycles a day and it will be asked for four. It will hold for the whole of the tide period. It is the only part of this that will not be asked about and it is the only part of this I am frightened of.*

Then the room went grey at the edges and the hum came up on the right and there was the smell of hot metal at the back of his throat, and it was over in about a hundred and forty seconds, and he stood in a public hall in Cathedral Rise with his hand on a brass case and a hundred and thirty people in it and nobody had noticed a thing.

He sat down. He wrote nothing, there, and he went and found Teo Arden.

"I am going to try to say a thing out loud in that room in about a quarter of an hour," he said, "and I am telling you first because I wrote a rule on a Sunday, and you are the only person in the building who can refuse me, which is exactly why I am telling you and not somebody else."

"Go on."

"The fourth cycle. The daytime storage will make four cycles a day instead of two, because the front has to give up its night water back in the day. Four. And the Weir One gate's escapement governor is thirty years older than the works' standard and it was made for two, and it will hold for the whole tide period, and it is the only thing in the whole of this that nobody is going to ask about." He had his hand on the rail at the top of the step. "I have never been more certain of anything in my life."

"Then say it."

"No. I want to hear you say no first, so that I have heard it."

Teo Arden looked at him for a while, and he was twenty years old and he was not being kind.

"No," he said. "You will not say it."

"I know."

"Go on, then. Tell me why, so that you cannot come back next week with a better speech."

"Because it came out of a machine and there is no form for it and there is no column and I have no standing in this room and I cannot show a person in this room where it is written down." He got it out flat, the way he got things out on a landing. "And because the first time one of us in this district puts a true thing into a room and cannot show anybody where it came from, that is the afternoon the district becomes a thing people are careful about. And I have watched it happen to a trade. I have watched it happen to nine districts in a book and to a man with a bar and a jaw mark on a governor and to a woman who signed a true copy eleven times and lost nineteen years. And you would hand it to a hundred and thirty people with no page in it, and then in six months' time when one of us has got something we can print, this is what you did with it. They'll all be thinking about this. There is no room in this building where a man says *I heard it in a machine* and gets a different answer."

"That is the third rule," said Orrin. "And it has been there seventeen days and it has already stopped me, and I want you to understand that I wrote it and that it is the only piece of my own method that has ever done anything."

"Write that down as well," said Teo Arden, "and then come and stand where you can see the rail, because you are going to want to see it, and I have never seen one run and I have wanted to for two years."

---

At two minutes to ten the operator at the left station read the question aloud, and it was the ordinary sentence, and it was a named act.

> *Lower Weirs pumping district, canal front, from Bellweather Road to the Weir One gate. That the night draw upon the district main be suspended from the fifteenth to the thirtieth inst., and that the daytime draw be held to the present schedule, and that the front's storage be drawn down by day only. Effective upon issue.*
>
> *Submitting body: the Water Users' Committee of the Lower Weirs pumping district, a body constituted under the water act, nine members, property qualifications.*
>
> *Paying district: the Clockwork Quarter.*

"The paying district is on the question," said Orrin, to nobody, in the third bench. "It is never on the notice. It never has been."

At ten of the clock the record keeper took the four lines off the board and the operator read them out, because that is the procedure, and the procedure does not vary, and the reason the procedure does not vary is that the Compact requires the whole of it to be stated in a room with three people on a rail.

The notice was on a brass-framed card and the third line was set two sizes smaller than the other three, and sat above the operator's signature, and the operator read all four at the same speed and in the same voice.

> *QUESTION RECEIVED*
>
> *TRACE: with the night draw suspended and the daytime draw held, the front's storage will carry the spring tides from the fifteenth to the thirtieth inst. without a further draw on any district reserve.*
>
> *PRESSURE: the front will run four to eleven minutes late on eleven nights in that period; the front's school signals and the ferry will carry the delay; no district will be required to give reserve beyond the draw stated.*
>
> *BLIND MARGIN: unregistered district labour is not represented.*
>
> *TOLL: twenty-six district minutes and a witness interval.*

He read the last two and then signed, and put the card into the slot, and the Loom behind the screen began to move a little faster and did not stop for about two hours and ten minutes.

---

Nobody in that hall knew what the blind margin said.

That is the first thing that is not true about it, and the correct version is: everybody in that hall heard it, and the room went on, because the line is a required line and it has been a required line for forty-three years, and it is set two sizes smaller than an operator's signature, and it is a *category*, and a category is not a person.

The blind margin in the first month, on the seventeenth, at three o'clock in the afternoon on a day Orrin could remember the smell of, had said: *unregistered night work is not represented.* Four people in that hall had taken the number off the board and measured it, and one of them had written down that it stood at eleven to the point against twenty-two for the trace line and fourteen for the signature, and had said, out loud, to three other people, that it was a form.

This one said *unregistered district labour*. Not a night crew. Not a pump house. Not four men and a boy on a board in chalk. Labour — a category, drawn in a district-sized sweep, and the machine had got wider exactly as the room had got better informed, because the room had spent four months learning to say *the pumping district's own returns* in a chamber and had thereby taught a filing clerk that a district is a thing with hours in it.

Mara Venn stood up in the back of the hall at a quarter to eleven.

"I am the investigator of the Office of Public Reckoning and I have no standing in this room either," she said, "and I want to say that first and I want to be exact about what I am objecting to, because I have spent nine years learning that an objection which is nearly right is worse than no objection at all.

"The Compact has four safeguards. A concrete question. A benefit and a cost both shown. Three independent witnesses. A recorded toll. I am not objecting to the first, the third, or the fourth, and I have no quarrel with the question, which is as concrete as anything filed in this hall in ten years, and which I have read twice and would have filed myself if anybody had asked me to.

"I am objecting to the second. The cost has to be shown. The notice has shown it. The notice says the front will run four to eleven minutes late on eleven nights, and it names the school signals and the ferry, and that is a cost and it is a real one and it will fall on real people, and I have no objection to it. My objection is that the *whole* of the cost is not that. The whole of the cost is in the line the operator has just read at the same speed as the other three, and that line says the hours of the people who are going to be doing that fourth cycle are not in the calculation, and the Compact does not require the omission to be *corrected*."

She put her hand on the back of the bench in front of her.

"I have chosen this ground deliberately and I am going to say why, because I have been doing this for nine years and I have never once got a room to look at a person. There is no way in this hall to make a hundred and thirty people look at the men on a board. There is one way in this hall to make them look at a form, and the form is in front of them, and it has been read out loud, and the Compact requires it to be printed, and it is printed, and it is two sizes smaller than a signature. So I have got the room to read it. That is all I have got, and I have used it, and I am not going to be able to use it again after today, because a line that has been read out loud in a room is a line that has been read out loud in a room, and the second time is not news."

Nobody said anything.

"Three years ago," she said, "I sat in a committee room in a building on the Rise for two days and watched forty delegates be bored by a bracket. And then a clerk asked what the form number on it was, and I have had that for nine years, and I have built everything since on it, and I would like to say it out loud in this hall because I think it is the whole of what I know: you cannot get a city to look at a man. You can get it to look at a form, once, and then you have used the form up."

An usher came down the aisle.

"The investigator has addressed the room," said the operator at the left station, without turning round. "The question is before the rail. The room has been heard."

---

It ran for two hours and ten minutes and the result was better than the result in the first month, and Orrin had to sit through the betterness of it.

The trace held. The front carried the tides. There would be no further draw on any district reserve for the front between the fifteenth and the thirtieth, which was more than the pumping ward's reserve had managed in any month since the diversion, and which meant that a district with an empty reserve would not have to go out onto a street in the autumn and say so again, and that was worth more than Ferrywright Bridge had been worth, and everybody in the room including Orrin knew it, and it was true.

The schools would be four to eleven minutes late on eleven nights. The ferry would carry the delay and would carry it on a timetable that the ferry company had printed eleven weeks ago and could not reprint, because the schedule it was printed from did not exist until ten o'clock this morning.

The Committee had done the arithmetic. It had had the Assembly's six returns, printed in full with the minutes, in front of it, because those returns were the only public document in Morrowfen that said a district had worked hours at a civic machine, and it had had the notice of the first month with its measured blind margin, and it had had a survey of the front, and it had voted on the second inst., nine to four, and the four had gone in the minute with their names and their reasons, and the reasons were about the flooding of last spring and about eleven households in it and about a district that had once been on time and was not on time, and there was not one dishonest word in any of it.

The Weirs' delegates were in the room, at the fourth bench, and in the gallery, and along the aisle, and forty-one of them had come, and they had not voted, and they were not on the rail, and there was no form.

Mara Venn's objection was written into the minutes of the Question Hall in four lines at eleven o'clock that morning, and the minutes went into a ledger, and the ledger went into the Cathedral's own record, and the Compact gave no person or body the power to stop a Reading on the ground of what its blind margin said, because a blind margin is a *statement of what is not known*, and the Compact was drafted in a year when men believed that a machine which said what it did not know was a safe machine.

---

They came out into the street at about twenty past twelve and the rain had started again and stopped again and nobody could decide what to do with themselves.

Rook came out of the hall at the same time, and she came out on her own, and she was not on anybody's rota, and Orrin had not known until Mara told him in the lobby that the chair of the Assembly's public-health committee had been in the third bench from the front for two hours and ten minutes, in a plain dark coat, with her ward's badge on it, because the returns her committee had ordered printed had been laid before the water users' committee in the first month and had been read, and had been the arithmetic.

He went after her, because he had no idea what else to do with the morning.

She stopped on the steps and gave him about four seconds of a face, and it was not unkind, and it was not sorry.

"Mr Vale."

"Ma'am. Your committee's returns."

"Printed in full with the minutes, on a line in the estimates at four pounds ten out of my own ward's clerical allocation, and ordered by a chamber that would not have ordered it if I had not paid for it." She put her hood up against the rain. "They are on the table of a water users' committee, and the water users' committee has voted nine to four, and the four minority are in the minute, and the return is the strongest document in the district and it is the reason the district's water is being taken off it at night in the spring." She looked at him. "I have been in this chamber nine years and I have never been able to make a document do that. I have only ever been able to make a document exist. You put one in a room and the room finds a use for it that you would not have chosen, and it is always a better use than the one you had, and it is never the one you wanted."

"You could have said that in the hall."

"I have said it to you on the steps," said Imani Rook, "and I am not going to say it in a hall, because in a hall I would be a woman asking a room to distrust a return, and the room would hear *do not believe your own arithmetic* and they would hear it correctly, and it would be in the minutes in my own name, and in a year somebody would use it against a district that needs every sentence it has." She started down the steps. "I do not apologise for the arithmetic. I was shown arithmetic. I have never been shown anything else and I am not going to pretend to have been."

She was gone before he had worked out whether he had wanted an apology, which he had, and which was not going to be any use to him, and which he would not have got.

---

He went down the Rise at ten past one, on foot, because he wanted to walk, and about halfway down he understood that he was walking the way a man walks when he is counting.

Three minutes in the first month. Twenty-six in the third. Off the same district, in the same direction, under a question he had filed himself the first time and had not filed the second.

The Toll Stair was scrubbed at half past twelve and re-chalked at one, and the new chalk was still wet on the third step, and there was a bucket and a board and a man on the stair with a piece of chalk, and it said:

*26*
*CLOCKWORK QUARTER*

Twenty-six minutes. Off the district that holds the most reserve in this city and has the most accurate public clock in this city, and has never once in forty-three years had an opinion about a Reading it did not care for. The draw was in proportion to what the Quarter held, and the Quarter held a great deal, and the draw from a full reserve is a rounding error, and the whole of the volume's argument about where a toll lands was sitting in wet chalk on the third step of a public staircase in the rain.

And the number on the stair was still the only number this city published, and it was the smallest number in the book, and it was the same as it had been in the first month, and the only thing that had changed was that it was now eight times as big and the omission in the footer had gone from a crew to a district, and both of those were improvements, and both of them were the Cathedral doing exactly what it was built to do, in front of forty-one people from the Weirs who had read the footer in the first month and understood it perfectly, and who were not on the rail.

He stood on the stair in the rain and looked at the two figures and did the sum, because it was the sum, and because it was the only thing he had.

*First month. Third month. Three minutes, and a footer that said a night crew was not represented. Now twenty-six minutes, and a footer that says district labour is not represented. Twice the size, twice the width, and the second one is better in every measurable way, and the water is worth more than the bridge was, and nobody did anything wrong, and four people in a room nine days ago in the Weirs sat in the rain reading a notice that had been posted on the outside of a pump house because the ordinance required it.*

*And there is not one thing in this city that I can put in a sentence that makes the next one smaller.*

*And my rule says no result of mine goes out of this room without a person named who can refuse it. It stopped me at about twenty to ten this morning in front of a brass case, and it was right, and I have not got anything.*

*Twenty-six minutes. It is still the only number anybody publishes, and I still cannot get to the other end of the sentence, and from today there are two of them, and the second one is eight times the first and names a bigger thing.*

He got out the grey book on the top step of the Toll Stair in the rain and wrote four lines, standing up, in the sound of a hundred and thirty people coming out of a building behind him, and the rain was getting into the page faster than he could write and he did not care.

> *Wednesday the 8th, the third month. Question 4 112, Lower Weirs canal front, night draw. Read at ten. Three witnesses. Nobody from the pumping district on the rail, and a written standing is a claim and a claim is a form and there is no form by which a district stands, and nobody in the building thought that was a trick.*
>
> *M. Venn's objection entered in the minutes in nine words. A blind margin is a statement of what is not known. The Compact requires it printed. It does not require it corrected, and it gives nobody the power to stop a Reading for having one.*
>
> *Half past nine, indicator case, left wall of the Question Hall, question asked of one named load: what gives first if the front runs four cycles a day instead of two. Answer: the Weir One gate's escapement governor, made for two, thirty years older than the works' standard, will hold, and is the only part of this nobody will ask about. Cost of the question: a hundred and forty seconds, edges clean, hand on brass, the room afterwards, the stair. Entered as a reading cost and not as a toll. I knew the price before I asked. The price was a hundred and forty seconds of my life and it bought me the fourth line of a notice I could not file, and I could not say it in that room, and the reason I could not say it is a rule I wrote seventeen days ago and a boy of twenty who is not grateful to me for it.*
>
> *And the footer. Unregistered district labour is not represented. It was unregistered night work in the first month and it is unregistered district labour today, and it has got wider as we have got better at speaking, and it is still the same line and it is still set two sizes smaller than a signature.*
>
> *Twenty-six minutes, against three, and the Clockwork Quarter both times. On the stair in wet chalk at one o'clock in the afternoon, in my district, and the draw is in proportion to what we hold, and the third step of that stair is the entire public record of what a city spends to be right.*
