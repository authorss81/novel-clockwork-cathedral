# Chapter 112 — What Was Not There

He went up Cathedral Rise on the Thursday morning of the twenty-fourth of the second month, at ten, with a lamp in his right hand and nothing else in either of them that could be called a tool.

The steps were the same steps he had walked down in the first year of this and had walked to the foot of in January and turned round at. The stone at the fourth tread from the bottom was worn into a shallow bowl by a century of people going up to be told something, and the wear was deepest in the middle of the tread and not at either edge, which is what a century of people does to a step, and which had given him, at fifteen, the idea that this building had been designed for the walking of it and had not been designed for anything that happened at the top.

He had a whole morning and no reason to be early, and he was at the door at two minutes to ten, and he was early because being early is a thing a man can control and nothing else about that day was.

The north door was a door. That is the first thing to say about it and the only thing that matters. It was a double door of plain oak with iron furniture and a stone lintel, set into a wall of the same stone as the steps, and there was a lamp beside it at a working height and no brass on it and no plate and no lettering of any kind, and a man in a cap sitting on a chair inside a glazed box two feet away who had evidently been sitting there for some hours and had evidently been left there deliberately, and who let him in without asking who he was, and who then said, "Miss Cray will be at the door, sir. She is coming down now."

Orrin had not expected to be let in by a porter. He had expected to be asked for his name and a reason and a signature, and he had had four answers ready, and the porter had needed none of them, and it turned out that a door with no form on it is administered by a man in a cap who opens it, and that is the whole of the difference and it took him twenty minutes inside the building to understand that the twenty minutes were the point.

The Director was at the door.

She was smaller than her handwriting, which is a thing that happens to people whose whole work is on paper, and she wore no collar of any kind and had a folder under her arm that she did not open. She said good morning, and gave him her hand, and looked at him for about two seconds longer than a greeting needs, and then said, "You wrote three questions and I have answered two of them and refused the third, and you have come, so I am going to say the other half of it before we go down. You have not been asked about anything in this building and neither has anybody else in it, and I am not going to pretend that an open door is a permission. I cannot make anybody walk through it. I have had a door open for four years and I have watched two hundred people walk past it. Come down when you are ready."

"I am ready."

"Then you will go first," she said, "and I will not come, and the reason I will not come is not that I do not trust you. It is that if I come in after you then the room contains a Director, and a Director is a thing that cannot be refused, and you came here to find out what can be."

He had not thought of that. He had thought of several things and not of that.

And then, from the stair behind her, a woman of about fifty-eight in a brown coat with a key ring in her hand and a folded sheet of paper in the other, who said, "I am Miss Cray," and then, before he had said anything at all, read from the paper she was holding.

"North chamber and the two rooms behind it. Held sealed since the second of December of the year before last, and held before that, in the same words, by a Director in writing dated nineteen forty-one, which is sixteen years ago, and the direction is in the file upstairs and the file is nineteen inches. One: nobody enters without a person of this building with him. Two: nothing is carried out of either room, including paper, and that one is mine. Three: no instrument is brought into either room without the written consent of a person in this building who is not the person carrying it. Four: nobody is to be alone in the room. Five, which I added in nineteen fifty-one and which is mine, because nobody else wanted it, is that nobody is to be alone with a machine that nobody in this building has asked about."

She folded the paper and put it in her coat pocket.

"That last one has been in that file for six years," she said, "and I have never once had cause to use it, and I am using it now, and I am telling you that I am using it before I use it, because you will want to know afterwards whether I sprang it."

"You have not sprung anything," Orrin said. "I have read about you."

"You have read a true copy in a lock-up with a covering note on it," said Miss Cray. "That is not the same document and I have never seen it. Come."

The stair went down a long way and turned twice and was older than the building's other stairs by a hundred years, judging by the tread, and the walls at the bottom were a different stone and colder, and the air at the bottom was not the air of a corridor. It was the air of a room that has had no fire in it for a long time and has a window somewhere that does not close.

The door at the bottom was iron and it was not locked, and Miss Cray took a key out of the ring and used it anyway, and said, "The lock is a good lock. It is unlocked because a lock that cannot be opened is a form with a handle on it, and this building does not keep those."

Then she put the key back in her coat and turned to him and said the thing that the rest of the morning turned out to hang from.

"I am going in with you and I am not going to be alone in here with you. If you put your hand on anything in either room I will walk out, and the door will be shut behind me by the spring on it, and you will not be able to follow me out, because that is what the door is for, and I am not going to apologise for it and I am not going to treat it as a trick. If you want me to be in the room with you while you touch something then you do not want me in the room, you want a witness, and a witness is a different job and a cheaper one."

"Understood."

"Say it back to me."

"I will not put my hand on anything without you in the room, and if I do you leave and I do not follow."

"Good," said Miss Cray, and went in, and he followed her, and the door swung to on its spring behind them both.

The north chamber was about twenty-two feet by fourteen.

That was the first thing and it was a mistake, because everything in him went to the size. It was twenty-two feet by fourteen and eleven feet to the ceiling, and the ceiling was a plaster vault with ribs, and the floor was stone flags, and the proportions were right. He had spent nineteen years learning what a room built for a machine looks like and the room was one. The proportions of it were the proportions of a thing that needs to be a particular size for a particular reason, and the reason was not in him, and the room told him so in the only language it had, which was shape.

There was a rectangle in the floor.

It was nine feet by five and it was not a different colour, because there was no colour left in a room that had been shut for sixteen years, but the mortar in it was newer than the mortar round it, and the edge of it was a straight cut with a chisel in it that was barely rounded, and somebody had made it good and had made it good badly, and had been in a hurry in a way that has not changed since nineteen twenty-two.

In the middle of the rectangle there were four threaded holes in a square, about eighteen inches apart, each one clean, each one with a steel washer lying in the bottom of it, and the washers were the only bright things in the room. Around the rectangle, at about waist height, the paint on the wall was a different shade in a band about six inches high, and the band went the full width of the rectangle and not one inch beyond it, which meant that whatever had stood there had been stood against the wall and had been wider than the floor it stood on.

Overhead, in the vault, there was a rectangular opening about two feet by three, and a blanking plate had been screwed over it, and the four screws in the plate were bright, and the plate had been painted the same colour as the ceiling at some point in a different decade.

And in the room there was a chair, and a desk with one drawer, and a barometer on the wall, and a mantel clock on the desk, and a tin cup beside it, and a wooden box under the desk with about eighty circular forms in it, eleven inches across, blank, ruled with concentric circles and a straight line from the centre to the edge, and the paper in them was the colour of paper that has been in a dry room since the war.

Somebody had worked in this room. There was a chair for it and a desk for it and a barometer because people who work want to know the weather, and a cup, and eighty blanks of a form that somebody filled in and somebody else checked, and the whole of that was in a room the size of a machine that was not in the room.

Orrin stood in the middle of the floor, on the newer mortar, and did the thing he could not stop doing, which was to go round the room at a walking pace and count.

He counted without meaning to. He had told Wenna Pruitt in January that a bare surname in his own head was the kind of thing that cost forty-one seconds, and he was not going to write anything down in a room like this, and so he counted, and the number came up: nine feet by five of floor, four anchors, one duct, six paces by four, and eighty-one forms, and he had held all of it in about eleven seconds, and he wrote the number of paces in his own head and not on any paper, and he was aware while he was doing it that this was the exact skill that had been given away in a cold room in the Weirs in the autumn before last, in exchange for the loss of a sequence and the knowledge that it had gone, and that what he had put in its place was a book on a shelf behind a bench and a habit of writing things down, which had cost him the thing and saved him the other one.

He stopped, and made himself stand still, and then said out loud, to a woman in a brown coat, "There is a shape here and the thing is not in it."

"There is a shape here," Miss Cray agreed, "and I have had the inventory in my coat for nineteen years and I have never seen the room. I signed it. That is the honest position and I am not going to improve on it in front of you."

She took the folded sheet out of her pocket and did not open it, and then opened it.

"I have the description in my own writing and I am going to read it to you because you asked me a question by post last week and I am not a machine and I do not answer questions with a gesture." She held the sheet away from her face. "North chamber. Dimensions twenty-two feet by fourteen by eleven. Floor, stone, flags. Anchor fixings, four, threaded, with washers. Overhead duct, two feet by three, blanked. Fittings, one chair, one desk with drawer, one barometer, one mantel clock, one cup. Forms, unexecuted, in a wooden box, number not stated. That is the description of this room and I wrote the whole of it from a piece of paper in a file in nineteen thirty-eight and I have been in this building nineteen years and I did not put one of these sentences in this room with my own eyes until this morning."

She turned the sheet round and held it out to him, and it was four sheets stapled, and the top sheet was a list of about forty lines in four hands, and the last line was a classification of the whole of the north block.

*Chamber fittings, north chamber: none.*

"That is the only line in this file in nineteen inches that is a statement about the absence of something rather than the presence of it," Miss Cray said, "and I have never known who wrote it, and it is dated the ninth of March nineteen thirty-eight, and I found it in nineteen fifty-one when they made me sign the inventory, and I have been carrying it about in a coat for six years."

Nobody said anything. The room had a temperature and it was not a comfortable one and it had been the same temperature since nineteen forty-one, which was sixteen years and a long time for a room with a door on it.

Then Miss Cray turned to the second and third of the four lines on her sheet and read them out loud, which she said she was obliged to do.

"The second: a unit is installed on the floor below the north gallery, entered in this building's records as a unit, described in nineteen thirty-eight by a person who was not a surveyor. The third: the description is as follows and I am going to read you the description, and I want you to hear that the description is accurate."

He stood very still.

"Two hundred and thirty feet overall, continuous, occupying the gallery and the two floors below it, of which no part is visible at any time to any person in this building except by the removal of the plate on the north stair, which is fixed and has never been off, and which the nineteen thirty-eight description says is not to be taken off, and the nineteen thirty-eight description is signed by a Director of this building and I have read it and it does not give a reason and I have looked for a reason in four inches of type for six years."

"And what does it say it is?" Orrin said.

"It does not say what it is," said Miss Cray. "It says what it is made of, which is brass, and cast iron, and glass, and a quantity of something the writer calls plate and does not define, and it says it stands on nineteen foundations, and it says the nineteen foundations are not to be cut, and it says the north plate is not to be taken off, and it says the whole of it is kept, and it says, in nineteen thirty-eight, in these words, *the unit is not required to be understood in order to be kept.*"

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above them, four floors up and a long way off, a door closed in a building that was on its ordinary Thursday.

"That is the whole of what is written about it," Miss Cray said, "in nineteen years, in four hands, in four inches of type, in a file nineteen inches deep, and I have signed it eleven times without ever asking what a unit was, and I would like it recorded that I asked the question in the year before last, in writing, and I was told that the question was not the registrar's."

"Who told you?"

"I will not tell you that," she said, "and it is not secrecy and it is that I am a person in this building and I am about to be asked to do something in it, and I am not going to be the person who put a Director's name in a conversation with a clockmaker in a cold room. Ask the question somewhere it costs somebody else more than it costs me."

He had come up the steps that morning for three questions and he had got four, and none of them was the one he came for, and the last of them was a refusal with no name in it, and it was the best-mannered refusal he had ever had.

They went into the two rooms behind. The second was bare, with a stone sink, a lead pipe, a drain in the floor, and a smell of wet stone that had been there for a century and would be there for a century. The third was the same with a bench in it and nothing on the bench, and the walls of both had been painted at some point and the paint had gone off in blisters where the cold had come through.

And then Miss Cray unlocked the plate on the north stair.

It took her nine minutes and a spanner she had to fetch from a cupboard two floors up, and Orrin offered and she said no, because it was in her hand and hers was the hand with the ring on it, and because there is a kind of competence that is not transferable and a man who offers to take a spanner off a registrar nineteen years in a job is a man who thinks the job is the spanner.

When it came off there was a space in the wall about four feet square and a stair going down into cold air, and a smell that was not a smell but a pressure, and a light from below that was not electric and did not flicker.

He did not name it. He has never named it. There is a name for it in a file nineteen inches deep on the fourth floor of this building and it is the name of a thing that is kept, and the whole of what he had been told in four years in a room off Bellweather Road is that a person who cannot say what a thing is can still be shown what it does to the people near it, and he was not going to spend the first two seconds he had ever had in front of it on finding a word.

What he did, in the four seconds the plate was off, was listen. Not with his ear. With the thing that had been in his ear since he was a boy in a room over a chandler's shop, the thing that used to give him a sequence he could hold and now could not, and which had cost him nothing yet this year and which he put his hand out towards, at the level of the rail, the way a man puts his hand out to a horse he has not decided about.

What it gave him was nothing at all. Not a hum and not a silence. A place where a hum would be, the way a floor is a place where a foot is.

He felt his hair go up on the back of his neck, and he understood, standing on a cold stair with his hand an inch from a rail, that the four keeper's tolls had each taken something specific off him, and that this was not one of them, and that he had never in his life had the sense simply refuse to arrive.

He took his hand back and put it behind him and stepped up one tread.

"If you touch that rail," said Miss Cray, from the top of the stair, "I go out now and you do not come after me, and you do not get to be a man who has been inside it."

"I was not going to touch it."

"I know you were not," she said. "That is not the reason I said it. I said it because the rule exists for the case where you are going to, and you have not been here long enough for me to know which kind of man is standing on my stair, and I am not going to find out with the plate off."

He came up. She put the plate back herself and did the eight bolts up by hand, and then went to the door, and did not open it, and stood with her back against it.

"Twenty-two years," she said. "Four inches of type. And a shape on a floor with the anchors in it. And I am a registrar, and I have spent my life on a document that says what a thing is, and I can tell you that a document which says a thing is *kept* and does not say what it *does* is not an incomplete document. It is a complete document about a different question, and the question it answers is not the one anybody asks it."

"Then what is it a complete document about?"

"It is a complete document about a person," said Miss Cray, "and the person is somebody who wanted it not to be touched, and the wanting is on the record, and that is the only thing about this floor I have ever been able to say out loud with a straight face."

"Nineteen years in this building," said Orrin. "You have carried that line in a coat since 1951 and you have never said it to anybody."

"I have said it to two people and one of them is a Director and one of them is you," said Miss Cray, "and I have not been thanked for it by either, and I have not wanted to be, and I would like it understood that I am not a person who has been wronged. People say that. They say *you must have been so frustrated* and they say it in the voice they keep for other people's servants, and I have been a servant of nobody for nineteen years, and the last thing I want is to be consoled about a stone floor."

She said it without heat, the way a person says a thing they have said before and expect to say again, and then she went and picked up her lamp.

He thought about his own four lines in the narrow book, and the two blank ones, and the two that had been left eighteen months and would probably be left for ever, and he did not say any of it, because she was leaning on a door in a cold corridor and because there was a form of this conversation in which he was offered something and he had come here to find out who may refuse him.

They went up. She locked the door on the spring and took the key out and looked at it and then put it back in her coat pocket, which was the answer to his second question given in brass and by a woman with nineteen years in a building, and he understood it and could not write it down, and did not try.

The Director was on the landing outside the north door, where she had been the whole time, with the folder still under her arm and unopened, and she had not come down and she had not gone away.

"You are not going to tell me what is under the north stair," she said.

"Not this morning."

"No," she said. "You are not, and that is the correct answer, and I have had four years of people telling me what is under the north stair, and every one of them told me because I asked, and I did not ask."

"I want the rooms and the records," Orrin said. "I do not want the machine. Not this month."

"That is nineteen years the first time anybody has said that to me," said the Director of the Cathedral. "Four hundred and some, if you count the men who came on surveying business, and not one of them wanted the paper. I have taken it for eleven years and I have been told eleven times that the paper is what anybody sensible wants, and you are the first person to ask me for the paper and then not to ask me anything at all about the other thing, and I would like you to understand that I am going to remember that, and that remembering it is not a form and there is nothing you can do about it."

The porter let him out. The stone at the fourth tread from the bottom had a bowl worn in the middle of it and nothing worn at either edge, and he went down it and down the rest, and out onto the pavement, and stood for a moment in the middle of Cathedral Rise in the middle of the day with a lamp he had not lit in his hand.

On the Friday the twenty-fifth he wrote it out at the bench in the grey ledger, at length, because a room that is only in one man's head is a room that a person in a bad situation cannot copy, and the shape of that rule is the only part of anything anybody in this city has ever been able to take away.

He wrote the dimensions, and the four anchors, and the blanking plate, and the eighty-one forms, and the five things a person had needed in order to work, and the four hands and four inches of type, and the sentence about being kept and not about being understood, and he wrote the thing Miss Cray said in the corridor, and then he stopped, because a document that says a thing is kept and does not say what it does is a complete document about a person, and he wanted to copy that sentence into his own book more than he had wanted anything in two years, and he was not going to, and he wrote down why.
