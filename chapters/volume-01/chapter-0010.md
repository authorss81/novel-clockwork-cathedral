# Chapter 10 — The First Demand

"You have to understand what you are asking me for," said Mara Venn, "and then I am going to tell you what I think of it, and then you are going to disagree with me, and we will both be right in the usual horrible way."

She had two chairs pulled round to her table, a lamp burning at eleven in the morning, and a sheet of Assembly stationery in front of her, which was a heavier paper than anything else in the building and had a small crown on it.

"I'm not writing a speech. If you write a speech, a speech is a thing people vote against, because a speech has a tone, and the tone will be the story." She dipped the pen. "I'm writing a demand. A demand is a thing with a number in it, and a number can be read by a clerk, and a clerk can put it in a bundle."

"Two clauses," said Orrin. "And they are not the same size, and I want the small one first, because if they throw the big one out they will have touched the small one, and the small one is the one that opens the door."

She kept writing while he talked, which she was better at than he was, because she could take a thing in the order it arrived and not in the order he wanted it.

"The first clause. On the seventeenth, a question was filed in the Question Hall and charged to the Clockwork Quarter, and three minutes were drawn from the Quarter's reserve, and the draw was displayed on the Toll Stair in chalk. I want that entry in the Assembly's record, with the district named, so that the charge is a charge and not a rumour."

"The stair says the district."

"The stair says the district in chalk at half past five every morning for about sixteen hours." She looked at him. "Do you know who keeps it? A man called Ostrow with a bucket and a board. Does his record survive? Does the Assembly have a copy of any of it? Go and look at the binder on the shelf behind you, the tall one, the blue one."

He looked. The blue binder was four inches thick and full of printed minutes, and the last third of it was annual reports, and he turned it over.

"Chalk," he said.

"Chalk. Every reading for forty-three years exists in the world as a bucket and a board and a man's memory, and a district that wants to know what it paid in a given week cannot find out, and cannot complain, because complaining requires a figure." She put that down. "So the first clause: that the toll of every Reading be entered in the Assembly's record, with the district it was drawn from, and that the entry be durable. That is a clerk copying a line off a slate. It is the cheapest thing anybody has ever asked this Assembly for and it will be refused, and I want it refused in writing, because the refusal is worth something to me."

"And the second clause."

"The second clause is the reason the first one will be refused, and I am not going to pretend otherwise." She put the pen down. "The fourth safeguard of the Hour Compact says that the city must record the physical toll of every calculation. It does not say how, and it does not say where, and the Toll Stair has carried minutes and districts since the beginning, and I have a binder of a hundred feet of the minutes. Nothing in this city records the other toll. The other toll is a person's attention, and it is recorded in a health form as *calibration strain*, which is a phrase with no fields in it, on a form that has no column for a name and no column for a duration and no column for the date it happened, and which is filled in for people in beds."

"And you want the columns."

"I want a name and a duration. That is the whole of it. Forty-three years of a Compact that says *record the physical toll* and a city that has spent forty-three years deciding that a person's lost attention is not a municipal asset." She picked the pen up again. "And I have to tell you three things about that before you put your name to it, because you are going to walk into a room in an hour and I am not going to have you walking in there with a clean heart."

"Right."

"One. It will be refused. Two. It will be refused with a date, and the date will be in the spring, and the refusal will be a real refusal, and I will have to help you draft the next one, and the next one, and the one after that. Three." She looked straight at him. "When it is published, some people are going to read their own intervals in a column with their name against them. Do you know how many people there are? I have a figure. There were sixty-one entries of calibration strain in the public-health committee's own files last year, and that is one committee's files, and the wards are a fraction of the city, and I have been unable to find a figure for the whole and I do not intend to invent one. Sixty-one people, in one set of papers, in a year. And you and I are going to stand in a room and propose to make that legible, and the women in those entries did not ask for it, and some of them will be furious, and some of them will not be alive to have an opinion."

"I know," Orrin said.

"Do you? Because there is a version of you that hears the words *name and duration* and thinks *a man in a well*, and there is a version that hears *we are putting sixty-one people's lost hours on a wall in a public building*."

"Both," said Orrin. "That's why I want the small clause in first."

She worked on it for a while, and then she read the draft back to him, and it was four hundred words and it did what he had wanted, and the fourth sentence of the second clause was a sentence she had put in on her own account about not being able to state a duration that no instrument had recorded, and he looked at that sentence for a long time and did not touch it, because she was right that it was the only honest way to write the demand: you cannot ask for a column and then fill in a number you made up.

"One more thing," she said, "and it is the only thing in this document I actually care about, and it is not in the document."

"Say it."

"Who pays for the record."

He looked up.

"Forty-three years ago somebody decided that the city should know what a Reading cost, and they did it out of a bucket and a piece of chalk, because the Cathedral pays for the chalk. Every time a clock is corrected by hand, a man from the works is paid by the works. Every time a line is transcribed, a clerk is paid by the Assembly. And if you put a name in a column and a duration in a column, then somebody has to read the forms, and copy the forms, and carry the forms, and store the forms, and answer for the forms, and that somebody is paid by — what, exactly? By the district whose clocks are drawn on? By the Assembly? By the people whose intervals are in it?" She put her hands flat. "That is the sentence that will kill this, and it is not an evil sentence, and it is not an excuse, and it is the sentence every sensible person in that room will be relieved to say out loud. I have watched it kill better petitions than this one. I wanted you to hear it from me first so that when you hear it from Councilor Rook you do not think you are being lied to."

---

The public-health committee room was on the first floor of the Rise, and it was smaller and colder than the Hall and had a gallery with nine chairs in it, four of which had anybody in them.

Orrin had never been in a committee room in his life. He found out in about four minutes what that was like. There was a long table, and there were eleven delegates, and there were papers, and every man and woman at that table had been elected by people and had a clerk and had spent a morning on the question, and he stood at the back in a shop coat with a blue-ink copy of the demand in his hand, and for the first time in his life had the distinct sensation of being a thing people were dealing with rather than a person they were talking to.

A man near the middle of the table found his place. Mara, behind him, said, "That's Councilor Imani Rook. Green Span, chair."

Rook was about forty, in a plain dark coat with her ward's badge on it, and she read the demand twice, all of it, including Mara's fourth sentence, and she did not interrupt once, and when she looked up she did not look at Orrin.

"This is from the Office of Public Reckoning," she said. "Seconded by a private citizen of the Clockwork Quarter. Is the private citizen a member of this committee?"

"No, madam."

"Then I am obliged to note that he has no standing before this committee, and that the Weirs, which are named in the second clause, have sent no delegate to speak to it, and that the Clockwork Quarter, which paid the three minutes in the first clause, has sent nobody at all." She set the paper down. "I am not being unkind. I am describing the room, because the room is the first clause of anything that happens in it."

"Ma'am, the Weirs have no standing in the civic model," Mara said, from the back. "That is the second clause."

"Yes," said Councilor Rook. "I have read it."

The arithmetic took her twenty minutes and she had it with her, which was the thing Orrin did not forget afterwards.

"Sixty-one calibration-strain entries in this committee's own files last year," she said. "From four wards. Four Green Span wards hold a hundred and ninety thousand people. The city holds two and a half million. If you scale sixty-one — and I say plainly that a linear scale is a crude instrument, and I have asked for the district returns to see whether it is a crude instrument — you are looking at figures in the low thousands every year. Low thousands of people who have lost an interval they cannot get back, printed with their names, in a book that a district's insurers and employers may lawfully consult." She looked up then, and at the table, not at him. "I have spent my career not causing panics. A panic in a ward is a real thing with real costs, and the costs fall on the people who cannot afford them, and they are not the people in the entries. I am not willing to hand this committee a bill for a stampede on the strength of a figure I have just admitted is crude, and I am not willing to do it on the strength of a *feeling* in a Weirs pump house, and I would say that to the Feeling in the Weirs Pump House if he were in front of me, which he is not, because he is not a member."

Several people at the table were nodding, and it was not a performance; it was four Green Span delegates who had read the paper and did the same arithmetic in their own heads, because it was easy arithmetic, and the arithmetic was not wrong, and Orrin found that he had no answer to it and had expected to have one.

"The first clause stands on its own," Mara said. "A line in the minutes. It costs a clerk's morning."

"It costs a clerk's morning every week of the year, and the clerk is a post, and posts are money, and the money is in the estimates, and there is no estimate for it, and therefore the answer to the question the investigator has just told me is *nobody pays for it, so no*." Rook turned a page. "I am going to move that the demand be **referred to committee**, which is what a referred demand does, and it does not die, and it is not the answer, and it will come back in the spring with a report and a number of witnesses, and I am telling you now that I will vote against it again in the spring unless somebody produces the district returns I have asked for, because a demand built on a guess is how this committee ends up burying a real thing under a bad reputation for four years."

Orrin felt the whole of the next thirty seconds happen in his chest.

*Referred to committee.* It was the phrase he had been told to want, by a woman who had told him to want it because it was better than the alternative, and it had come out of the chair's mouth in the tone of a woman doing her duty to a document, and it was going to cost him a spring.

"I have people in that ward," Rook said, and stopped, and put her hand flat on the paper for a moment. "That is not a figure and it is not evidence and I am aware of how it looks. I am telling you it is there so that nobody in this room believes I do not understand what is being asked. Now: is there anything the mover wishes to say before the question is put?"

Orrin stood up without meaning to, and had nothing, and Mara Venn, who had spent nine years in rooms like this one, put a hand flat on the back of his coat — a small pressure, and gone — and he sat down again.

The question was put. The division was taken. The demand was referred to committee, and the date of the return was named, and it was read twice and entered in the minutes, and the whole thing took nine minutes, and it was discharged.

In the gallery, a young man with a list of names on a board came down the row to fill it in, in the way of a man collecting a petition about a drain or a lamp, and he stopped at the fourth seat where a woman of about fifty-six in a good coat was sitting with her handbag on her knees.

"You signed a rail at the Cathedral last month," he said. "The third station. Can I put your name against a request for the committee to look at the witness stations? Only your name."

"I signed something," said Alma Festinger. "I was on the rota, so I was asked. Do you know what it was for?"

"That's what I wanted to ask, ma'am. Do you understand what you signed?"

"No," said Alma Festinger. "I did not understand a word of it, and the young man at the rail was very kind about the wrist band and I did not ask further, because I did not want to be a nuisance in a queue." She looked at the board. "I have been on that rota three years. Nobody has ever explained anything. Put my name down if it helps. I have a sister on the far side of Ferrywright and I would like somebody to look at something."

"You did understand what you signed, though," the young man said, half to himself, writing.

"I did not," she said. "I know that you are putting it down that I did not, and I would be obliged if you put that down as well, because I am not a fool and I do know the difference between a thing I have understood and a thing I have not, and I would not like to be on a list of people who pretend they know."

The young man put it down. Nobody followed it up. Nobody in that room had any standing to follow it up, and the man with the board was twenty-four, and the list went into a bundle with a lamp-post repair and two boundary alterations, and the woman in the good coat went out into the rain to a bus.

---

They took the Weirs tram at four, and the district was on time.

That was the sentence Orrin heard in every kitchen on the lane as they walked down it. *We're on time. We're on time, we fixed it Monday night, we're on time.* And the chandler's window said a quarter past two when the sky outside said one o'clock, and the nine of them who had held the rate and lost an hour of their Tuesday morning had it back, and the ward had its forty minutes back, and nobody in the district had any idea that those two things had been connected, and Orrin did not say so, because it was not his to say, and because Bevin Sarr said it to him first, in the lane, without being asked.

"You'll be at the hearing," Sel Kade said. She was standing in the doorway of the pumping ward office in a coat, going out, on her way to a tenants' meeting about a different drain. "The committee. You'll be there."

"I was this morning."

"You'll be there again in the spring." She had the manner of a woman who has been in a hundred of these rooms and has never once been on the side that gets recorded. "I want you to know that I have moved a pump reserve and I have not hidden it, and I did it in March, and it is written in the ward book, and any man may read the ward book. I moved it for seventy-one people and I would move it again, and if you put that in front of your committee you had better put the number of people whose kitchens would have been in the water as well, because the whole of this town is full of decisions like mine and there is not one of us who is a villain and about half of us are wrong."

"That's not a fair thing to ask a committee."

"No," said Sel Kade. "It isn't. Nobody has ever asked us for anything that was fair. It is simply the only thing I have got, and it is public now, and I have said it out loud in a public street to a man with a notebook, and if you want to use it, use it, and if you don't, I shall have said it anyway, which is what I actually wanted."

---

The bench at ten past nine, the last bell gone, the Quarter keeping its perfect time.

Orrin took the grey book down and turned to the page with the long entry in it, the one he had written in the small hours on Tuesday morning, and read it over, and it was all true, and it was a load report.

He took a fresh page, and ruled it the way he had ruled six years of pages, in the two columns he had always used: hours against work. Then he put the pencil on the first line and set down, in the second column, *17th, 20.00–04.30, Weir Two second stage, 8h30* — and stopped with the nib still on the paper, because that was how a man is entered on a shift board, and that was how the man had been entered on the guild's book for twenty years, and it was a line about hours, and he was trying to write a line about a man.

He tore the page out and started again without ruling it:

*Vance, Perry. 41.*

*Vance, Perry. 41. Weir Two, twenty years, unlicensed. Shuts things by hand and complains. Left the board saying hand shut. Went down at about ten on the seventeenth to shut a valve the clock said was shut, because the board said the valve was shut and he could hear the water, and because the bypass was running and he was the one who was there.*

*His last act was finishing a shift.*

*Ada, 13. Six o'clock tram.*

And he left the space under it for a duration, and put two words in it: *none recorded.*

Then he went back four pages to the entry of the seventeenth and copied the two numbers out, side by side, as they were on the two documents they had come from.

*Charged, on the Toll Stair, entered in the public record: 3 minutes. Clockwork Quarter. Every other clock in this district corrected itself by Friday morning and nobody in the Quarter lost a working minute.*

*Street clock, Halloway Street, found: 5 minutes slow. 2 of them charged to nobody, drawn from the same reserve, appearing in no receipt, no entry, no hand's writing, and not knowable from any document in this city.*

*Monday night, the trestle: the district's morning an hour short. Eleven people, a ward, forty minutes, two hours unbudgeted. Nobody charged. Nobody.*

He looked at the two lines for a long time.

The second line was longer than the first by a factor he could not put a number on, because the second line contained people and the first line contained a figure, and there was no arithmetic in the world that would turn a person's missing attention into a number of minutes, and everybody in Morrowfen from the Cathedral down to the chandler's window believed the conversion was straightforward because that was the founding promise: *the city will calculate the price of a decision so that you do not have to guess it in private.*

It was the promise. It was forty-three years old and it was on a stone lintel in letters a foot high, and Orrin had been selling it, in his small way, with a shop sign and a clean certificate, for his whole life.

Under the two lines, in a hand that got smaller as it went, he put this:

*The three and the five do not agree, and the difference is not mine and is not anybody's and is not on any document. 3 is what was charged. 5 is what it cost. I cannot account for the 2, and I have asked four people, and the answer in every case was that a hand correction is not a toll. So either the stair is wrong, or the city has been correcting its own clocks out of somewhere it has never written down, and I know which of those I believe, and I am writing it down here because I am the only ledger in this city that nobody can read.*

Then he turned back three pages, to the entry he had made in the small hours of Tuesday on the trestle, and read the last line of it again, because it was the first rule he had ever set himself and he wanted it where he could not get at it.

*Nothing of mine is used again without its blind margin and its toll named in public, in a room, to somebody who can object.*

He read it twice, and thought it was probably too high a standard for a man who kept the only copy of it in a shop with a lease, and then he copied it out again at the top of a clean page, because a rule that lives at the back of a book is not a rule, it is a note.

He shut the book.

Behind it, on the shelf, in the dark, in its cloth, was a governor with a dent in its flank and a pin engaged in travel, eighteen years old, in no book anywhere in the city.

The letter from the hall had come on the noon post. It was courteous. It was four lines long and it was signed by the guild's secretary, and it had come to a man whose licence to certify anything at all would come up for renewal in about eight weeks at the discretion of the man who had sent it.

*The hall keeps a certification ledger, which is a record of machines. The hall does not keep a record of hours for work that is not part of a certified shift, there being no such hours, there being no such shifts. The hall is asked to note that form GH-9 is a request form and not a record, and that the hall's price schedules are the hall's own property.*

He read it four times. It was, as far as he could see, entirely true; that was the trouble with it. Somewhere in the building on Verge Lane there was a book with a column of dates and works and hours and a line in a copying clerk's hand, and the line said *Weir Two, night pumping, non-certified standby, one crew, three nights*, and the reason there was no labour ledger was that the hall did not consider those hours to have been worked, and the hall was not wrong, and the man whose name was in the ninth line had not been paid for any of them.

Orrin put the kettle on, and looked at the case, and thought that there was a form number in a guild series in a price schedule in a book on a table in the hall on Verge Lane, and a batch of forty-one entries, and a warden who had signed it at twenty-six, and a woman on a rota who had said *no, I did not* and had it written down, and a district with no standing, and a ward with forty minutes and no column, and a man in a well with a bar in his hands, and a piece of paper, somewhere, on a bench, in a book nobody could read, that was the only copy of any of it.

Eight weeks. He could wait eight weeks, or he could not.

He took out the grey book again, and turned to a clean page, and set the number of the form at the top of it in a hand that did not shake, because it was the first time in three weeks that his hand had been entirely steady.
