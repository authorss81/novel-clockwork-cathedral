# Chapter 11 — The Honest Man With a Form

He had been putting it off for two days, which in a shop with a lease was the beginning of a habit, and the reason he gave himself on the walk down the slipway was that a quarterly inspection was a quarterly inspection and there had been a man in a well.

The rain had stopped. The Lower Weirs smelled the way it always smelled after a wet week, of tar and low water and the particular sourness that eight inches of river gets into brickwork, and the slipway was shining, and there were four men outside the Weir Two pump house at ten to ten in the morning on a working day, which meant one of them was not getting paid for being there.

"—that's him," said Bevin Sarr. "Gauge man. Office."

She said it the way she said everything now, flat and short, as though words were a currency she had stopped trusting. She was in her coat with the board-hook over her shoulder and there was chalk on the side of her right hand, and the pump house door stood open behind her, and a man was standing just inside it in a good wool coat with a leather case in his hand and his hat still on.

"Orrin Vale," he said, before Orrin had got up the last three steps. "Inspector Rill. Continuity Office, hourlines. You're the maintainer for this building."

"I am."

"May I?" He lifted the case a little. "I'll want the door open for about an hour and I'd rather ask than turn up a second time."

It was the wrong weather for politeness and he did it anyway, and Orrin found, walking in behind him, that this was a thing that made him suspicious in a way he could not immediately justify, because a man who asks has nothing to hide and a man who does not ask usually has a form.

The pump house was warmer than it had been on the seventeenth and it smelled of tea and hot oil, and the second stage was running, and the hand valve was shut with a white paint mark on the spindle that had been repainted since, because somebody had been painting over it. Bevin Sarr had repainted it. Orrin noticed and did not say.

Rill took his hat off, which nobody in Morrowfen does in a pump house, and set it on the bench, and opened the case.

The instrument inside was not a repairman's gauge. It was a squat brass thing the shape of a flat-iron with a dial on the top and a folding handle, and there was a small glass window in the side of it, and the whole of it weighed about four pounds, and the case had a lining of oiled cloth with shaped holes in it, so that the owner of the case had bought the case for this one instrument and nothing else. Orrin knew what it was. It read an hourline, and it read it without touching the machine, and it was the only reason a class-one inspector could stand in a pump house in the rain and certify a line he was not certified to open.

"You'll know this," Rill said. "Half the hall has one and half the hall pretends it does."

"Quarterly?"

"Quarterly, same week, every district with an hourline on the schedule. I did the Quarter on Tuesday, which is why your form came to your bench, and I'm in the Weirs until Friday, and the Weirs are the last stop because they're the furthest and there's nobody here at a quarter past four."

He said it as a joke and it was very slightly a joke, and Orrin did not laugh, and Rill noticed that he had not laughed and did not try again.

---

He worked the way a man works who has been doing it for nineteen years, which was to say that he did not explain.

He had Orrin stand at the far end of the building while he read the line at the junction box on the works wall, so that Orrin could watch the dial and not the man. He read it four times, at four different seconds, and wrote each one in a hardback notebook with a printed head and ruled columns, and the printing said, in small letters at the top of every page, *regional water and power scheduling*, and under that, in a heavier weight, *hourline inspection*, and under that a space for a date and a space for a building and four columns, and Orrin could see from the doorway that the first column was headed *time* and the second *line* and the third *reading* and the fourth *within tolerance*.

That fourth column was the whole of the Office's authority, and it was four inches wide.

"Eleven forty-one," Rill said. "North pump house spur, Weir Two. Reading four one eight. That's within, and it's within comfortably, and I'll want to read it twice more before I sign, because that's the practice and because I would rather you saw me do it twice than take my word for it once."

He read it twice more and wrote all three down and then, on the fourth line, in the same hand, he wrote a word that made Orrin come three steps further into the building.

*Consistent.*

"Consistent with what?" Orrin said.

"Consistent with itself, and with the schedule it was last surveyed on, and with the last three visits." He closed the notebook and did not look up. "I do not certify that the district's clocks show the right time, Mr Vale. I certify that the line agrees with its own record. Those are two different questions and only one of them is mine, and the one that is mine is the one with a column."

That was true, and Orrin had no argument against it, and it took him the better part of a minute to work out why he was standing in a warm room feeling like a man who has been left out of his own trade.

He said it out loud, because Leda had made him say things out loud and it had begun to work on him the way a bad habit works on you.

"The chandler's window on Weir Lane," he said. "Eight minutes fast on Sunday morning and eleven by the evening. I put it right with a bar in about an hour and nobody was charged for it. Your gauge says the line's consistent."

"My gauge says the line's consistent."

"And the line is eleven minutes out, and the eleven minutes came from a hand correction, and a hand correction is not a fault and there is no column for it."

"No," said Rill. "There isn't."

---

He asked his question at a quarter past eleven, and he asked it the way he did everything else, which was as a formality he had asked four hundred times and expected to ask four hundred more.

"Complement of the shift on the night of the seventeenth. Certified men, by the hourline works' return."

Bevin Sarr answered it from the step. "Four."

"Four is what the return says. I have the return." Rill wrote it down. "Four, and the district's own figure is four, and that's the finding, and that's a clean one."

"There's five on the board," Orrin said.

Rill did not look up. He put the pen on the column and then picked it up again, which was the first thing Orrin had seen him do that was not part of a procedure.

"Where's the board?"

"Behind you."

He turned round. It was four feet by three, hung where the old plan used to hang, and it was covered in chalk in two hands, and the top line said, in different chalk, *board is ours. board is not the works'.*

Rill looked at it for about as long as a man looks at a thing he has decided not to understand on purpose, and then he said, "I can see it's five."

"You can see it's five."

"I can read a number off a board." He turned back. "Mr Vale, I'll explain what happens if I put that on this form, and then you can decide whether you want me to, and I would rather do it slowly because I know what you're asking me for and I would like to be understood before I refuse it."

"Please."

"The form asks me for the certified complement of a shift at a named hour. That's the question and it's got a column and I can answer it: four, from the works' return, which the works signed." He held the notebook up. "Now. If I also record that a board in the building shows five, then the form carries a discrepancy, and a discrepancy has to be a discrepancy *between two things of the same kind* — a return against a return, a certified entry against a certified entry, so that an office can weigh one against the other and say which is wrong. That is the only arithmetic this column can do. A guild labour book and a piece of painted board are not two of a kind. One has a form number, a countersignature and an office that is obliged to receive it. The other has a woman and a piece of chalk and nowhere at all to send it. So the moment I write your five on this line, the form stops being able to say which one is wrong, and it becomes a form with a sentence on it, and a sentence is not a finding."

"What if you wrote it as an enclosure?"

Rill looked at him properly for the first time.

"Then it goes into the file, unattached, and nobody reads the file. Not nobody in this office — nobody anywhere. A file in this building is a thing that is carried up a stair on the first of the month and put in a room the public may read the index of." He shut the case. "I have read those files. I have written in them. There is a great deal in them about the weight of a casing and the depth of a bedplate and not one word in the last nine years about who was in a building at three in the morning, because there is no form on which anybody has ever written that, and a piece of paper with no form under it goes into the drawer and stays there and is not a lie and is not a record. It is furniture."

Nobody said anything. Outside, one of the four men who was not getting paid for it laughed at something and was told by another one to shut up.

"And the fifth name," Orrin said. "The one that isn't certified."

"Cannot go on the form at all," said Rill, "and that is the part I find I cannot make people hear. I can only enter a man as a certified man. That boy in the district who sits nights and has never held a certificate — if I enter him, I am certifying a man who does not exist in the terms of the form, and my name is on the page, and my licence is my own, and I have held it for eleven years. If I leave him off, the return is correct. Five is not a form of the discrepancy, Mr Vale. The fifth name is the only reason the form is wrong, and it is the only one I have no way of writing down, and if I could write it down you would not need me."

---

He made his finding at twenty past twelve, sitting on the bench with his hat on his knee, and he wrote it out in full before he showed it to anybody, which Orrin noticed, and which was the first thing that told him what kind of man this was.

It read:

> *Weir Two floodgate and second-stage pumping station, Lower Weirs. Hourline spur north, read at 11.41, 12.06 and 12.19 hours. Readings consistent and within tolerance on all three. Complement of certified shift, night of the 17th, per works' return: four.*
>
> *No shift record exists for the Weir Two night crew. There is no hour, room or form in this Office's custody by which a complement other than the certified complement can be established, and no such form is held by any other office. Nothing to certify. File closed.*

He read it back to the room and then said the part that was not on the form, and he said it without looking at Orrin, in the voice of a man delivering a conclusion he has checked twice.

"Mr Vale asked me this morning whether I could put five on that instead of four. I cannot. I have been asked by a district for a record for twenty years — I use *they* loosely and I do not know who — and what I have to tell you is that the absence is not a hole. A hole you can point at. This is not a hole. There is no office in Morrowfen that holds the names of men who were not on a certified shift, and there is not supposed to be, and the reason there is not supposed to be is that a man who is not on a certified shift is not, in the terms of this city's paperwork, a person who was at work. He is a man who was somewhere."

"I know he was somewhere," Orrin said.

"I know you do. I am not explaining the law to you, Mr Vale, I am explaining my column to you, and there is a difference, and the difference is the only thing I have to give anybody." He put the notebook in the case and shut it. "If you want a record of that night, you want a person who can sign one. You need a name, and a signature, and an office that is obliged to receive it, and a form number. And there is nobody in this district who can sign one, and I cannot manufacture a person from an office on the Rise with a gauge in a leather case."

Then he said the thing that Orrin took up the hill with him and did not put down for four days.

"One more thing, and it is not about this building. Everything the Office does is in a file. Inspections, transfers out of the open record, deferrals — anything a maintenance item gets put off for, with a date and a price on it. That is the Office's business; if the Office did not keep it, the Office would be guessing, and I have seen what happens in other cities when a regulator guesses. It is public on petition. Anybody may ask, everybody may read, and reading takes eleven days, because the box on the second floor is emptied once a day at noon and not at all on a Sunday, and the count runs from the day after it goes in, and the day after happens to be the one that costs you."

"Who reads them?" Orrin said.

"Lawyers, mostly. And me."

He got his hat and went out into a wet afternoon, and Bevin Sarr watched him down the slipway from the step, and then she said, without any particular heat in it at all:

"He was honest."

"I know."

"You can always tell. It's the ones that are honest that get you." She went back in. "I've had four of them in twenty years and they've all been right about everything and it has never once made a difference, and I'd rather a thief than another one of those."

---

Orrin put his case down on the bench at half past four and the brass bearing weight was sitting on an envelope with no stamp on the corner of it and no letter in it.

It was not a letter. It was a printed card, hall stock, the guild's own mark in the corner and a rule across the top and a form number in the corner, and it had the particular weight of a thing that has been printed in several thousand and sent to two hundred and forty men.

> *NOTICE OF ANNUAL RENEWAL — MAINTAINER'S LICENCE, CLASS THREE.*
>
> *Vale, Orrin. Bench: two benches, Verge Lane, Clockwork Quarter.*
> *Current licence expires: [the nineteenth of the month after next]*
> *Renewal fee: 4s 6d.*
> *Endorsement required: one (1) master, Guild of Horologists, Clockwork Quarter.*
> *Enclosure: work book, hall form W-4, current issue, to be lodged with the fee.*

He read it four times. The renewal was about eight weeks off, which was not the same as the renewal being near, and he made himself be precise about that, because he had spent a life being precise about things and had never once been thanked for it and did not intend to stop.

Under the renewal line, in a smaller type, the standing request:

> *A maintainer shall keep a work book of faults observed and work performed, in the hall's form, current issue, and shall produce it on request. Holders who maintain a private record of faults are requested to lodge the private record with the hall for transcription, or to produce the hall form W-4 completed from it, at their own cost, at the time of renewal.*

*Private record of faults.* He looked across the bench at the shelf behind him, at the dark, where a school exercise book with a hard grey cover had lived for six years, and where, behind it, in a guild cloth, a regulator with a dent in its flank had been standing in the dark since the sixteenth of the month.

Four shillings and sixpence, about eight weeks, one master's signature, and a book he had been keeping since he was twenty-six, in his own hand, in a shop with a lease, that had never been entered in anything and was not the hall's property and could not be produced to anybody and had never once been asked for by anybody in six years.

He put the card in the out-tray with the certificates.

Then he took it out again and put it on the bench with the other things he was working on, because the man who set it there had not put it in with the certificates, and it had not come with the post, and that was the entire difference between it and every other piece of paper in the building, and he was not going to be the man who filed it wrong.
