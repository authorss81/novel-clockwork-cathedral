# Chapter 65 — A Return Is a List of Properties

On the Thursday evening of the twenty-eighth of the seventh month a clockmaker with no licence sat on a bench in Verge Lane and wrote four pages in a grey book for nobody, and the four pages are the whole argument in the order it actually runs, and the first of them is the sentence he has been walking towards since the eleventh of the seventh month.

> *A pumping district has no standing. It therefore has no return. It therefore has no way of knowing what its own water does.*

That is the whole of it and it took him six months and two documents to get to, and it is on the first page, and the rest of the four pages is what the sentence costs.

---

**Page one, and the four places.**

A district's water is written down in four places and they are four miles apart and there is no form in this city that connects any two of them.

> *One. In the head of an engineer's assistant who is fifty-eight and has held a title this building has never put on a door for eleven years. She reads a gauge at half past two in the morning and she rang a room in the fifth month and it is in a book, and she was wrong the following week, on the page, in her own hand, at half past nine in the evening, and being wrong on the page is the most useful thing in the district.*
> *Two. In a night book in a pump house, which is not a record of the guild and not a record of the works, and which is now nine pages for the fifth month and six for the sixth and one for this.*
> *Three. In chalk on a board eleven years old on a wall in the same pump house, which nobody may take down, and which has no column for a name and never has.*
> *Four. On a printed sheet dated 1919, reprinted eleven times, with a fourth window added to it in 1951 and no name and no minute, nailed behind a ladder in a works' yard, and copied onto a shelf above a barometer in a room over a shop, where it will be until a woman stands down on the first of the ninth.*

Then the sentence that made him put the pen down for ten minutes.

> *I have got six figures from the ninth of the sixth in a notebook and in six lines of chalk, and the six figures are the only measurement of anything in this district that anybody has ever taken, and **they are the only ones, and the reason is not that nobody thought of it. The reason is that the four places are four places and a document cannot be a party, and a district is not a body, and so there is nothing for a figure to be about in a way that an office has to receive.**
> *I have been looking for a form. There is no form. There is a great many form.*

---

**Page two, and the turn, and it is the reason he is going to be wrong about something for the rest of his life.**

> *I do not need a Reading.*
> *I have wanted a Reading since the fifteenth of the first month. I want a question put about that branch by somebody with standing, and I have wanted it every day since a stranger stood on the Cathedral steps and asked me how many more and I said I don't know, and a Reading is the only instrument in this city that a room believes.*
> *And I have been asking the wrong question for five months, and the wrong question is *how does a district get a Reading*, and the answer to that is a body and an instrument and a person and there are three of them and two of them are a body, and there is no body.*
> ***The right question is: how does a district get a return.***
> *Because a return is the thing that makes a rate. A return is a list of properties and of the persons chargeable in respect of them, and the ward's water rate is made on it, and the water act does not know a district exists and knows every property in it, and a pumping district has properties and is not on the list.*
> *So if the district made its own return — if the ward's office compiled the return for the ward, out of the ward's own book and the ward's own gauge sheets and the ward's own night book and the four standpipes and the eleven miles of main and the six clocks and the two stations — **then that return would be a document, and the district would have lodged it, and a district that has lodged a document is a party, and a standing is a body.***
> *That is it. That is the whole of it. **I have been trying to get the district into a room. The way into the room is the rate book, and the rate book is the return, and I have been standing in a queue at the wrong counter for five months and there was a counter in the same building I have been going to twice a month since the sixth of the sixth that would have told me this in four minutes and did not, because a girl of twenty-six told me what a return *was* when I asked her what a standing was, and I did not hear her, and she was not wrong, and she was extremely kind about it.**

---

**Page three, and the price, and he does not flinch from it and he does not answer it either.**

> *A return has to be true. That is the condition and it is not a technicality. A return that is not true is not a return, it is a complaint.*
> *And a true return is rateable. That is what a return is for.*
> *So: if this district makes its own return, then this district's water is rated on its own account, and the rate is a shilling and tenpence in the pound and not nine, and the base is forty-eight thousand pounds, and that is two thousand six hundred pounds on the ward, and thirteen shillings a year on a rated house of twelve pounds. It is a number I have had since the fourth month of this year and it has a notice with it, and the notice carries a number and no name.*
> *And the whole of the increase lands on the people whose hours are not in it. Not on a works. On a laundry on Bellweather Road with thirty-one women in it, and on a gate at Weir One, and on about a hundred and forty households in tenement basements off Weir Lane.*
> *I am going to write the two objections down here in the words they were said to me, and I am not going to answer either of them, because I have not got an answer and because a man who answers an objection in his own ledger has answered it to himself.*
> *B. Crane, the eleventh of the sixth: if I send that form in with the book then I am telling the hall what thirty-one women are, and there is no column on that form for what they are, and it will go in a drawer and be a number in a drawer.*
> *I. Pike, the eleventh of the sixth: then the pump ward is a place the model can see, and there is a form on which a station on the witness rail takes a person of the district, and if the hours go in the return then there is somebody to put there, and I have wanted that since January and I have wanted it more than I have wanted my own back.*
> *Both of them are right. Both of those were said in a room over a shop to eleven people and neither of those women would choose and both of them have refused to choose four times this year.*
> *I have left one figure out of this page. I am not going to say which and I am not going to explain why, and a man who explains a rule to himself in a ledger is a man who has already broken it by accident.*

---

**Page four, and it is the sentence that turns the argument inside out, and he wrote it at about ten past eleven at night and then did not sleep for a while.**

> *A return is a list of properties.*
> *A property has an address.*
> ***Therefore a return is a list of addresses, and a list of addresses is a list of routes, and a list of routes is a list of visits, and a person decides which door gets a knock, and the person who is on the list does not get to say no, because the person on the list is the person being knocked on, and that is the definition of it.***
> *Mr Arden has been making that argument since the fifteenth of the sixth month in a room over a shop and he has been right every time he has made it, and I have never once been able to beat it, and on the fourth page of a book in a shop in Verge Lane, on the twenty-seventh of the seventh month, his argument stopped being an argument and became a document.*
> *And I have not asked him. He does not know. He is twenty years old and he has an argument and a list of eleven items in his pocket and he has never seen a return, and what I have done is take the thing he has been saying for six weeks and put it in a form with a heading on it, and **a thing with a heading on it is a thing an office can be sent, and a thing an office can be sent is a thing an office can refuse, and I do not know whether that is better or worse and I have not asked anybody and I am the only person in this city who can be asked, because I wrote it.**
> *The answer to a return is a rate, and the answer to a rate is a notice with a number and no name, and the answer to a notice with a number and no name is a district that cannot be asked, and the whole of the loop is six sentences long and I have known every one of them since the fourth month.*

---

**He went to the mutual on the Friday morning, and it is the only part of that week that happened outside a shop.**

The **Bellweather and Weir Lane sick and burial mutual** was founded in 1889, has about nine members, keeps a book, and meets when there is a drain — which is not a figure of speech and has never been one, because in a district where the lowest floor is a cellar, a drain that backs up onto a footway is a funeral expense, and the only time nine people can agree to be in one room is when there is a reason to be in the same street.

It had met on the Wednesday the twenty-seventh, because a drain on Weir Lane between the second court and Bellweather had come up over the flags on the Sunday and had been down again by the Tuesday and had come up twice, and the clerk of the works had said it was a matter for the landlord, and it was not.

He got there at half past nine and was not let in for an hour and forty minutes, and he stood in a passage, and at about eleven he was asked into a kitchen and given a cup of tea by a woman of fifty-eight called **Adela Marrow**, who has kept the mutual's book for nineteen years, and who put the book on the table closed and put her hand flat on it.

She was extremely fair. She gave him the argument in four sentences, and the argument is the same one Ambrose gave him from a works' counter and the same one Ivo Pell gave him on a railway platform, and neither of those two men ever said it better.

"A mutual is not a body," said Adela Marrow. "A mutual is nine people who have agreed to bury each other, and that is the whole of what we have ever been, and if we lodge the first instrument in this district then we are the district's representative in a room where the district is not allowed to speak, and in about four years there will be a form with our name at the top of it and a question on it that we did not agree to, and we cannot refuse it, because refusing it is a thing you do when you are a party and we would be a party."

"That is the reason I was given before I came," said Orrin Vale.

"Then you were given it straight, which is more than I get from anybody, and it is still not the reason."

He waited.

"You have not asked us what we want," said Adela Marrow. "You have asked us for a piece of paper. Those are not the same thing and you know they are not the same thing, because **you have spent six months standing in rooms telling anybody who would listen that a form is not a room, and you have just come into a kitchen and asked nine people for a form, and you did it in the same voice.**"

Somebody at the far end of the table said, "That is the whole of it."

"It is not the whole of it," said Adela Marrow. "The whole of it is that we would have said yes in about 1931 and we would have said no in 1949, and in between those two years this street lost a hundred and thirty households and nobody asked us at all, and I have not got the energy left to be the thing that gets asked now that it is finally the sort of thing that could be asked."

**They said no in four minutes. Nobody was angry and nobody thanked him and the book stayed shut.**

And then Adela Marrow took her hand off it and opened it, and turned back about forty leaves, and put a sheet on the table, and it is the thing the chapter is actually for.

> *Every household entered in this mutual, with the number of the property as the water authority has it, the address, and the date the service pipe was last looked at.*

**Twenty-six households.** Nine members, twenty-six households, and every one of them with an address the water authority uses, and a date against each service pipe going back to about 1931 in about eleven hands, and four entries where somebody died and the pipe was never looked at again.

"There is our return," said Adela Marrow. "That is what we have got. Not a list of properties for a rate book, because we are not a ratepayer and the water authority does not know we exist. **Twenty-six addresses and twenty-six dates and a name against each date, and it is the only list in this district that has ever been kept by the people it is about, and it is in a kitchen, and if I die it goes in a box.**"

---

He got back to Verge Lane at about half past one on the Friday and he did three things in the next two hours, and two of them were mistakes and one of them was right, and the right one is the only one of the three that will still matter in four years.

**The first thing was that he wrote the twenty-six addresses into the grey book**, on a page of its own, in a column, with the dates, because that is what he is, and because a man who has been handed the only list in a district and does not write it down is a man who has decided it is worth more unwritten.

**The second thing was that he did not use it.** Not that night, not on the Saturday, and not since. It is a list of twenty-six doors on two streets, and it would take a man about two hours, and on page four of a book written the night before he had written out in his own hand that a list of addresses is a list of routes and that a person decides which door gets a knock and the person on the list does not get to say no.

So he wrote under the column, in the same pen, the sentence, and the sentence is the reason the book is worth opening:

> *Twenty-six addresses and twenty-six dates. I have not used them and I am not going to, and I am writing that down on the day I was given them and not on the day I decided, because a man decides on the day he decided and would put that date instead, and then in four years it would read as a decision and not as a promise.*
> *If a person in this city ever knocks on twenty-six doors on those two streets, with that column in his hand, then that is not a district deciding something. That is one man in a shop deciding which door gets a knock, and the twenty-six cannot refuse, and every house on those two streets that is not in the column will find out afterwards from a neighbour.*
> *That is the argument. It is Mr Arden's argument and it has been his since the fifteenth of the sixth and it was never once wrong, and it is now a document, and the document is mine, and **I am the person who decides whether the document is a list of routes, and I have decided, this afternoon, on my own, with nobody able to say no, that it is a list of dates.***

**The third thing was that he went and found Teo Arden, and he did not tell him about the return, and he told him about the twenty-six addresses, and Teo Arden did not say anything for about a minute.**

"You have asked a mutual," said Teo Arden.

"I have asked a mutual."

"You were told to ask a mutual. He told you to ask a mutual and to take the worst one and not to go back twice in a fortnight, and I know he told you because I know him." He did not say it bitterly. "Which one."

"The Bellweather and Weir Lane."

"That is the worst one," said Teo Arden. "That is the one that has been asked three times in nine years by three different men in three different buildings and has said no three times in about four minutes, and I know that because I have been in that kitchen."

"They said no," said Orrin Vale, "and they gave me twenty-six addresses with a date against each service pipe going back to 1931, and it is the only list in this district that has ever been kept by the people it is about, and I am not going to use it, and I am telling you about it because the argument about lists of addresses is yours and it has been yours since June and I have just turned it into a document without asking you, and that is the second time this year."

Wenna Pruitt was on the landing. She had come down to see what the shouting was and there was no shouting, and she stood in the doorway with a book under her arm and listened to the whole of the last part of it, which nobody stopped her from doing and which nobody told her she could not do.

"Then that is three," she said.

"That is three," said Orrin Vale.

"And you have not asked him whether to publish it," said Wenna Pruitt. "You have asked him whether to knock on it. Those are not the same question and I want it noticed that you asked the second one, and I want it noticed by both of you, and I am not settling anything and neither of you is settling anything and we said in February we would not be the same arithmetic in a year and it is not a year."

"**It is not the same arithmetic**," said Teo Arden, "and I have not said it is. And we are not going to be the same arithmetic by having a conversation about it, and I have got about four things this month and none of them is a conversation, and one of them is the seventeenth of the eighth, and I am not telling anybody else what it is, and I am not going to be anybody's anything, and you—" to Orrin Vale "—are not going to be able to get a question put about that branch by writing it down twice more."

Then he went up, and the two of them did not follow him, which is what they always do, and the disclosure argument stood in a doorway in a Weirs landing on the Friday afternoon of the twenty-ninth of the seventh month exactly where it had stood in June, with one more document on the floor of it, and **both of them still right, and neither of them going to choose, and it is eight months away from a February.**

---

**And on the Sunday evening the thirty-first of the seventh month he wrote the last page of the four, and it is the shortest thing in the book, and it is the question the whole of this volume turns on, and he has not answered it and has written down that he cannot.**

> *A return is a list of properties. A standing is a body. A body is what lodges the instrument and the instrument is what puts a person of the district on a rail in a room.*
> *I have found the chain and every link in it is a document and not one of the documents is a person.*
> *And here is where I stop, and I am writing down that I stop, and I am not writing down a reason that would let me start again on a bad morning.*
> *I cannot write the form. Not because of the price. **Because I do not know what a body that is not a body signs with.** A council signs with a minute book and a seal it does not have. A mutual signs with a kitchen table. A man signs with his own name, which is why I have one and why a form I filled in myself in May is a document and not a party, and I have been going round in circles since the eleventh of the seventh and the circle is this: the instrument requires a body and there is no body and the only candidate in this district with four pounds ten in a year has a room over a chandler's shop, a minute book nobody can produce in a court, and a two-year habit of refusing on purpose to be a single thing.*
> *There is no form. I have looked. And on Tuesday the eleventh of the seventh a girl of twenty-six at a counter told me the three requirements in eleven minutes and the first of them was a body, and she was not wrong, and she could not have helped, and I have written this page out four times this month and I have got to the same wall four times.*
> *The next thing is not mine.*
