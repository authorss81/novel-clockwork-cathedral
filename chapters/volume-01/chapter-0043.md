# Chapter 43 — A Rate, and a Card

The card came back on the Friday the twelfth of the fifth month, eleven days after the form went in, and it was the four lines, and it was the four lines in the hall's own type, and he had written them out in longhand in the grey book on the Saturday before last as a thing he knew would happen, word for word, with the emphasis where the emphasis goes.

> *The hall does not keep a record of hours for work that is not part of a certified shift, there being no such hours, there being no such shifts.*

Della Marsh put it on the counter with a book under it, the way she puts everything down, and said, "That is the answer to your request and it is the standard answer and it went out at nine this morning, and there is no second card coming, because there is nothing else there is."

"You told me on the fourth of the fourth month," said Orrin Vale, "in a room over a boat-builder's shop, in about the words I have got in that book."

"I did not tell you anything, Mr Vale." She had her hand flat on the book. "I have never in my life said anything to a maintainer on this counter. I have a drawer with four hundred and something in it and about four of them go out a year and any of them will tell you the same four lines whatever the request is, and if a man can guess them in advance then the guesser is a very good guesser and I am not going to be the one to tell him so."

She was right. He had not told her on the counter and she had not told him in the room; Sel Kade had told him, in about ninety seconds, with a sluice key on the table, and it had taken him four days to get here and a fortnight to work out what it was.

He stood at the counter and understood the whole of it, and it is worth setting down in the order he understood it, because he is not a quick man about this kind of thing and it took him the better part of twenty minutes standing up.

For one year, from the first of the fifth month, forty-one heads are in the Lower Weirs pumping district's standing, in the ordinary class, at the guild's price of two shillings the head, and the hall keeps a record of machines and does not keep a record of hours for work that is not part of a certified shift. Those forty-one heads are now part of a certified shift, because that is what a reinstatement in a form series does, and therefore for one year and for those forty-one heads the four lines on the card are not true.

And nobody in the hall has noticed, and nobody in the hall will ever notice, because the four lines are true of every other site in the building and the hall does not check.

So a warden's condition in a room over a boat-builder's shop, in a conversation about a signature eighteen years old, is now in force. A form can be a lie by being true of everything except the one case nobody tests. He has paid four pounds and two shillings of his own money to make a hall form say the opposite of what the hall says about the subject, and it will do that for a year, and the hall will go on sending out the same card to everybody else, and at the end of the year the crew will fall out of the standing again on the day the form runs out, without a hearing and without a vote, because that is what a form with a year on it does.

He wrote all of it in the grey book that night, in the plainest words he could find, and then he read it back, and the last line of the entry is the one he underlined and did not soften.

> *Fifth month, the twelfth. Four pounds two shillings, paid, and it has bought one year in which the hall's own answer is not true of forty-one named heads, and no person in this city can be made to notice, including me, on any day that is not the day the form expires.*

That is not nothing. He sat with it for a while and let it be not nothing. It is a year in which the Weirs' night crew exist in the civic model in writing. It is not a shift and it is not a wage and it will not bring back eleven years and it will not give anybody back one minute of a lost afternoon, and when the form runs out in the fifth month next year it will stop, and nobody will be in a room when it stops.

He did not write that down. He wrote the first four lines and he underlined the last one and he shut the book, which is a thing he has done about four times in two thousand pages.

The rate notice went up on the ward board outside the Weir Two pump house on the Friday afternoon, and it went up on the same board as the schedule notice with the clerk's sentence on it, and it is a single side, and it is a form.

> *WARD WATER RATE, LOWER WEIRS PUMPING. For the rate year next: one shilling and tenpence in the pound.*
> *For the rate year last: nine pence in the pound.*
> *The return on which this rate is based has been corrected. A ratepayer may see the corrected return at the ward office on any weekday between ten and four. Objections in writing to the ward clerk by the thirtieth of the month.*
>
> *Rateable value of the ward, £48,000. Increase, one shilling and a penny in the pound. **£2,600.***

It is a very good notice. The base is printed because the ordinance requires the base to be printed, and the objection window is generous, and a ratepayer may see the return, and the return is the corrected one, and the correction is the district works' own reconciliation note and it is in a file on the canal road and anybody may read it.

It is a piece of paper with a number on it and no name. That is what it is. It is a number on a board, and the number is right, and the return it is based on is right, and the reconciliation that produced the return is right, and the nine pence that the ward paid for eleven years was nine pence on a certified return that said the ward had no hours in it, and a ward with no hours in it costs nothing to run, and the ward has been running two jobs off one tank since the fifteenth of the third month.

Nobody in the Lower Weirs knows that a man in Verge Lane paid four pounds and two shillings on Monday the first for the standing that caused it. Nobody in the Lower Weirs will ever know. He bought the standing for a year and the standing is the reason the return was nil, and correcting the return takes the standing back out, and the rate goes up one shilling and a penny in the pound, and the rate falls on a rated house of twelve pounds as thirteen shillings a year, and it falls on the pumping district and on the ward and on the same people the crew stands on, and the only man in this city who could put the two documents on the same table is the man who put one of them there with his own money and told nobody.

On a rated house of twelve pounds it is thirteen shillings a year, which is a hundred and fifty-six pence, which is nearly twice the eighty-two shillings that the form cost him on the Monday, and the form is the cheaper of the two, and both of those figures were correct when they were written down and neither of them is anybody's fault.

The insurance company wrote to Hanna Stroud on the Friday and it took two letters to say what one line had done, because the first one was a form.

Her schedule of premiums on nine blocks has been under review since the week the district works' records were classified, and the review has now come back, and it has not refused her, and it has not declined to renew, and it has done something worse and more ordinary than either.

Where the returns are produced, the nine blocks are assessed at **£512 a year**. Where the returns are not produced, the nine blocks cannot be assessed on their own history and go onto the district's blanket scale, and the district's blanket scale is **£796 a year**. The difference is two hundred and eighty-four pounds. The word in the second paragraph is *unavailable*, and it is the same word as in the fourth month, and there is a footnote under it that says the source records for the Lower Weirs pumping district are held under a classification and are not obtainable by the insurer or by the ratepayer, and the footnote is in six-point type and is the only honest sentence in the letter.

And the letter does not say who to write to.

There is no appeal in it. There is no address. It is a letter that has taken a woman's nine blocks and moved them from a schedule to a scale, and the woman has nineteen years of rent books and a Michaelmas rise she took off a block of thirty-one households in the first month of the year and got back, and none of that is in the letter, and a letter that does not say who to write to is furniture.

She telephoned the shop at twenty past four on the Friday afternoon, which is an hour after Orrin had shut it, and she did not apologise.

"It has gone up two hundred and eighty-four pounds and I have got one block of the nine that I can answer for, and I am not going to tell you how, because you will be pleased and it will not help the other eight." She was reading from something. "The one block is Ferrywright Row. Twelve to twenty. I telephoned them on Monday and sent them the certificate with your name and the pattern and the rate on it and the man came round on Wednesday and looked at the four clocks on the fourth floor and wrote *rate held* in a box, and it is eleven pounds a year, and it is eleven pounds a year for the rest of my life, and I have never had eleven pounds from a piece of paper in nineteen years of being a landlord."

"That is not a good outcome, Mrs Stroud."

"It is the worst outcome there is," said Hanna Stroud, "and it is the only one that is going to get better for anybody, and I have not got the energy to be bitter about a piece of paper at four o'clock on a Friday, and I have got two hundred and eighty-four pounds instead."

"That certificate certifies four clocks against each other and not against the district. It is worth nothing to you and you know it is worth nothing to you."

"Then why did I get eleven pounds out of it?"

"Because it is true," said Orrin Vale, "and because it says who looked at them and when, and because your insurer cannot argue with a thing that a licensed man has put his name under, and a true document about a small machine is worth eleven pounds a year even when the big documents are unavailable."

"That is the most depressing sentence anybody has said to me this year," said Hanna Stroud, "and I have had a bad one about a ceiling." She put the telephone down on him, which is a thing landlords do.

He wrote it in the book, all of it, including the eleven pounds, because a thing that costs somebody two hundred and eighty-four pounds and gains eleven is not a story and is an account, and he keeps accounts.

---

The council's room over the chandler's shop on Weir Lane had eleven people in it on the Saturday afternoon, the thirteenth, which is the most it has held since the first month, and the reason it was full is a piece of paper on a board outside a pump house that anybody on that stretch can walk out and read, and it took two people saying so at the market on the Friday to fill a room.

Wenna Pruitt had the return. She had asked for a copy on the Friday morning, and a ward office will give a copy to anybody who asks and cannot be refused, and she had asked, and she had it in her hand, and she had done the arithmetic twice before she came down, which is what she has done since the first month and what she will be doing in September for the following spring, and the September is the same September it has always been and the year has changed underneath it.

"Twelve pounds of rated house is thirteen shillings," she said. "That is nearly twice the price of the reinstatement, and the reinstatement is the thing that caused it, and I have checked that four times, and I want it written down that I have checked it four times and that I still do not know what to do with it."

Bessie Crane said, "Then it is not for me to say, and I have got thirty-one women and one of them off eight weeks this year and paid out of her own pocket, and the tin this council takes its money out of is my tin and it is four pounds lighter than it was in the first month, and the page in the Advertiser is cancelled, and I have not got a thing to sell that I did not have in the first month."

"Thirty-one against ninety-one," said Ivy Pike, from the end of the table. "I have got the pay book for the ninety-one and sixty-one of them live in this ward, and their water is going up a shilling and a penny in the pound on the strength of a return that got corrected, and the correction is right, and I have watched a works close for want of water in a winter and two of its men go to the docks and not come back, and I am not going to be the woman in this room who says a rate rise is nothing. A rate rise is a thing about a hundred and forty thousand people and I have the book."

Bessie Crane said, "And I am not going to be the woman who says the water should have gone somewhere else, because I have been the one that had no water, and so have you, Ivy, in a different month, and we are both sitting in a room arguing about a rate that neither of us set and both of us are going to pay, and I am not going to choose and neither are you, and we can go and have the argument again when there is another notice."

They did not choose. It is the third time this year those two women have been asked to choose and the third time they have refused, and it is now a habit of the district rather than a courtesy, and it will outlast both of them.

Wenna did the September arithmetic on the back of the return, in public, out loud, and that is a thing she does because she says that a set of figures in a hand is a figure and a set of figures said out loud in a room of eleven people is a set of figures somebody can be asked about.

Teo Arden was at the back, and he had been at the back, and Wenna Pruitt had not looked at him once in an hour and a half, and everybody else in the room had noticed and nobody said anything, and they had been not speaking since the ninth of the fourth month, which is four weeks and six days, and which is the longest they have gone since the first month of the year.

He had a sheet of paper in his hand and he did not put it on the table for the first hour. At about twenty to five he got up and put it on the table in front of Wenna Pruitt, and it was a fair copy in a young man's upright hand of four lines, with a date and a source and a name at the bottom.

> *Green Span hospital line, canal crossing, east span. Fifteen thirty-seconds of an inch at the third bracket, against the setting-out marks cut in the north coping in 1911. Fifteen thirty-seconds measured on the 8th and 9th of the fifth month by M. Vale, class three, in the works' permit book, page 61, in the district's own book, in my hand, against the works' own figure of twelve thirty-seconds on the survey of two years and three months ago.*
> *Seven pounds short at the hospital end of a mile and a quarter of conduit.*
> *Pumping ward's reserve making it up. Tank below two days since the second of the third month. Low-level alarm inhibited since the same date. No report made.*
> *Canal front drawing four times a night since the seventeenth of the fourth, by order of a committee that could not ask the district.*

"Copied and given to Mrs Pruitt without asking her," said Teo Arden, "and put on this table in front of eleven people, and my name is at the bottom of it, and it goes on the board on Monday if she will let it, and if she will not let it then it goes on the board on Monday anyway and she will be the one who has to take it down, because that is how boards work."

"I will not let it," said Wenna Pruitt.

"Then I will put it up at eight in the morning when the board is empty and I will not tell you, and you will find it, and you may take it down, and we will both know what we have done."

"That is not a method," said Wenna Pruitt, "that is a quarrel, and we said in the second month that we would not settle it and that we would not be the same arithmetic in a year, and it is the thirteenth of the fifth month and we are not the same arithmetic and I have not moved and neither have you, and I will say one thing about your sheet and then I am going to stop."

"Say it."

"You have put four addresses on it." She did not touch it. "There is no address on it. There is a crossing, a tank, a pump house and a schedule, and a person could read all of it and go and stand at any of the four places and say *that is the number*, and a person who wanted to do harm could not get from any of the four to a door in half an hour, and you know that, because you said it yourself in this room in the third month and you were right, and I have not forgotten it, and you have gone and printed it anyway."

"That is not the same thing."

"It is the same thing with a date on it," said Wenna Pruitt, and picked up the return, and the hour was up.

He had put it on the board on the Monday morning, and it was on the board on the Monday, and she did not take it down, and neither of them said a word about that either, and that is where the argument stands on the fifteenth of the fifth month: open, unresolvable, and correct on both sides, and neither of them has been made a liar and neither of them has got what they wanted, and they had said in the second month in this room that they would not be the same arithmetic in a year, and it is three months and they are not the same arithmetic and both of them were right about that as well.

Dilly Amnersley came up at eight on the Monday morning of the fifteenth with a lamp in her hand, and she wound four clocks that did not need winding, because they were wound, and they were wound because he had wound them on the Thursday, and the lamp is still in her hand and the card is still in her cardigan pocket and she is sixty-six and she will carry it up that stair on a Monday morning for the rest of her tenancy.

"They agree," she said. "All four. I put them against each other with my own watch and the two long-cases were the same to a second and a half and the one on the wall is three seconds behind them, and three seconds in a week is a house moving, and the big one says what it said to me last Sunday, and I have looked at the card on the wall and the hour on it and I have not touched it."

"Nobody touches those cards."

"I have been in this building fifty-one years," said Dilly Amnersley, "and I have watched two men touch a card like that and I have watched one of them ruin a clock in about a month."

She was going down when she stopped, and came back up two steps, which she would not have done for anybody.

"There is a card," she said, "about the key. It is in the letting agent's file and the agent's office is a room over a tobacconist's now, and the card says the key is not a service, and it has said that since before Mrs Stroud had the block, and I have never once asked to see it, because I did not want to be told it was a service. You are a man who reads forms. Could somebody ask, and is it worth asking?"

"What would you want if you did ask?"

"I would want it to say what it does," said Dilly Amnersley, "and it does wind four clocks on a Monday morning, and that is what it does, and I have been doing it since my husband died, and his mother did it before him, and nobody in this building has ever been asked and nobody has ever answered." She went down. "And I am not asking you to. I have never asked anybody for anything and I am not starting at the end of a good week."

The weather turned on the Monday night and the glass went down nineteen hundredths in six hours, and the wind came round into the west, and by two in the morning it was blowing the rain across the towpath in sheets and the river was making with a three-inch surge on the top of a spring tide, and the whole of the fifth month had been leading up to it for a week without anybody noticing except the two offices in this city that keep a barometer, and both of them had written *rain, heavy, south-west* on a form with a column for the district on it, and the column for the district had said *Lower Weirs*, because that is the district the weather is in, and not one of the two forms had a line for what the district could give.

Orrin stood in the doorway of the shop at twenty past two on the Monday night with the rain coming across Verge Lane in a wall and the grey book shut on the bench behind him, and he worked out that he had spent four days with a rule and a steel rule and a four-inch level and had got a number anybody could check, and that he had spent the Monday before that on a landing with a chronometer and a badger brush and had got four clocks that agree with each other and cannot tell anybody the time, and that in between he had put four pounds and two shillings into a form and read a rate notice and watched a woman not be thanked and two women refuse to choose, and that on Saturday night it was going to rain on about eleven hundred people who live at the bottom of a canal and who own nine-tenths of the schedule that is about to be used to hold the water up, and who cannot be asked, and who will find out from a nail on a board.

The last thing he did that week, on the Monday night, with the shop shut, was to telephone the Green Span hospital and ask for the third floor, and ask for Elsie Cobb by name, and she came to the telephone in about ninety seconds, because she is on nights and has been for eleven weeks.

He asked her one question, which was whether the card in the nurses' room still had her name on it, and she said it did, and he said that on the night of the thirtieth, at about ten o'clock, four people were going to be on that card and it would be hers, and would she be there, and she said yes, and he said good, and put the telephone down, and wrote nothing down, because the whole of the rule is that a person who is asked and says yes is not a result, it is a person, and there is no column for that either.
