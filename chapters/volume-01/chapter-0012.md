# Chapter 12 — What the Form Wants

Hask's office was the second door and it was warm and there was tea, and the cup was the same chipped porcelain with the rim gone white, and it had been in that office since before Orrin was apprenticed.

"Sit down. You look like a man who has been arguing with a form and losing."

"I have had a conversation with a form," said Orrin. "On Thursday, down in the Weirs, with a man who measured a pump house and told me he could not write down a name. It was better than the argument."

Hask pushed the second cup across and turned a quarter turn on its saucer, which he did when he was arranging his thoughts, and which Orrin had watched for fifteen years and had never once been able to get ahead of.

"Then you know more about it than I do," said Hask, "which after the performance at the Cathedral last week is a thing I'd not have bet on."

"I didn't file a note."

"You didn't file a note. You went down to Weir Two instead, and Inspector Rill tells me you were there at ten to ten on Thursday morning, and I don't mind that, and I'll tell you why I don't mind it, because it's the correct use of your time and I would rather you spent your Thursday in a pump house than in my office with a complaint." He put his hands flat on the desk. "Do you know what he writes in those books? I've had four years of them on my desk. He writes *consistent* and *within tolerance* and he signs it and he never once has written down a district that I could act on. In nineteen years of quarterly rounds that man has never sent this hall a line that has cost anybody anything, and that is not because he is careful. It's because there isn't a column."

He said it without bitterness. He said it the way a man says a thing he has made peace with at some length.

"Then what is the column for?" Orrin said.

"For the line." Hask took the card out of his own drawer, which told Orrin that Hask had put it there himself and not had it delivered, and he turned it round and pushed it across the desk so Orrin could see the printing upside down. "This is a card. It's hall stock. It goes to two hundred and forty men once a year and it does the same work on every one of them, and the reason it does the same work is that it asks for four things and there are only four things it can ask for. The fee. The endorsement. The date. And the work book. Everything else about a renewal is a conversation, and a conversation is optional, and this hall has never once in my time made a conversation compulsory."

"Then why is it on the card?"

"Because it's the only part of a renewal anybody has ever failed at." Hask took the card back. "Forty-one years I've been in this room and I have had four men let their licence lapse, and three of them let it lapse because they were angry with me and one of them let it lapse because he was eighty-one, and I could sign all four of them back in the morning. Do you know what I have never once had? I have never had a man come to me and say, Gerren, here's a book, here's what's in it, take it. Not in forty-one years. And you know why? Because a book belongs to whoever kept it, and a hall that takes a man's book is a hall that has admitted it wanted to see it. So we ask for it, and we write *requested*, and we have never once taken it, and every man in this hall knows that we have never once taken it, and that is the only thing standing between this hall and being the kind of hall that takes things."

He put the tea down.

"So I'm asking."

It was a good answer. It was also, Orrin understood about four seconds later, a complete answer to a question that had not been asked, delivered with the timing of a man who has had thirty years to get good at it.

---

The interview was the part he had not been ready for, and he did not know until he was in it that it was a part.

"I've added something to your card," Hask said. "Nothing binding. It's in my own hand at the bottom, you can read it, and it says that the master would be glad of twenty minutes with the holder before the renewal goes up, as a matter of good practice, and that the holder is welcome to bring the book or not bring the book."

"What kind of conversation?"

"The kind where I ask how the year went." Hask turned the card back. "That's the whole of it. I ask how the year went, and I write it down in the file, and the file is not published and the conversation is not binding and nobody's licence hangs on it. It's in there because the hall runs on knowing what its men are doing, and the two men I have lost licences in forty-one years both stopped telling me what they were doing about two years before I lost them, and both of those men told me afterwards that they'd stopped because there was no way to say what they'd been at without a conversation first."

"Which of those is the conversation for?"

"Neither." Hask looked at him. "That's exactly the point of it, and I could not make it plainer. If you come, you have a conversation, and nothing follows from the conversation. If you don't come, I write *offered, declined* in the file, and nothing follows from that either. There is no third thing. You'll not find the third thing because there isn't one, and if a man in my trade ever tells you there is, he wants something."

"And if I don't come, when do you put it?"

"The twenty-eighth of next month, at ten, in this room, and I'll leave it in your book here." He wrote it on the card's blank line in a small neat hand and turned it round. "That is a little over a month from today and three weeks before your renewal, and I have put it in the middle on purpose, because a man who is going to have a conversation about his year is better off having it before he has decided what he thinks of it, and a man who is not going to have one is better off having it while there is still time to write to me about it."

Orrin looked at the date. The renewal was about eight weeks away and the conversation was a month, and Hask had put the conversation in the middle of the run rather than at either end of it, which meant that for the next four weeks the whole of his trade was going to sit in a warm room with a bad cup of tea in it at ten o'clock on a Tuesday morning, and that the eight weeks after it was going to be spent already having had it, and that there was no version of the next month in front of him in which that Tuesday was not standing in the middle of it like a chair in a waiting room.

"That's fair," he said.

"It is meant to be. It isn't interesting, but it is meant to be."

---

The book came up at about a quarter to twelve and it came up because Hask had put it there, in the middle of a conversation about a regulator, without any change of tone at all.

"You've had a strange month." Hask was not writing; he was reading a lever escapement in a case on his own bench, through a glass, with one eye. "The Cathedral, Weir Two, a hospital, and something on Saturday night that half the Quarter heard about before I did, which I take as some compliment and also as a complaint. You keep a book."

"Yes."

"I know you do. There is not a maintainer in this hall I could not tell the habits of." He put the loupe down. "It's yours and it's not in our form."

"No."

"Right." He nodded. "I'm not going to ask what a school exercise book is worth, and I'm not going to ask to see it, and I want you to hear me say that plainly because I would like it written down that I said it and did not do it, even if nobody ever asks me whether I did."

"Fine."

"But I want you to hear the other half too, because I have never in my life given anybody half a thing and let them think it was the whole." He took the loupe off the bench and put it in a drawer, which was a signal. "The hall cannot have your book. I agree entirely and I have told the licensing floor the same and they can print it. The hall is not going to seize a man's private book and I would resign before I signed that, and I say resign and I would not, but I would resign, and I want that on the table as a fact about me and not as an argument."

"All right."

"So the hall must have a work book. That is a requirement of the licence and it is older than me and it is not going away because a good man kept his own. And the work book must be in the hall's form, current issue, lodged with the fee. Which means, Orrin—" he turned round and looked at him properly, and there was nothing unkind in the face, and that had always been the difficulty. "Which means that from today, the only record of what you have looked at and what you have found and what you have thought about it, in any office in this city, is going to be one I write. Not today. Not until you hand it in at the renewal, or hand in a W-4 you've filled from your own, which is the same thing with more work in it. But from today, if anybody in Morrowfen asks me what my maintainer found in the Weirs in the spring, I can only read them what I have put on a hall form, and the hall form has a column for *fault observed* and a column for *work performed* and a column for *recommendation*, and there is no column on it, and there has never been a column on it, for a man to write down that he is not sure."

The room was very quiet. Down the stairs somebody dropped a tray.

"So that's the bill," Hask said. "About eight weeks from the morning that card went on your bench, and there it is, and I have not moved your date and I have not moved your fee and I have not said a word about the Cathedral, and I have had two people in this office since March who would have moved all three and I have not, and if you want to know why I have not, it is because I have read your face since you were twenty-two and I do not think there is anything in that book that could be got at by anybody who has not already got at it. But the hall cannot go on a licence for what a man is nice about. I have a form and the form is the only honest thing I have got."

"I know."

"And you'll come on the twenty-eighth if you come, and I'll be glad of you, and the tea will be as bad as it is."

---

He was almost down the worn stair when he stopped, because there was one more thing and he wanted to ask it while the room was still warm.

"Before the trestle. The roster. Did it come back?"

"The roster." Hask did not look up. "I am not the office that holds a roster."

"I know you are not. I am asking whether the woman who is, said anything."

"Not to me, and not in a form I have seen, and I would know, because I see everything that comes through that door." He put a hand flat on the labour book, which was shut, and did not open it. "Ask her. And when you ask her, ask her what the room is called and not who holds it, because the second question is the one that makes people stop asking."

"Good," said Orrin, and meant it, and did not say so.

"Hask. If I have taken work that isn't on the board this month."

"Would you be able to tell me?"

The staircase was cold on the other side of the door and the licensing floor was loud and Orrin stood in it and worked out, with the flat efficiency he worked out everything, that there were two answers available and that he had known there were two answers available before he had asked the question, which meant the question had not been a question.

"There was a job on Saturday night," he said. "The hospital trestle. I went out with the line."

"You went out with the line." Hask did not raise an eyebrow. He was standing in his doorway with the card in his hand and he nodded, once, and it was a small nod and it went on a fraction too long. "I know about the trestle. Half the Quarter knows about the trestle. Did you certify anything?"

"No."

"Then there's no form for it and there's nothing anybody could do, and if there's a job in this city that would rather somebody had called the hall first, that job is the trestle and I'd have been the same." He went back to his desk. "Orrin. When you take work off the board, tell me. Not so I can do anything about it. So that if a man in a good coat ever comes up here and asks me whether there was anybody on your trestle, I can say yes, there was, and I can say when, and I will not have to stand here and discover what I don't know."

"All right."

"That's a yes, then."

"That's a yes, then."

He went back to the lever escapement and put the loupe up again, and Orrin went down the stairs, and in the lane outside he stood for a moment with his hands in his pockets and understood that he had just said yes to something, and that the yes had cost him about four seconds and had saved him about an hour, and that by the time the fee fell due the only copy of any of it would be in a hand that was not his, on a form with three columns, and that he had agreed to that this morning in a warm room over bad tea without once putting it into a sentence.

---

Mara Venn's office was two rooms on the second floor of a building on the Rise and there was one window and a gas jet and a wall of pigeonholes with paper in them, and she was in the middle of eleven days' correspondence when he came in and did not take his coat off.

"Thursday," he said. "The inspector's finding. No shift record exists, nothing to certify, file closed. It's honest, it's complete, and it's on my bench in his handwriting, and it is the single most useless document anybody has ever produced about Perry Vance."

"Sit down."

"He also told me the Continuity Office keeps a file of everything it does. Inspections, transfers, deferrals. Public on petition. Eleven days, and he thought that was slow."

She put her pen down.

"When did he tell you that?"

"Thursday afternoon." He stopped. "Mara, when did you lodge for that file?"

"On the twentieth," she said. "The afternoon we were in Lake's room. I walked back and put in a petition for the whole Lower Weirs inspection and transfer file, because Lake showed me the routing card and the routing card had a line saying the Continuity Office keeps a copy of every transfer, and I have been asking that office for records for seven years." She turned a sheet over. "It comes up on the second. That is the earliest date it can possibly come up, and I want you to have worked out why, so that you know the arithmetic of this office and not just its manners."

"Eleven days."

"The twentieth plus a day, because the box is emptied at noon and I put it in after the noon, and the twenty-first was a Sunday, and the box is not emptied on a Sunday, so the count starts on the twenty-second. Eleven days, and the second is the earliest it can be, and a day later it would have been the third. That is the entire difference between this office and a person, and I want you to look at the file and understand that nothing in it is a favour."

Orrin sat down on the stool.

"You'd already asked."

"I asked on the twentieth. You'll have the file on Thursday, the second, at nine, in a file room with a table and no fire, and I have booked the table." She was writing again. "Now. Tell me what Hask said, since you have come down here in a hurry on a Friday afternoon and you did not take your coat off, and one of those two facts is about the file and the other one is about the hall."

He told her. Both halves, in order, and the tea and the date on the card and the loupe going into the drawer and the third column that did not exist, the four weeks to the Tuesday, and the fact that from today the only record of what he had found in any office in this city would be written by a man who had not been in the room.

When he had finished she read back one sentence of it, which was *from today the only record of what you have looked at and what you have thought about it will be one I write*, and said:

"He did not say that to be cruel. He said it because it is true and because he thought being told the true thing early was a kindness. That is what the hall is. It is a place that tells you the price of your own independence on a Friday and then gives you eight weeks to pay it." She put the pen down. "What are you going to do?"

"Hand them a W-4 on the nineteenth and fill it in from the book."

"That is a good answer and I do not think it is your answer, and I am not going to argue with you about it, because you have not got to the decision yet and a man who argues about a decision he has not made is only making a noise." She put a sheet in front of him and turned it round. "Read that. It's Monday's post. It came back this morning with a note on it, which is the courtesy this office extends to a request it is not going to fill."

He read it. It was her request for the pumping ward's certified shift returns for Weir Two for the four months to the end of last quarter, and the note on the front was in a clerk's hand and read:

*Received and noted. This office has no record of certified shifts outside the guild series. Application to the hall recommended.*

Four months of returns. Receipted on the twentieth. Answered on the twenty-sixth, in six days, without being refused, and without being answered by anybody.

"It is not a refusal," Mara said. "I want that understood, because it is worse than a refusal. If they had refused me I could appeal it. They have noted it. There is no appeal against a note." She took the sheet back. "There is a week of this waiting in front of us, and I have had nine years of it, and I have never once got used to it, and I have never once found a way to make it shorter by wanting it shorter. So: what are you going to do about the book?"

"Keep it," said Orrin.

"Yes," said Mara Venn. "That is what I thought, and now you have said it out loud, and you can put your coat on and go home."
