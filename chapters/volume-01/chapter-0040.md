# Chapter 40 — The Door on the Rise

He had it in the grey book as a fair copy and he had had it since the second of the second month, and on the Saturday morning of the twenty-ninth of the fourth month he took it out of the book and put it in his coat and walked down to the pumping ward at half past eight, and the walk takes forty minutes and he did it in thirty-five, and the reason he did it in thirty-five is that he had been not doing it for eighty-six days and he was aware of the shape of the not doing it and the shape had got into his walking.

The pumping ward is two rooms over a boat-builder's shop on the canal road and the outer one has a bench in it for men waiting for the tide, and the inner one has Sel Kade's desk in it and about nine feet of shelving and a window that looks at the back of the boat-builder's.

He had written the rule on a Sunday in the second month and it had been the reason for two things that had not worked and it was the only piece of his own method that had ever done anything. *No result of mine goes out of this room without a person named who can refuse it.* He had told the Weirs' room in the third month that the rule was free and available and had been offered to a district and declined. He had had this sheet for eighty-six days and he had not taken it round.

So he said it out loud in the outer room before he went in, to nobody, because the rule is not a rule about being alone, it is a rule about a person who can refuse, and the refusing has to be available to somebody who knows what is being refused.

"I have got a sheet in my coat that is about you and it is eighteen years old and I have had it since the second of the second month and I have not brought it to you, and I am bringing it to you now, and you can refuse it and I will take it away and I will not put it anywhere. That is the whole of what I am saying and you may tell me to go and eat my lunch."

"I have got eleven minutes before the tide," said Sel Kade. "Say it again in the room and shut the door."

---

He read the whole of it out. It took about ninety seconds and it is a short document and most of it is a form number.

> *Transfer out of the public record. Guild of Horologists, form GH-9, standing withdrawal, maintenance-deferred class. Batch 21/4 (Weirs series), forty-one entries. Transferred eighteen years ago. Requested by the hall on behalf of the district. Price, two shillings the head for a year. Routing copies to the hall, the ward, the Continuity Office inspection file, and a public-health rota.*
>
> *Countersigned for the affected district by the pumping warden of the Lower Weirs, deputy: **S. Kade**.*

It is the countersignature that is the whole of the document. Everything else about it is an index entry, and an index entry is a thing anybody in this city may read in a public room on Cathedral Rise, and he has read it in one, and so has Mara Venn, and so has Sindri Lake, and nobody ever thought of giving it to the only living person whose name is on it.

Sel Kade read it herself, twice, out loud the second time, and she read the countersignature the way a hydraulic engineer reads a name on a drawing, and then she sat back.

"Right," she said. "Say the next part out loud, because I want to hear whether you have got it or whether you have got a version of it."

---

"I have got this," he said, "and I have not got the other one, and the other one is the part I came to say and it took me eighty-six days to say it and I would like to say it now while I have the nerve.

"You were twenty-six. You were a deputy warden. You signed for a district that had no standing, on a form that needs one. And nobody has ever explained to you — or to me, or to a woman in a kitchen in Weir Lane, or to a woman in Green Span who paid four pounds ten to have a return printed — how a place with no standing manages to have its name in a record eleven times. And I have had it in my hand for eighty-six days, and I worked it out in about four seconds on the second of the second month, and it took me until about a month ago to understand that this is what it says, and I want to say it to you in a room and not in a corridor and not in a file.

"A district cannot sign. A district has no box. A form cannot be given to a place. **So the hall got a person.** A deputy pumping warden of the Lower Weirs is a person in the Weirs, and a person in a district can sign for a district, and there is no rule that says the person has to have been asked by the district, because there is no body in the district that could ask. It is not a trick. It is the only door in the building and it is wide enough for a person and not for a place, and I do not think anybody designed it, and I do not think anybody had to, and a form that can be signed by a person and not by a district will get itself signed by a person in about eleven years every time it wants something.

"And that is the answer to a question I have been carrying since the eighth of the third month, and it is the answer to a question a woman in this district has been carrying for nineteen years, and it has been sitting in a public index on the Rise the whole time and nobody had any reason to look at the countersignature line, because a countersignature line is the part everybody trusts."

Sel Kade said, "I remember the room."

---

"I did not expect you to."

"I remember it because of the weather. I remember it because it was the storm month and I had been in post eleven months and I was twenty-six and I was in a room on a Tuesday with a chandler in it who is one of the nine and has never once in his life been on a pump house roof." She turned the sheet round to face herself and squared it. "I remember being asked whether I would sign, and I remember saying that I would want to know what the class meant, and I remember being told.

"I was told that the class meant the work would be done and not paid for. That is the whole of what I was told and it was true. I was told the alternative was a rate rise on the front, in the spring, on about ninety properties, and that a rate rise in the spring of a year like that one is a rise you cannot put back. And I was twenty-six, and I had been in the post eleven months, and I had no reserve and no money and no way of telling a difference, and the district could not be asked because the district cannot be asked, and a person was in the room and the person was me."

"What was the person called," said Orrin Vale. "In the room. The man who asked you."

"I have thought about that for eight years. His name was — no. I am not going to say his name. He was the hall's man and he did not do anything wrong and he did not enjoy it and I have never seen him since and I have not gone looking, and if I have to be honest with you I could not tell you his face this morning." She put the sheet down. "I remember that I asked him one question and it was a good question and I have been angry with myself for twenty years for not asking it twice.

"I said: *if the work is done and not paid, who is it done by?* And he said the district. And I said, *the district is us.* And he said that a district is a list of properties and not a list of persons, and that the hall prices the list and not the persons, and that I was signing the list.

"And that was true as well. It was the first true thing anybody had said to me about the district in my life and I was twenty-six and I signed it, and two shillings a head was never mentioned to me, because two shillings a head is what the district paid and I had no function in the price."

---

The tide was at the window and the boat-builder's went out and came back and Sel Kade looked at the sheet for about ninety seconds without saying anything.

"Three things," she said. "In order, and I am not going to be talked round any of them and I am not going to be thanked for any of them.

"**One.** I am not a fool and I was not a child and I have had thirty-one people on a pay book in my life and I did not need a man to explain the arithmetic. I want that out of your book. If you have written that a woman of twenty-six was tricked, take it out, and if you have not written it, keep writing, because somebody will.

"**Two.** There is one form and I know that, because I have known it since the first month of the year and I have been waiting for somebody to say it out loud so that I could stop being the only person in this district who knows it. **The same series.** A standing withdrawal is a request on form GH-9, priced at two shillings the head for a year, and to put forty-one heads back into a district's standing you put a request in on the same form, in the same series, in the same box, with the same price. And I am a pumping warden and I know what a district's water costs and I have never in my life been able to say what a district's standing costs, and it is on the same page as two shillings a head for a year, and it has been on the same page since the year before the Long Nine.

"And the answer you are going to give me, and I have worked it out already, is that the hall will send back the card. The four lines. *The hall does not keep a record of hours for work that is not part of a certified shift, there being no such hours, there being no such shifts.* And it will be true, and my night crew is not a certified shift, and my night crew is not a certified shift **because they were taken out of the model by a form I signed**, and if I put the form back in they will be a certified shift, and if they are a certified shift then the hall does keep a record of them and the four lines will be a lie.

"And it will not be a lie, because I will be holding the receipt. It is that kind of a system, and it has been that kind of a system since before either of us, and there is no office in this city that can be made to answer for a definition, and you know that better than anybody alive, because you have watched it happen to me for four months and you have not once been able to get me out of it."

"Three," said Sel Kade, before he could answer it. "**Three, and this is the one that matters and it is the reason I am not going to say yes to anything this morning.**

"If forty-one heads go back into a district's standing, then the ward has hours. And a ward's rates are set on its return. And the ward's return is a nil, and it has been a nil for eleven years, and it will not be a nil if the hours go back in. So **the price of putting my night crew back into the civic model is a rate rise on the poorest ward in this city, and the rate rise falls on the same people the crew is.**

"That is the whole of it. I have known it since the first week of the fourth month and I have not said it to anybody, and I have said it to you first, and I would like you to sit with it for about a minute before you tell me what you are going to do, because you are a man who has been handed an instrument eleven times this year and has never once had to pay for it himself, and this one costs money and the money comes out of the ward and the ward is the water the crew stands on."

---

He sat with it. Outside, a boy went along the canal road with a handcart and did not hurry, and the light came off the water in the ordinary way, and the district indicator at the corner two streets away had a hairline of paint on the bezel at a quarter past six and a needle sitting under it, and nothing happened at six that evening, because the front was giving up three goes and not four, and the line had stopped, and would not start again until somebody gave it four.

"Ninety-one heads?" he said.

"Forty-one. The batch is forty-one entries. I have looked at the number about four times in eight years and I remember it and I did not know that until this morning." She squared the sheet again. "It is not the whole of the ward and it is not the whole of the crew either and I am not going to be sentimental about it. It is a form. It is a batch of forty-one entries in the maintenance-deferred class and it goes in for a year and the year is a year and I have been a warden for twenty-two years and I have never had a year in which I was not doing the arithmetic in September for the following spring."

"Eighty-two shillings," said Orrin Vale.

She looked at him.

"Two shillings a head, forty-one heads. Eighty-two shillings. Four pounds and two shillings."

"That is not what the price schedule is for," said Sel Kade. "That is what a page of a hall's book costs to get the class changed on. The hours are the other thing. The hours are the three hundred and twenty-four thousand gallons and the rate base and the alarm I had inhibited and the review that will not come, and you are not going to get a ward's standing back for eighty-two shillings and if anybody tells you that at the chandler's shop you are to tell me where they got it."

"Yes," he said. "I know. Eighty-two shillings is the form. The rest of it is the ward."

---

The Cathedral of Consequence keeps a filing counter on its ground floor on Saturday mornings, because a great many people in this city can only come to that hill on a Saturday, and the counter is in a lobby behind a grille and there is a clerk behind the grille and a bench and a mat and a form rack, and there is no other thing about the lobby at all.

The clerk was about thirty and she had a Saturday manner, which is a manner that is quicker and kinder than the weekday one because there are fewer people and they are all in a hurry for a Saturday.

"I'd like to see the Director."

She did not say no. That is the first thing, and it is the thing that took him a fortnight to understand afterwards.

"The Director sees matters on Tuesdays and Thursdays, by appointment, on form D-2. Is it a matter of the operating schedule?"

"I don't know what that means."

"It's a matter about a machine or a question that's been read, or a schedule, or a licence matter above your class. If it's one of those, the form is there and I'll put you on the list." She turned round and took a card off the rack and gave it through the grille.

It is a single side and there are four things printed at the head of it and there is a ruled table beneath and the table has three columns, and the third column is the countersignature, and the first of the four things printed at the head is the class of the applicant.

> *Matter requiring the personal attention of the Director. To be completed by a holder of a maintainer's licence of the second class or above, or by an Assembly member, or by an officer of the Continuity Office. The applicant must state the matter in not more than forty words. The Director will not see applicants in person, and no interview is offered. Matters are read, and answered in writing, in the order in which they are received.*
>
> | Nature of the matter | Machine or question to which it relates | Countersignature |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | | | |

There is a slip under the rack, printed on card, for applications returned unexecuted, and there is a box of them on the Saturday counter and on every other counter, and the clerk took one out and wrote on it and gave it back through the grille, and it is four lines.

> *Returned. This office does not receive matters under form D-2 from a holder of the third class. No appointment has been made and none will be. An applicant of the third class may put a matter before the operating schedule of a question that has been read, on form D-4, which is completed by a licence of the second class or above. The standing orders may be read at this counter.*

*May be read at this counter.* He read them, standing up, in a lobby, in the rain, on a Saturday. It is one sheet and it is a different set from the licensing floor's, and it is set in a size of type that he would have called the same size of type, and the fifth order on it is about the hours of a counter and the sixth is about the order in which papers are taken up, and by the seventh one he had stopped reading the words and was looking at the type. He has been in two buildings in this city for eleven years that print forms and he has never once looked at the type.

A class-three maintainer cannot complete this form. That is not a judgement and it is not a policy; it is a printing decision, and it is the same decision as the fifth standing notice on a licensing floor in another building in another district, set by the same forms printer along with eleven others, and it has been on that rack for as long as the rack has been there, and there is not one dishonest word on the card.

He stood at a grille in a lobby on a Saturday morning and read a card that had been printed to make his trade unable to reach a room, and he thought, in a way that surprised him by how calm it was: *that is notice five. That is the whole of the second hour on a Sunday. I have been reading a form for four months and I have not once asked myself who printed it.*

He did not fill it in. He put it back in the rack, square, and thanked her, and she said she was sorry, and meant something by it that was not a thing she could put a name to, and he went out into the wet of Cathedral Rise at about half past eleven and stood on the steps for a while.

Nobody offered him anything. That is the last thing about the lobby and it is the only thing about the lobby that he would want written down. There was no man on the steps, and no message, and no clerk who came out after him, and nobody in that building knew his name, and there was nothing in the world on that hill that wanted anything from him, and he had spent four months going into rooms where people needed things from him and had not once noticed how different it was to walk out of a room where they did not.

---

He got the bus at a quarter to twelve and it was on the Rise for a quarter of an hour, and he sat upstairs with his hat on his knee and did the arithmetic twice, and the second time he did it out loud on a bus on the Rise, which is not a thing a man of thirty-two does in a public conveyance in this city, and which he did, and which nobody said anything about.

*Eighty-six days, and a form, and a price schedule, and a district that cannot be asked, and three hundred and twenty-four thousand gallons of a ward's water that a warden of forty-four gave away in March for a works with ninety-one people on it, and a bridge that stands because three minutes off the Clockwork Quarter is a rounding error, and a man on a green span line who came into my shop on a Thursday evening with a carriage clock under his arm.*

*And the one instrument left is a form that costs four pounds and two shillings, at a counter, in a hall, from a man who has never once seen my face, and I do not know whether it will do anything, and I believe it will do the one thing it can do, which is put the right to be counted back for a year, and the right to be counted back is not the eleven years, and it is not the hours, and it is not one minute of anybody's lost afternoon.*

*And the hours are the ward's rates. And the ward's rates come out of the ward. So the true thing costs the water.*

*So I cannot put it to them. It has taken me an hour and a bit, on a bus, and that is the answer and it took an hour and not eighty-six days, and the reason it took an hour is that at about ten o'clock this morning somebody finally put the countersignature in front of the person whose name is on it, and she told me the price, and I have not got a counter-argument, and I have not been going to go and get one.*

*And that is my rule, and I have written it at the top of this book, and it is the only thing I have got that has ever worked, and I am about to break it.*

*No result of mine goes out of this room without a person named who can refuse it. The person is a deputy pumping warden of the Lower Weirs who is not my friend and who has not been recruited and is not going to be, and she has refused me twice in eleven weeks, and she has told me the price of the thing I want to do and the price is her ward's water, and she has not said no to the crew. She has said what it costs.*

*So I am not going to put a form in front of a district. I am going to put a form in front of a counter, on Monday morning, on my own, with my own money, and I am not going to tell anybody, and the reason I am not going to tell anybody is that the moment a district knows that a thing can be bought for eighty-two shillings then a district has been asked, and I have spent four months watching what happens when a district is asked, and the answer is that a district which has been asked becomes a thing that people are careful about, and I have never wanted that for the Weirs and I am not starting on a Saturday in a bus on the Rise with a hat on my knee.*

*And I will be breaking my own rule, and the rule is the only instrument I have, and I am breaking it because I have run out of every other one: a form I cannot complete on Saturday, a question I answered on Monday with eleven words nobody will read, a file I cannot see, a schedule with an interval on it, a number on a card in a hospital, and a sheet in a public index that a clerk is welcome to fetch for anybody who asks.*

*And I am not going to be able to say whether it is worth it. That is the part I have got used to. In eleven weeks I have not once been able to say.*

---

He got down at the bathhouse at ten past twelve and went up to his room and washed his hands and stood at the window with his tea he did not want.

From the window of the room above the bathhouse, on the fourth floor, on the top of a building on the north side of a street in the Clockwork Quarter, you can see two clocks and a great many roofs.

You can see the street clock on Verge Lane, three hundred yards down and to the left, and it is lit, and it has been lit since the first year of the century and it is a good light and it is the only reason anybody in that street knows what day it is. And you can see, at the far end of the same street, at an angle, about four hundred yards the other way, a corner clock over a shutter, and that one is not lit, and has not been lit since a man took a filament out of it in the winter before last and could not get back to it.

He had certified the Verge Lane clock in the third month of the year at £1 7s 6d, with a condition record and a certificate that said *within tolerance*, and had written the true thing on the reverse of it in four lines of pencil and had known before he sealed the envelope that nobody would ever read it.

He could see it from the window.

He could not have seen it from the window in the first week of the fourth month. On the first day of the fourth month the difference between those two clocks was four hundred seconds and it was a thing that took a gauge and a sounding board and a second watch and two days and a table with no headings in a shop book with a lease, and it was the only thing in the world he could not tell anybody, and he had told nobody.

From the top of a building, at twenty past one in the afternoon, in the rain, at a distance of four hundred yards, he could see it in about four seconds without an instrument of any kind. The lit one was six minutes and forty seconds behind the dark one, and it had been since the fifteenth of the third month, and it would be until somebody gave that main four draws a day for another month.

And so would every other person in this city. That was the discovery, standing at a window with a cup of tea gone cold in his hand, and it was not a good one and it was not a bad one, and it was the end of the only private thing he had.

For eleven weeks the slippage had been a shape in a shop book, and a shape is a thing a man can be the only one to hold, and a shape can be kept, and a shape can be handed to a person on a Tuesday in a room with a bar in his pocket, and for eleven weeks the whole of his usefulness in this city had been that nobody else in it could see what he could see.

It was out of the window. It was out of the window at twenty past one on a Saturday afternoon and it would be out of the window of every flat on that street and out of the window of the print shop off Church Street and out of the window of the room over the chandler's shop in Weir Lane, and it was not going to go back in, and there was not one thing he could do about it, and no rule he had ever written had anything to do with it.

He had spent the morning failing to get past a grille with a card, and the afternoon failing to get a district to say yes to the one thing that would cost it money, and he was going to go to the licensing floor on Monday morning and put four pounds and two shillings of his own into a form for forty-one heads, and he was not going to tell Mrs Pruitt, or Teo, or Leda, or Mara Venn, or Sel Kade, or the woman in the laundry, or the man in the shop on Verge Lane who had asked him a question in the first hour of a Saturday four weeks ago and had been told, correctly, that neither he nor his wife was in a position to say.

Four pounds and two shillings. That is one night at Ombersley's and change, and Ombersley paid four pounds eleven and gave him a cup of tea at half past four, and it was the only job in the last four months he has done that did not come out of a book of somebody else's hours.

He wrote it in the grey book that night, in one line, and it is the only line in two thousand-odd pages that is about him.

> *Saturday the 29th, the fourth month, nine at night. I have broken the third rule and I am not going to pretend about it. A person named who can refuse: S. Kade, twice, in writing, in eleven weeks, and the price of the thing I am going to do is her ward's water and she told me the price and I have not got a counter and I am not going to ask a district to pay it. So I am going alone, on Monday, with my own money, and I am not telling anybody, and if it is the wrong thing then it is the wrong thing by a licensed man of the third class in his own shop book and nobody will ever know and that is what I have got left.*
>
> *Also, and I want it in the same book as the other thing, because it is the only good news in four weeks and it is not good news at all: from the window of my own room, at twenty past one this afternoon, in the rain, I could see the difference between two clocks four hundred yards apart without a gauge, without a watch and without a hearing, and any person in this street could see the same, and by Monday morning every window on this hill will be able to.*
>
> *Which means that from today the error in this city is not a private fact. It is a fact about four hundred yards of street that anybody can see. And there is no form for it and no office that has to answer for it and no column in any book in this city, and no fund, and nobody in this city is going to be angry about it tomorrow morning, and every window on this hill can see it.*
