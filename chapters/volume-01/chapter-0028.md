# Chapter 28 — The Ledger, Copied

The work book came back on the Tuesday morning of the twenty-first of the third month, two days into a fortnight, with a note from the back room stapled to the inside cover in a small upright hand that got smaller as it went.

> *Transcription checked and returned. Forty entries confirmed against the certification ledger, and there is nothing left in it. The maintainer is invited to check this transcription against the enclosure and to notify the licensing floor of any discrepancy. Enclosure: one leaf, taken from the labour book at the thirteenth line, and returned with the volume.*
>
> *E.L.*

The enclosure was a single leaf, cream, the same size as the work book, and it was headed in the guild's own type: *Extract, Labour Book, series 3, Lower Weirs, Weir Two floodgate and second-stage pumping station. True copy.*

Orrin read it standing up at the bench, with his coat on, and got to the second line and put his hand on the vice.

---

Eleven years, one line a year, in a small upright hand that was not the hand of the man who had made the original.

*Weir Two, second stage, night pumping, non-certified standby, 1 crew, 3 nights.*

And under it, in the fair copyist's hand, which is a better hand than the original and a much more careful one: the class, the batch number, the year, and a ruled column, and a signature at the foot with the word *true* in it and the copyist's name against it.

He knew the original. He had read it in a hall on a Saturday in the first month, on a page where the ink above had a Sunday in it and the line under it was in a different, smaller, harder-pressed, wrongly slanting hand, and he had stood in a brick room that smelled of sizing and mouse two months ago and watched a competent woman find it in a drawer.

The fair copy was perfectly made. Every character of it was right. It was signed, dated, and stamped, and it was true, and it was in a hand that any clerk in the licensing office could have identified as the work of the woman who had made eleven thousand of them.

The note was signed with two letters. Six weeks ago Orrin had watched a woman with those initials stand in a room over a chandler's shop and say *it is me who made the copies* in a flat voice, and then lose nineteen years for it inside a week, and she was the hand that had straightened the page.

It was also the only thing in the world that had taken the evidence out of the labour book.

That was the whole of it. Not an opinion. A physical fact he could have demonstrated to a magistrate with a rule and a pair of compasses.

The original entry was in a different hand from the eleven lines above and below it. That was the evidence. Not that the line said night pumping — the line said that in a hall's own book and the hall had been saying it for eleven years and had every right to say it. The evidence was that on one day in one year, in a book kept up fair for the tax man by a woman who was paid fourpence a page and a signature, a *second* person had put a line in. Different hand. Harder-pressed, because the pen had been pressed harder, because the person writing it did not have the book propped on anything, or was standing up, or was in a hurry, or did not want it read. Slanting wrong, because the page was turned and the wrist could not get round it.

The fair copy had straightened it. The fair copy was, in every respect a building recognises, better than the original: better made, more legible, more complete, more honestly produced, and signed by a woman who has never in nineteen years been asked what a standby is.

---

He was in the Weirs at about half past three the same afternoon with a single cream leaf in a folder in his case, and he did not go up the slipway to the pump house, he went into the kitchen over the chandler's shop, because Bevin Sarr was in it and he wanted a witness and he wanted somebody who was not him.

Wenna Pruitt was there, and Teo Arden, and a woman called Ivy Pike from the Weir One gate, and Bessie Crane was not there and had sent word that she had work at four.

He put the leaf on the table and turned it round.

"That's a fair copy of our line," he said. "Out of the hall's own labour book, in the hall's own form, made by a copyist in the back room, and signed, and dated, and true. It came back with my work book. There is no address to send it to because you have no address, so it came back to me."

Nobody touched it for about half a minute.

Then Bevin Sarr came in from the passage with her sleeves up and read it standing, and then leaned over the table and put her hand on the bare wood on the other side of it from Orrin's, about six inches away, and did not say anything for a while.

"That is beautiful work," she said at last. "That is a beautiful piece of work. In about nineteen years I have not seen that hand do better."

"Yes."

"And it is no use at all."

"Yes."

"I said it would be like that." She took her hand off the table. "I said the board does not leave the pump house and I said the reason and the reason was not sentimental, and every one of you heard the reason and half of you thought I was being dramatic, and I have waited four weeks for a piece of paper to come and prove it and it has come, and I want to say that I am not enjoying this, because there is a thing I have wanted for about twenty years, which is a document, and I have just been given one, and it is worse than nothing."

"Why is it worse than nothing?"

"Because nothing can be argued with and this can." Bevin Sarr sat down, which she almost never did. "Look at it. It is perfect. It says the right thing in the right place in the right form and it is signed by a woman with nineteen years of her hand behind it, and if you take that into a room — any room, a committee, a landlord, an insurer, a licensing floor — then there is nothing to ask. It is finished. The hall has spoken, the hall has signed, and if the district wants to say that the fourth line was written by somebody in a hurry on a Sunday, then the district has to say what the difference is *between two hands*, and the answer is that the difference is in a thing nobody in this city knows how to put on paper, and so the hall's copy stands, and the original is a scrawl, and a scrawl loses."

Orrin turned the leaf over. There was nothing on the back.

"It does not show the hand," he said.

"It cannot show the hand. A fair copy is not allowed to show the hand. A fair copy shows what the page establishes and a page that establishes eleven years of the same line establishes eleven years of the same line, and the wrongness of one of them is a *reading* of a document, and a reading is not a document, and if you have to produce a reading in a room you are a man making an argument and not a district producing a record." She spread her hands. "Mr Vale. Do you know what the board has got that this has not got?"

"Two hands."

"Two hands and a wet night," said Bevin Sarr. "That is all the evidence there has ever been for the Weir Two night crew in twenty years. Two hands, and a window where the ink above has got a Sunday in it, and a man who pressed harder than the man above him because he was not supposed to be writing at all. And you cannot file a window. You cannot put a Sunday in a form."

---

Wenna Pruitt had not sat down and she came and read it standing.

"Two things," she said. "The first is that it is a lie, and I am going to be the one in this room who says that, and I am going to say it properly and then never again.

"It is a lie because the original is not eleven years of the same line. The original is ten years of the same line and one line by a different person, and a fair copy of a page on which there is a fault is a fair copy of the page *with the fault in it*, and a copyist is instructed to reproduce the fault, and a good copyist would have put a bracket in and a note. There is no bracket. There is no note. The reason there is no bracket is that the copyist did not see a fault, because she cannot see a fault that is a hand, and the reason she cannot see it is that she has never been asked to look at one in nineteen years and nobody has ever written down that a hand is a thing to be reproduced."

She put her finger on the first line.

"The second thing is worse. Read the year on the fourth line. Four years ago. And read the day." She looked up. "There is no day. The original has a Sunday in it. **The fair copy has no day in it at all**, because the original line does not carry a day either, and the only reason we have ever known that a Sunday is in that week is that there is a Sunday in the ink on the line *above* it. The fair copy cannot carry the ink above, because the ink above is not part of the line. So we have lost the Sunday. We have lost the only thing in that book that told anybody a man was writing at an hour he ought not to have been writing, and we lost it by making a better copy."

Nobody said anything.

"That is what a fair copy is," said Wenna Pruitt. "It is a copy that cannot carry the truth, and it is signed by a woman who is not at fault, and it is correct, and it has cost her nothing and it has cost us the only thing we had."

---

The third thing happened on the Thursday the twenty-third, and it was not about Weir Two.

It was in the licensing floor at about eleven in the morning, and Orrin was there to hand back the acknowledgement slip for the returned volume, which is a thing a maintainer does, and he was standing at the counter with the slip in his hand waiting for a clerk to initial it when the counter's clerk was elsewhere and Della Marsh came round the end.

She put her hand on the counter and looked at the slip and did not initial it.

"The second sheet," she said.

"The second sheet."

"You brought the second sheet." She took it off him and looked at the endorsement and the wet stamp and then turned it over, which he had not expected, and which is not part of the process. "Yes. That's right. That's the copy. He does it every time now, since you, and it is entirely your doing and I want you to know that I have never said so to anybody and never shall." She initialed the slip. "Forty entries, and there is nothing left in it, and there is a name on it, and there is an enclosure, and I have not had an enclosure come off that counter in thirty-one years. And the copyist has done all of it on her own time, which is what she has done since she was let off the floor, and I have said nothing about that, because I say nothing about anything."

She put the slip down.

"I am going to tell you one thing about the enclosure and then I am going to go and do an inventory, and I am telling you because you are going to find out anyway and because I have decided that I have said what I said to you in a shut room once in my life and got nothing for it except four shillings, and that is fine, and it is what I said it would be."

"I did not think you—"

"You did not think anything about it, and that is correct, and it is the correct arrangement, and we both know it and neither of us has to perform it." She turned the second sheet back the right way up. "That enclosure was not requested by anybody. Nobody in this hall asked for an extract of the Weir Two line and nobody in this hall has ever heard of the Lower Weirs pumping district. The man on the counter sent your book down to the back room because forty entries is forty entries, and the woman in the back room came to the tenth one and read the words *Weir Two floodgate and second-stage pumping station* and did not know what a station was, and she did the only thing anybody in this building has taught her to do, which is to go and get the source and copy it out so that the maintainer could check her work against it. That is the whole of it. That is not a conspiracy. That is a copyist in a room with a name she does not know and a rule about enclosures."

"I am not suggesting—"

"You are not suggesting anything and I am not accusing anybody and we are two people standing at a counter on a Thursday morning, and Mr Vale—"

"Yes."

"Thank you for the second sheet." She picked up her tray. "It is a wet afternoon."

"It is," said Orrin Vale, and Della Marsh went and did an inventory.

---

The woman who had signed the note as E.L. was Hester Lomas, and had been since before Orrin was apprenticed, and she had not been inside the back room since the sixth of the second month, and the hall did not have her on its floor and was never going to have her on its floor again. He found her on the Thursday afternoon three doors down Verge Lane, in a front room with a shutter across the window and a table with a copying press on it, where a man who did conveyancing in Bellweather kept her on his books and paid her at a rate that was, by her own account, an insult with a small book-keeping attached.

She was forty-four. She had a good hand and she had the specific stillness of a woman who has spent nineteen years not being thanked for anything, and she took one look at him in the doorway and said, "You have come about the Weir Two line."

"You heard about it."

"There is a man from the hall on the first floor who came to see my man about a copy of a book and my man told him I was out and my man was lying, because I was in, and I have been in for six weeks." She put the press down. "You have not come to ask me what a standby is."

"No."

"I have known for six weeks that the hall's a disgrace and the whole of this street has known for six weeks, and I have had four offers of work from men who wanted a copy of the book, and every one of them has been a man who did not ask where he was getting it, and I have done two and refused two, and one of the two I refused to a man who put money in my hand first, which I did not think I was going to do in my life." She looked at him steadily. "So. What is it."

"I want your copy of the extract. Not the hall's. Ours."

"Yours is a pound of paper you cannot show anybody. If you want it copied I will do it at a shilling and my signature for the leaf, same as the hall, and fourpence a page for anything longer, same as the hall, and I have not moved off either rate in nineteen years, and I will sign it a true copy and I will not know what a standby is and the copy will be exactly as much use as theirs, and you know that, because you have read it."

"I have read it."

"Then why do you want it?"

"Because you sign it," said Orrin, "and because the district will want a paper of their own, and because in about a year somebody is going to ask whether the district holds a copy of anything, and I would like the answer to be yes."

Hester Lomas looked at him for a moment. Then she took a sheet out of a drawer, squared the press, and set the cream leaf in it, and worked for about six minutes, and the room was silent except for the press and the rain.

"A shilling and my signature," she said, "and that is the hall's rate for a leaf, and it is more than my page rate because a leaf is a leaf and not a page, and I have not moved off either in nineteen years, and you are not getting a discount for being decent to me, and I would be offended if you tried."

He paid her the shilling. He did not try to reduce it and she did not offer.

"One question," she said, at the door. "You have not asked me what I want. Everybody asks me. What I want is a lease and a wage a month, on the Bellweather Road, from a man who has a chandler's shop and a business premises, and I have wanted it for eleven weeks, and I am not going to pretend that I would not take it."

"There is a chandler's shop two doors from a kitchen in Weir Lane," said Orrin, "and the man upstairs keeps a copy of a labour book because a copy in a shop is a copy in a business."

"I know the shop," said Hester Lomas. "I have been past it forty times."

---

He told the rest of it at half past two that afternoon, on the slipway, with the rain going sideways off the water, to four people who had come for no reason anybody could name and who stayed for an hour and a half.

The boardinghouse on the Kestrel side was the last of it. Nell Arter had asked and Orrin had asked and it had cost nobody anything, because the hall's position is that anybody in the public may read the labour book off an open shelf, and there is no form for copying it, and there is no fee, and there is no name required, and Constance Kear is fifty-eight and a presser and can no longer lift her arms above her head on a Tuesday and is not, in the terms of every document in this city, a person who has ever worked.

There was a fair copy too, of course. One of the copyist's, made on the same afternoon, for the same reason, in the same excellent hand, of a line that read *Kestrel side, boardinghouse, stair and landing light, maintenance-deferred, batch 22/1, 1 crew, 3 nights*, and it was signed, and it was true, and there was not one mark on the whole of it that a person had ever made.

"That is worse than nothing," said Teo Arden. "Do you understand that it is worse than nothing? I can take that into a landlord's office and he will throw me out. I can take it into a workhouse and a clerk will read it and write *no record of employment* on a form, in a good hand, and sign it. Nine women, six years, and I have handed them a piece of paper that says the same thing as before in a way that cannot be argued with, and I paid a shilling for the privilege."

"You paid a shilling because a copyist in a hall made a mistake with a name she did not know," said Orrin.

"I know. That is the whole of it. She made a mistake with a name she did not know and it cost the guild nothing and it has cost that house six years of proof and it will cost them the rest of it, and there is no way to say *that is not what it says* to a document that is true." He folded the copy and put it in his coat. "Right. So the district has got a copy. What is the district going to do with it?"

Nobody answered him, and Orrin understood while he was standing in the rain that the question was not going to be answered that week, and that when it was answered it would not be by him, and that it was not a question about the truth of anything.

It was a question about which of two true things a district was going to walk into a room holding. The board had two hands and a Sunday in the ink and no address. The extract had a form number and a copyist's signature and a table of its own, in a room, on a shelf, in a building anybody could petition.

One of them was right. The other one would be believed.

And a district had to choose, and every person standing on that slipway understood — and had understood since about half past three the previous Tuesday — that the choice was not about the district's hours at all.
