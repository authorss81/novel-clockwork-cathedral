# Chapter 18 — Rumour With Page Numbers

The copies went out on Monday morning, eleven of them, and by four in the afternoon Hanna Stroud was holding one of them in a room off Weir Lane with a landlord's memorandum in the other hand, and the arithmetic had already been done for her by a young man of twenty who did not have the patience to do it slowly.

"Sit down," she said. "I want to say the whole thing to you in one go and then I am going to put it on the table and I am not going to be talked out of it, and you may have your say after, which will be recorded."

Orrin sat down. There were four other people in the room and he knew two of them: Teo Arden, against the wall with his hands behind him, and Wenna Pruitt, who had not sat.

Hanna Stroud owned nine blocks in the Weirs. She was fifty-four, she had started with one block and her mother's name on it, she paid her own renters on the first and had never once been two months late, and Orrin had done four clocks for her over the years and had always been paid the same day.

"This is a copy," she said, "of the hall's labour book. And I am not going to pretend to a man I don't know that I have not had it looked at twice by two people, because if I do, you will find out and then nothing I say in this room will be worth anything."

"Where did you get it?"

"It came into the estate office on Monday morning in an envelope with no name on it, and there are eight others in that building, and by the time I had it on my desk the other seven had already read it." She put it down on the table, square, and turned it round so that it was the right way up for him. "That is the Weir Two night pumping line. One crew, three nights, non-certified standby, and the same line eleven years running. And there is a Northglass boardinghouse stair, and there is a pumping station in the Sloughs, and there is one I can't read because somebody's water has got at it."

"I can," said Wenna Pruitt. "It's a laundry. Bellweather Road."

"A laundry — or a generator, I never saw the page whole." Hanna Stroud looked at her. "Then that's four, and I don't need four. I need one, and I have got one, and it is a pumping station in my district that a hall has written down as not existing for eleven years." She turned back to Orrin. "Mr Vale. I do not care one single farthing about your floodgate. What I care about is that I have a building on Carrow Street of thirty-one units and eight other buildings in this district, and I went to my insurer on Monday morning — I went on the Monday morning, before I'd had my tea, because that is what you do when you get a piece of paper like that — and I told them a pump station in the district had been running a crew on uncertified hours for eleven years, on a hall's own record, in writing, with a form number on it."

"And they said what?"

"They said the block is uninsurable on the present schedule, and that if the whole district is like it then the whole district is uninsurable, and that I can have cover at a different schedule at a different premium, and the different premium on thirty-one units in this district comes to about ninety pounds a year, and I have eight other blocks in the district and not one of them is in that quarter of it, which is the whole of the problem and is in your copybook in your own handwriting. and I have been going to put a rent increase on that block at Michaelmas because the block has been empty below the second floor since the spring." She was not raising her voice. "So I have taken the increase off, and the increase was going on all nine and it is going on eight, and I have told my agent to tell them the reason, and my agent will tell them the reason, because I am not going to lie to thirty-one people about money and then lie to them again when they ask why."

Nobody said anything.

"So there is your afternoon," she said. "Thirty-one households have not had a rise they were told they were getting. And I want you to understand, Mr Vale, that I did not do that because I am a hard woman. I did it because a piece of paper in a hall in Clockwork Quarter said that a pump station in this district has been running on hours nobody paid for for eleven years, and my insurer took it at face value, because why in God's name would I not? It is *their* book. It is the hall's own labour book. Who am I to hold a document like that cheaper than the people who keep it?"

---

"I told him to send it," said Teo Arden.

He said it from the wall without moving and he said it to the room, and Wenna Pruitt's head came round.

"You did not," she said.

"I did. Monday morning, before the shop opened. Eleven copies, and the eleven addresses are in a list, and the list is not here, and I did not take them off the hall's shelf myself, and I want that said first because Orrin has been standing in that corner for a quarter of an hour looking at me as though I stole it."

"Nobody thinks you stole it," said Orrin.

"I don't know what he thinks." Teo took his hands from behind him. "Mrs Stroud. I sent it to you because you have nine blocks and a good name and because you would do arithmetic instead of shouting, and you did arithmetic, and the arithmetic came out where I hoped it would come out, and that is the part I have got to say out loud in front of five people instead of at three in the morning."

"You hoped a block lost a rise."

"I hoped a district got *counted*," said Teo Arden, "and I sent eleven copies and eleven of them went to landlords and employers and a rates clerk and a man who writes for the Weirs Advertiser, and eight of the eleven did exactly what I asked and it cost a woman her rent increase, and I have been standing here for two hours working out whether I would send it again and I have not finished, and I am not going to finish in front of you."

Wenna Pruitt put her hand flat on the table.

"Fifty-one years in this family," she said. "Eleven of them mine and forty of them my aunt's, in this kitchen, on that table, with a lamp, though the part that goes back past the last war is a different book and nobody has read it since because somebody had to write down what happened to us. And it has never once been out of this room. Not out of this room and not out of a room, because a piece of paper with nobody's signature on it is worth nothing the second it leaves, and everybody who has ever told me otherwise has been selling something." She did not touch the copy on the table. "And now it is in nine buildings, and thirty-one women have been told that the reason they are not getting a rise is a pump station, and that is *true*, and it is the only true thing anybody has ever said to them about their rents, and it is going to be the reason in the Advertiser by Thursday."

"Wenna—"

"Don't." She put her finger down on the copy. "You are right. That is what I have got against you, so I am going to say it and then I am going to sit down. You are right and I have been wrong for eleven years and I said out loud in this kitchen to a man from the guild that a district that produces hours and cannot produce a signature will not be believed this century. And you have just proved it. You have proved it in the worst possible direction, at a table with a form on it, and the proof took ninety pounds a year off a block and it is *indistinguishable from a lie*, because it is the same paper and the same ink and it came in a plain envelope with no name on it, and if it had come with your name on the front of it and a stamp on the corner it would have been worth a hundred times more and it would have cost that woman the same money."

"That is not a fair thing to say to him," said one of the Loose Minutes, a man of about thirty with a bad hand and a good coat.

"It is the fairest thing anybody has said in this room for a year," said Wenna Pruitt.

---

They let the woman in at about half past five, because the woman had asked to come and had said why.

Hester Lomas was forty-four and she was a copyist at the Clockwork Quarter guild hall and she had been nineteen years in the back room, and she had copied the labour book fair, and she had signed every page as a true copy since before Orrin was apprenticed.

She was not a member of anything. She had come because Teo Arden had come to her and asked her, and she had said no for a day and a half, and then she had come.

"It is me who made the copies," she said. "All eleven. There is not a page in that book that my hand is not on at some point, and I did it in the back room on three nights, and I did it with the door shut and I did it after the licensing floor went and before the porter came, because I am not a fool and I did not want to be seen doing it and I want to be clear that I did not want to be seen."

"Then why did you do it?"

Hester Lomas looked at the table for a while.

"Because I have copied that book for nineteen years," she said, "and I have never once been asked what is in it. Fourpence a page and a signature, and you sign that it is a *true copy*, and I have signed the Weir Two line eleven times, and I have never known what a standby is." She took her gloves off. "I asked the steward in my second year what non-certified standby meant and he said it meant a machine that was standing by, and I wrote that down nowhere, because you do not write down things the steward says off the record, and I have had it in my head for nineteen years, and last week a boy of twenty sat on the end of my table and asked me whether I knew what I had been signing."

"They've let you go," said Wenna Pruitt. "I heard on Thursday."

"They have let me go." Hester Lomas said it without any weight at all. "For copying a document out of a room in a building where the document is on a shelf that anybody in the public may walk up to and read, and there is no rule against reading it and no rule against copying it, and I have read the licensing ordinance and I have asked a man who administers it and he says there's no rule. So I have lost nineteen years and there was no rule, and I would like somebody to write that down in the same kind of place, please, because in about forty years somebody is going to come along and do the same thing for a worse reason and there *will* be a rule by then, and I would like whoever writes it to know it took a woman's job and not a man's liberty and that I would do it again."

Nobody said anything. Outside, a district that was on time went on being on time.

"Now," said Hester Lomas. "I am going to say the last thing and I have not said it to him and I am not going to be fair about it. Mr Vale. You are not the man I am talking to, and it is worth saying so before I do, because there is one of you in this room who has been getting the credit for two days and he did not ask for it and did not do it.

"He came to me in the back room on a Wednesday and asked me whether I could take a fair copy of a page of the labour book out of the room in my own bag, and he asked it the way a man asks when he does not know what it costs, and I have been in this room nineteen years and I have never once had a man ask me for anything and be straight with me about what he wanted, and he was straight, and I have thought about it every day since, and it is the reason I did this. And what I want said in this room is that it cost me nineteen years and cost that boy nothing, because a boy of twenty has not got nineteen years yet, and one day he will, and on that day he will know exactly what I am talking about and it will be too late to be any use to him."

"But you did not send it. He did." She looked at Teo. "And that is the whole of the thing and I have watched all of you not say it, and I am going to say it. It went where it went because a boy in a hurry sent eleven copies to eleven addresses, and you were all standing here for two hours waiting to be the one who did not have to say it." She put her gloves back on. "Nine of the copies went somewhere useful. Two of them went to a man who sells the Advertiser's front page and he has had one since this morning. That is what I know and that is all I know, and if either of those things gets a woman on that block evicted then I put my name to the paper as well, and you can print that."

---

Orrin walked down Weir Lane at ten past six with the last of the light in the canal and did not go up to the shop, because his hand was still not closing properly on the Sunday and it was not much better on the Monday, and there was a difference between a bad hand and a hand that would drop a regulator pin into a movement, and he was not going to find out which he had in front of a customer.

He was thinking about a thing that had been said twice that afternoon and had not been joined together in anybody's head, including his own, until the middle of Weir Lane.

*A fact nobody can see is a rumour.*

*A district that can prove its own labour is a district that can be invoiced for it.*

Both of those were true. He had spent a month building the first and the second had been sitting in a kitchen on the same table the entire time, and a woman with a pair of scissors at her belt had said it eleven years ago, and he had written it down as a sad fact about the Weirs instead of as a warning about himself.

He got as far as the bridge and then the call-box at the corner had a boy from the hospital standing in it, which was the one on the hospital line and not on his, and he was told Mr Vale was wanted at the Green Span, and he went.

It was Leda, and she was not on the ward floor, and she said so first, before he asked, which was the thing about his sister.

"I'm not on until six. I'm in the corridor. Listen — the discharge pressure on the third-floor pump has been going down since Friday. The engineer is off until tomorrow and the estates man says it's the regulator, and the sister on nights has been compensating by hand, and the pharmacy has started drawing the insulin off the corridor clock instead of the ward clock because she trusts it more, and the corridor clock is the one that's eleven minutes out from the ward one." She let that sit. "I am not asking you for anything. I have not asked you for anything in a fortnight and I am not starting at eleven at night."

"Which pump is it."

"The one in the wet wall on the third-floor landing. It's the old one. It's not on anybody's rota." A pause, and then: "I only rang because I'm going to be there on Tuesday and I will be able to tell you what it sounds like, and you are the only person in this city I would tell for free."

"I'll come Tuesday," said Orrin. "Don't let anybody change anything because I said I'd come."

"I won't. And Orrin — I'm telling you and not asking you, and I would like you to notice the difference, because I have read what your rule says and your rule is about somebody who can object, and a ring at eleven at night from a woman who is not on your rota is the *cheapest* form of it there is and I am not going to let you pretend it was hard for me."

He stood on Ferrywright Bridge in the dark for a while after she had gone, with the tram wires hissing over his head and the river going under the deck toward the sea, and thought: on Tuesday I will put my hand on that pump and ask it one question, and it will cost me something, and I will be right, and there will be a woman in a corridor who is not on anybody's rota, and a room with an engineer in it, and an investigator, and a rule of his own that he wrote on Sunday and meant.

And none of those, he thought, is a licence.
