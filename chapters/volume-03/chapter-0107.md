# Chapter 107 — A Letter With a Date On It

On Thursday the third of the second month Corvin Hale wrote to the Weirs tenant council, in writing and with a date on it, asking whether he might ask the district about the drift of its clocks.

Orrin did not see the letter until Friday the fourth, when Wenna Pruitt showed it to him in the tenant council's rooms over the chandler's shop in Bellweather Road, with Sel Kade at the table and Ivy Pike at the window and nobody else, because a letter with a date on it is a thing a body answers and not a thing a man carries round.

The room was two rooms, knocked through in 1938 and never quite finished, a long table, a stove, a shelf of minute books in cloth that went back to the first of the first month, and a window that looked at a yard nobody used. Four people were entitled to be in it. Three were.

The letter was short, in a broker's plain hand, on arcade paper.

*To the Weirs tenant council, 3rd second. May I ask the district, on Pindar Row and elsewhere, what the drift of its clocks may be, for the purpose of my rate card, and may I put the answer on my board? C. Hale, No. 14.*

"It is polite," Wenna said. She was thirty-eight, organiser for nine years, not a member and never had been, because if she was a member she was inside the decision. "It names an act. It has a date. It asks a body that exists. Half the letters in this city do none of those."

"Is it expensive?" Ivy Pike said from the window. She was sixty-one, signed for a gate, chaired because a body that cannot be refused is not a body.

"It is one sheet of arcade paper and a walk to the post at the Weigh Lane end," Wenna said. "A man who wanted to be refused would have had to work at that."

Sel Kade read it twice. She was forty-four, a tenant-council representative since the seventh of the first month, on the body that held the new right, not chairing it. She had no water and no hours and was no longer the person who had to be asked, and she was the only person in the Weirs who could be asked about a market, because a tenant council can be asked about a room and a light on a stair and it cannot be asked one question about the water.

"What does the right say?" Ivy said.

Wenna turned the minute book to the five clauses. The fourth was the one that made it a right and nobody had argued about it: the council might not put a question to the row either, the row got seven days, the row might refuse the council, and there was nowhere to carry either refusal.

"He is asking the council whether he may ask the district," Sel said. "The council may refuse. A refusal binds the person who asked and nobody else. It is not a form, not a resolution, not a vote. It is entered in the row book and published with the minutes."

"That is a lawyer's reading," Ivy said. "That is a reading by a body that has found a thing it can say no to and would like to say it to somebody this month."

"It is the reading of the clause," Wenna said. "The fourth clause says the council might not put a question to the row. Hale is not the row. If we answer him, we are not answering him. We are asking the row what its drift is and using a broker to do it, and we would be doing it in a form the clause does not permit and in a way nobody in this room could defend at a meeting in eleven months."

"So the answer is no because the question was not addressed to us."

"The answer is no because the only way we could answer it yes is by doing the thing the clause forbids," Wenna said. "I have checked it twice and I would like somebody else to check it."

Sel checked it twice, slowly, and then Ivy checked it, and nobody said anything for a while, and out in the yard somebody moved a ladder.

"And if we refuse," Sel said, "will the row know anything? Any of this?"

"No," Wenna said. "It is a refusal of a permission. It says a man may not use this body. It does not say to the row that anybody asked and that anybody might."

"Then we are spending a right on ourselves," Ivy said, "and calling it defence."

"Yes," Wenna said. "I have been a committee organiser for nine years and I will tell you that this is what committees do on the day a clause first becomes usable. We spend it. The clause exists so that we can, and not spending it is not being careful, it is not having read it."

"And if we refuse, will he stop?" Orrin said from the corner, where he was not a member and had not asked to be.

"He will stop asking us," Wenna said. "Whether he stops asking other people is not in our power and never was. A right to refuse is neither a safeguard nor a shortcut. A safeguard is a rule about what a question has to be. A shortcut is a way of asking it without the rule. Clause four is what makes it a right, and clause four is also what makes it harmless, and both of those are the same sentence."

That was the end of it. They gave him the seven days that a schedule gets, although the letter asked for nothing that needed seven days, because the terms said seven days and terms the district could be held to were the only terms worth having.

Wenna wrote the entry while the three of them sat there, and the entry is four lines, and the fourth of them is the reason the seven days are there and not a figure.

*3rd second. Letter from C. Hale, No. 14 Pindar Row, asking leave to ask the district. Clause four permits no question to the row from this body. Permission refused. He is notified within the seven days. Entry published with the minutes.*

"Published," Ivy said.

"Published," Wenna said. "Not filed. There is a difference, and the difference is the seven days it took him to notice it."

"Seven days in which a man does as he likes."

"Seven days in which a man does as he likes and is known to have been refused, and that is the only part of a refusal anybody can be shown." She inked it and blotted it. "We are not a body that stops things, Mr Vale. We are a body that can be *shown* not having stopped them, once, in public, and a district that has been refused in public and left alone anyway is a different animal from a district that was never asked."

On Saturday the fifth Orrin walked Hale's pitch and found it shuttered, because the arcade trades on a Tuesday and a Friday and the Saturday pitch was barred and the rate card covered with a board the size of the trays. He wrote that in the book with more than satisfaction: a market that shuts is a market that can be believed about closing, and a man who can be believed about closing is a man who can be believed about the four and six.

Sunday the sixth he did nothing that needed a counter or a board, because there is nothing in this city that keeps a counter on a Sunday and there is nothing that should. He sat at the bench with the narrow book and the seven days running down in front of him, two used and five to go, and did the arithmetic of the refusal: a body of four people entitled, three entitled and present and one of those three not a member, spending its first use of a clause on a question it had not been asked and could not answer, so that the two hundred and forty households in four streets whose minutes the market is now trading in would learn nothing at all from the event, and the only man who would learn anything was the man who had been refused.

He wrote the seven days out in the book, and the number of the clause, and nothing at the bottom of it, because a thing that is not going anywhere should not be given a conclusion.

On the Monday and the Tuesday the council's door was open and nobody came in, and Orrin was in and out of the rooms over the chandler's four times on unrelated business that he manufactured himself, and on the Wednesday Ivy Pike asked him, without preamble, whether he thought a right that could not be used twice a year was a right or a decoration.

"I think nobody has used it twice in a year because nobody has been refused twice in a year," Orrin said. "Once it is a notice. Twice it is a procedure. The procedure is what you want and the procedure has never been tested, and the second refusal will not look like this one, because the second one will be about the water and not about a broker's card, and the row will know it, and so will he."

"And then?"

"And then you will find out whether the clause was a door or a wall," Orrin said, "and I have no idea which, and I would rather find out in a room with three women in it than in a report."

The council met on Thursday the tenth, in daylight, with the stove lit, and refused, in its own words, and the refusal bound Hale and nobody else.

They argued the wording for twenty minutes. Ivy wanted *the council is unable to assist*, which was in every form in the building. Wenna said no: a body that cannot assist is a body admitting it has no power, and the row had read that sentence in three other years and learned nothing from any of them. Sel wanted the shortest possible thing. Ivy wanted the longest thing that was true. They took the longest thing that was true, because a refusal nobody can misread is worth more than a refusal that reads well, and because the longest thing that was true had a date in it and a person in it.

Ivy moved it and Sel seconded it and Wenna abstained, and Orrin was not a member and did not vote and was in the room, and afterwards nobody in that room thanked anybody, which is a thing that happens in rooms like that one and not in rooms like a kitchen, and Orrin could not have said why and did not try.

The minute, published where anybody might read it, ran:

*3rd second asked, 10th second answered. C. Hale may not put to this body, nor to any person of Sluice Row, any question about the clocks of that row, nor the row's water, nor any hour belonging to a person of the row. Refusal binds C. Hale and nobody else. No person thanked.*

Five days after that, on the Tuesday the arcade opened, by Hale's own book a token would be in a clock on that row. Nothing in February anticipated that and nothing in December was contradicted by it. Orrin wrote the refusal in the grey ledger with both dates on it and put nothing at the bottom, because a refusal that binds one man is the whole of the instrument, and the instrument was new in January and this was the first time anybody with money had tested it.

Nobody in that room thanked anybody. Nobody in the shop thanked anybody. The clerk's minute went into the cloth-bound book and the copy went out on the Saturday notice run, and two hundred and forty households in four streets received it, and one of them was a street that had never been named in anything before it had a name in a minute.

Wenna had said the streets would not be named, and she had been right about the table, and the minute was a different animal. The minute named a man, a pitch and a street, because a minute that names nothing is a minute about nobody, and the whole of what a person gets from being refused in public is that the refusal is legible as being about them. The row got the day-book street and the notice run together, and by the middle of the following week two hundred and forty households knew that a street had been written down by somebody, and could not find out which, and would have found out in one afternoon at any counter on Pindar Row.

Teo came in at six that evening with the snow gone to water on his shoulders and read the minute over Orrin's shoulder without being asked, the way a man reads a thing he means to be able to repeat.

"Is that published anywhere it will be read?"

"Where anybody might read it."

"Then the broker's read it too," Teo said. "He reads everything anybody prints. That is his trade. You have spent a right on him. He will pay the seven days and he will not feel it and he will be somewhere else inside a week."

"That is the intention."

"That is the intention," Teo said, and went out, and the door went on its spring, and Orrin stood at the bench and understood, too late to do anything about it, that a right in this city could not tell anybody where to go next. It could only tell them where they had just been.
