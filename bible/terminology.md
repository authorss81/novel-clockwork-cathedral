# Terminology Bible

Use these words consistently. The system is physical and civic; do not replace it with unexplained game language.

## Core civic terms

### Cathedral of Consequence

The public building on Cathedral Rise that houses the Consequence Engine. It is civic infrastructure with religious and political symbolism, not a church that owns the city.

### Consequence Engine

The machine that runs a Reading. It uses the Civic Hours network and physical models to show likely consequences of a concrete proposed action.

### Local instrument

A small, manually calibrated Loom that examines one district's connected hourlines. It can return a Trace, Pressure, Blind margin, and Toll for a concrete local action, but it cannot use the central Dark Reservoir, the Censor Gate, or the First Hour. A local instrument requires the same three witnesses, draws its toll from the district it is connected to, and has no binding authority. It is a repair and planning tool, not a replacement citywide oracle.

Naming rule: the policy object is a **local instrument**, the machine inside it is a **local Loom**, and the room it stands in is a **Reading room**. Never write "local Reading instrument" in prose.

### Civic Time Keeper

An independent public officeholder responsible for keeping the Council of Hours honest about tolls, refusals, and emergency records. The office is not subordinate to the Cathedral Directorate or the operators. The first Civic Time Keeper is Mara Venn after the final reform.

### Civic Hours

Morrowfen's synchronized network of public clocks and civic machines. It distributes timing and some mechanical reserve; it is not an all-knowing network.

### Hourline

A physical line of copper, toothed metal, and timing pulses connecting a civic machine to the Civic Hours. An hourline carries mechanical load and strain, not thoughts.

### Reading

A single calculation and its result. A Reading is not prophecy. It is valid only for the conditions and records present when it was made.

### Question spindle

The brass mechanism that accepts a concrete action or proposal. A question must name something that can be attempted, tested, or observed. Questions such as “what is the moral thing to do?” produce no useful answer and a dangerous toll.

### Consequence Loom

The visible engine room in the Cathedral nave: water columns for flow, weighted arms for bridge and roof load, copper paths for clock networks, and pressure drums for human schedules. The Loom runs a proposal through several versions of the connected city and returns the response. “Loom” is also used for a district-sized instrument, so a small instrument is called a *local* Loom.

### Hour Compact

The founding charter of Morrowfen's civic time system, adopted forty-three years before the opening. Its public promise: the Cathedral will calculate the likely consequences of major civic decisions so citizens do not guess the price in private. Four safeguards are written into it — concrete questions, both benefit and cost shown, three independent witnesses, and a recorded physical toll. Later generations reduce it to a motto. Near the end it is restored as a public charter that opens with the names of the people who were omitted.

### Toll Stair

The public stairway in the Cathedral where operators display the minutes spent from district clocks after a Reading. It is the city's visible receipt and its best public performance. It shows mechanical totals only. A missing body toll never appears on it unless someone files an objection.

## Reading language

### The ninth beat

A low, round, sourceless pulse that arrives under a machine in Morrowfen and counts one over: eight, and then a ninth, coming up through a floor, a frame, or a case, with the wet sound of water coming back into a pipe it has been out of. It is heard exactly twice in the prose, in Chapter 1 under the Cathedral's crown case and in Chapter 3 through the pump house's own line, and never again inside Volume 01. It is **not a stage, not a power, and not Aftershock**, and the two must never be fused on the page: Aftershock is a load that answers a question asked in plain words about a named load, and it costs a keeper's toll every time, whereas the ninth beat answers nothing, predicts nothing, and costs nothing. On the page it leans — it goes on past the tick with a weight on it somewhere Orrin cannot name or locate — and it gives no picture of anything, no night, no face, and not one word. It is the volume's title and it stays unexplained through Chapter 50. Never write it as a prophecy, as a vision, as a warning, or as a free reading; nobody else in Morrowfen ever hears it.

### Trace

The strongest mechanically supported result under current conditions. A trace is still conditional. If the pressure changes, the trace changes.

### Pressure

A likely range of consequences rather than a single event. Pressure can be represented by color, gear speed, vibration, water height, or operator language. It is not a percentage and should not be reduced to a game statistic.

### Blind margin

The part of the real situation the machine does not represent well. A blind margin may be caused by unregistered people, missing maintenance records, a new machine, or behavior that changed after the Reading. It is a warning, not a guarantee that the omitted event is safe.

### Toll

The physical and personal cost of a Reading. A Toll Stair displays mechanical minutes; the full human cost is often hidden in operators' health records, missed shifts, memory gaps, and repairs.

### Clock toll

Reserve taken from clocks and civic machines. Symptoms include slow clocks, missed tram windows, pump lag, and factory schedule losses.

### Body toll

An interval of a living person's attention, coordination, or memory drawn into a synchronized Reading. Early symptoms resemble exhaustion. Severe hour-sickness leaves a lasting gap. It is not simply fatigue and cannot be cured by ordinary sleep.

### Keeper's toll

The smaller, unrecorded version of a body toll taken by a *private* reading. When a clockmaker listens to a machine without a witness rail, a question spindle, or a filed proposal, the toll comes out of the machine's own reserve first and then out of the reader's body. Nobody files it, nobody is paid for it, and it is the ordinary reason practitioners end up deaf, addled, and unable to explain what happened. Directorate reports call it *self-inflicted occupational strain*. This is the form of the cost that most of Morrowfen's clockmakers actually pay.

### Network toll

The strain a city-scale or district-scale Reading puts on synchronization itself. A slipped machine repeats a movement, skips a movement, or runs on a stale timing pattern. Hospitals, flood gates, and tram signals are the usual victims, which is why emergency Readings are politically charged. A network toll is visible as machinery failure and is often blamed on bad maintenance rather than on the Reading.

### Calibration strain

The Directorate's official term for a body toll. The phrase appears in health ledgers, operator certificates, and compensation denials for forty years. When a character uses it in conversation, they are usually repeating an institution's euphemism. A patient-advocate, nurse, or investigator using the phrase in front of Orrin is a provocation.

### Local toll

The minutes taken from a district's reserve to hold a working window open on a shared main or a shared line, so that a difficult repair can be done. A local toll is not a Reading and not a gate fee: it is small, it is a number of minutes, and it is still a **district** toll, which means it may only be certified by a licence above class three or by the district that owns the reserve. **On the page in Chapter 19**, in a landing, in the plainest language available: the window is bought with minutes, the minutes come off a district's reserve, and if a district's reserve is spent doing a thing for another district then that is a Reading. A class-three maintainer may certify the machine and may not certify the window. Certifying the window without standing to do it is the Volume 01 licence offence, and it is the one instrument that would have saved the Green Span pump.

### Received and noted

The Office of Public Reckoning's non-refusal, and the single most useful form in the book. A request goes in, and comes back inside a week, courteous, addressed, and carrying *no further correspondence on this matter* in the corner in small type. It is not a refusal: a refusal can be appealed. A note cannot. **On the page in Chapters 12, 13 and 19**, and it is the reason the Office is a joke in its own building and the reason a person in a room can still get everything the office cannot. A book about what a form is for should give this phrase its own entry.

### Prediction plate

The removable brass plate on a public clock face that carries the hour index, so that a clock can display the hour without a moving hand. A prediction plate is a physical part with a serial number and a workshop mark. Orrin removes one in the final image, which is why it must be established as an ordinary repairable object long before then.

### Witness bond

The physical link that lets the engine draw a body toll from a person when the connected district's clock reserve is empty. Operators wear a bond on the wrist during a witnessed Reading; it is a regulation, not a punishment, and every bond use is supposed to be recorded. Bonds are how hour-sickness becomes systematic rather than occasional.

### Split-hour pin

A repair device Orrin builds in Volume 04 and develops through the series. A pin that lets one assembly be driven at two deliberate rates, or let slip without dragging the rest of the network with it. It is the practical ancestor of the post-reform local instruments. It cannot decide anything; it only keeps a chosen disagreement from becoming a cascade.

### Hour token

A small brass marker stamped with a district and a number of minutes, cut from a clock's surplus or returned from a Reading toll. Tokens are how workers trade time that the toll system took from them, and later how the black market in Volume 03 buys it back. A token is a physical object with a punched value, never a currency unit or an abstract point.

### Witness

A person who physically identifies themselves to a Reading and accepts responsibility for recording what happened. The original system requires three witnesses, but later officials substitute permits and seals.

### Witness rail

The three-station brass rail in the Question Hall. The stations represent operator, affected resident, and public record keeper. A later legal change allows a permit to substitute for a resident witness.

### Certainty permit

A legal instrument allowing an official to cite a Reading as sufficient reason to act without public review. It is introduced as emergency procedure and becomes a tool of centralized control.

### Question Hall

The public room where a proposal is submitted and a Reading is witnessed. It is not a courtroom, though the Directorate increasingly treats it like one.

## Hidden or later terms

### Unlisted

A person, address, job, neighborhood, or property missing from the civic model. “Unlisted” does not mean nonexistent. The term becomes a political identity in the Weirs and a moral indictment in the public archive.

### Archive of Unlisted

The sealed rooms beneath the Cathedral holding the names, addresses, work shifts, and property records removed from the civic model. Built during the first years of the Censor Gate as a quarantine area for “unstable” entries, it later became the place where removed records were preserved. The rooms are not empty. Volume 02 opens the index; Volume 09 exposes what the records were actually used for.

### Archive room

The ordinary public record rooms on Cathedral Rise, staffed by record keepers and open to petition. Most citizens first meet the civic system here, arguing over a shift record, a toll receipt, or a permit. The Archive room and the Archive of Unlisted should never be confused in narration; the difference is the point of the plot.

### Unlisted record

A single entry in the Archive of Unlisted: one name, one address, one shift pattern, one property line. Records are the physical form of the central mystery. Several Volume 09 and Volume 12 scenes turn on one entry rather than the whole ledger.

### Censor Gate

A network of physical routing gates in the old waterworks and Cathedral foundations that removes unstable or excluded paths from the active Loom. Its underground return line carries the strain of the omitted work into the Dark Reservoir. The Gate was originally meant to filter impossible branches; later directors changed “unlikely” into “unregistered” and “politically inconvenient.”

### Dark Reservoir

A buried chamber where the physical stress of omitted paths accumulates. It does not contain a complete alternate world or hidden people. It contains excluded work, heat, damage, fatigue, and lost time. A weighted pressure line from the reservoir feeds back into the First Hour's safety rule: as accumulated stress rises, the rule becomes more willing to clamp divergent clocks and remove new paths.

### First Hour

The Cathedral's original self-preservation rule. It is a fixed anti-cascade sequence with a mechanical feedback threshold, not a mind: it compares the represented network, routes excess strain through the Censor Gate, and locks toward one sequence when disagreement threatens a cascade. Years of hidden reservoir pressure lower its tolerance for divergence. It is not a newly awakened god or a secret final villain; its rule is present in the founding machinery from the beginning.

The First Hour keeps no record of intentions, and it never learned anything. What it holds is a **calibration history**: a notched brass drum that encodes, as tolerances rather than as memories, what the founding Cooperative considered safe. Saying the First Hour “remembers” the Hour Compact is a character's metaphor, not canon. The machine holds tolerances, not memories, and any workshop bench with a gauge can read them.

### First Hour chamber

The original regulator room beneath the Cathedral nave, sealed by Directorate order 9-18 and opened only twice in this story: Volume 12, during a failing calibration, and Volume 14. It holds the anti-cascade rule, the threshold spring, the binding plates, and the upper end of the feedback line. It is a small, hot, badly ventilated room full of brass. It is not a cathedral within a cathedral and it has no religious fittings.

## The hidden hardware

These are the parts the ending acts on. They are ordinary machinery with numbers stamped on them, and every one of them is introduced long before Volume 14. Do not invent a new name for any of them late in the series.

### Return line

The buried line that carries the strain of omitted paths from the Censor Gate's routing gates down to the Dark Reservoir. It carries strain, heat, and lost time — never names, numbers, or intentions. It is a physical pipe with inspection hatches, and loose Minutes tuners have been stealing section caps off it for a decade without knowing what it carries. Damage to the return line costs the active Loom its safety margin and starts slippage, which is why breaking it is a tempting sabotage and a terrible fix.

### Calibration drum

A notched brass drum beside the First Hour that encodes the founding Compact as a set of tolerances: how much divergence the rule will tolerate before it clamps. Every tightening since the Long Nine is a notch cut by hand. Reading it is arithmetic, not divination, and Volume 12's recovery of its history is the closest the series comes to explaining what the machine was built to do.

### Routing plates

Removable brass plates in the Gate hub that decide which paths are routed away from the active Loom. They are the physical expression of a policy, and they are set by hand. Each plate is engraved with a category rather than a name — the earliest set reads *unstable path*; later sets read *unregistered* and then *not represented*. Volume 04 finds the hub. Volume 14 removes the plates. This is the only act that permanently disarms the Censor Gate; nothing upstream of the plates can be fixed by cutting power or breaking a gear.

### Gate hub

The sealed chamber under the Cathedral foundations where the routing plates are set and the return line begins. It is the room the Directorate has kept locked since the Long Nine, and the room Orrin enters twice: once in Volume 04, once in Volume 14. It is not a throne room and contains no throne, altar, or secret architecture.

### Feedback line

The rigid line running from the Dark Reservoir to the First Hour's housing. It carries a weight, not a signal.

### Feedback float

The weighted float that rides the surface of the reservoir and presses down on the feedback line. It rises as omitted work accumulates. It is the machine's only memory of the people it does not count, and it rises in proportion to their unpaid labor.

### Threshold spring

The adjustable spring that sets how much pressure the First Hour will tolerate before it treats divergence as cascade risk. It was set generously in the founding years. Every tightening since the Long Nine was done by hand, usually in the dark, always by an operator who believed the city was less stable than the last operator had believed. Volume 12 recovers the tightening history from the notches on the drum. The spring is the whole plot in one part.

### Binding plates

The First Hour's execution plates. They let an external order be *applied* to the rule as a command rather than merely recommended by it. The rule and the plates are different things: a safety rule that cannot be commanded is still dangerous, and a command that cannot be executed is only paperwork. Volume 12 exposes the plates as the reason a Continuity Minister can order a lock at all.

### Consensus lock

The First Hour's clamp action: it forces every connected hourline toward one sequence so that the network cannot disagree with itself. It is not a weapon and it does not destroy anything directly. It produces stability by removing people's ability to act on their own evidence, which is why its victims die later, at their convenience, in places nobody is looking.

### Command line

A physical request line from the Continuity Office to the First Hour chamber. During a declared emergency it can carry an authorized request for a consensus lock. It cannot make the rule choose a better path, and it cannot create the lock on its own; it only removes the need for a human being to be physically present to throw the handle. This is the reason the final volume's danger is political: the line survives decentralization, and it is the target of the reform's kill-switch.

### Kill-switch

The reform's physical answer to the command line: three separate release handles, held by three independent stations, any two of which must be pulled to sever the command line. A kill-switch cannot switch off a rule, a judgement, or a person. It can only remove the authority to give an order. That limitation is deliberate and must survive the ending: the Council of Hours is not able to make anyone stop arguing.

### Assurance copy

A quick unofficial re-run of a Reading made to reassure a frightened public without going through the question spindle and the witness rail. It is not a Reading, it carries no filed toll, and it is a common and usually well-meant practice until Volume 06, when a junior operator falsifies one to cover a genuinely bad result. The word is the Directorate's and should be said with a straight face.

### Non-certified standby

Guild bookkeeping for work that keeps a machine running but is not part of a certified shift. It is how a night pump crew is written off the books as not existing, which is how Perry Vance's crew was removed from the model. An inspector's phrase, and the phrase Orrin learns to search for. **On the page in Chapter 2**, as one line in a copying clerk's hand in the hall's labour book: *Weir Two, night pumping, non-certified standby, 1 crew, 3 nights.*

### Standing withdrawal

The guild request that removes a district's or a crew's standing from the civic model, recorded as work not done because there was no money to pay for doing it. Requested on a numbered form in the guild's own series, in the maintenance-deferred class, and priced per head per year. It is the paper side of the omission that the Censor Gate performs mechanically: the form says a crew was not there, the machine then has nothing to represent, and the hours become a saving. **On the page in Chapters 8 and 10.** The form series prefix is the guild's; the class number is fixed; the price schedule is the hall's own property and is not ashamed of itself. The withdrawal is lawful, priced, signed, and — this is the point — never described as a withdrawal anywhere a district would read it.

A standing withdrawal requires a countersignature from the affected district, which is how a district that has no standing still ends up with its name in the record. It also generates a routing card, because a transfer out of the public record has to be registered or the public record would simply have holes in it. **The routing card goes to the guild, the affected ward, the Continuity Office inspection file, and one public-health rota, and a rota is a list of volunteers' names.** That is how a woman on a rota ends up signing at a witness rail and on a routing card four removes apart from a night crew, and nobody connects them for twenty years.

### Continuity movement

The Assembly faction, led by Halden Rusk, that wants every hourline, reservoir, and Reading room under one office with the power to act in an emergency. Keep the three terms apart in every file, including outlines:

- **Continuity movement** — the people, the faction, the votes, the campaign. Capital C, lowercase m.
- **Continuity Office** — the executive office and, by extension, the building and the staff. It is the institution that acts, issues, certifies, and installs the command line.
- **Continuity Minister** — the post and its holder.

Do not write "Continuity faction" anywhere. In a list of factions, the movement belongs and the Office does not; in a list of locations, the Office building belongs and the movement does not. A character may blur them, because Rusk's supporters do, but narration and outlines may not.

### The Returning Tide

A storm surge, river swell, and an Outer Sluice failure arriving together. The name is the city's, used first by Northglass boatmen and only later by the Assembly. It is not a term the Cathedral uses, because the Cathedral does not forecast it usefully.

### No unseen cost

The Directorate's stated motto, carved above the Question Hall. It is true in the way a balance sheet is true.

### No visible dissent

What later generations discover the Directorate actually operated by. If a line is quoted in the series, give it to a named character who has a reason to be defending it, not to an anonymous narrator.

### Emergency Continuity Committee

The six-member joint body of Directorate officials and Assembly delegates that authorized the first emergency use of the Censor Gate during the Long Nine under Continuity Order 9-17. The committee thought it was filtering impossible branches. Halden Rusk was **not** one of the six: he was the Assembly's emergency secretary, the officer who drafted, numbered, circulated, and countersigned the order as the record of the proceedings. His guilt is procedural and institutional, not membership. Later directors expanded the order without renewed consent, and Rusk spent eighteen years defending that practice as good government.

### Continuity Order 9-17

The sealed emergency order that permitted the Long Nine bypass and the first Censor Gate exclusion. It authorized a temporary stability rule, not the permanent removal of residents from civic life. The distinction was lost when the order became routine procedure.

### The Long Nine

The nine-minute citywide clock and communications failure eighteen years before the opening. Officially blamed on a storm and sabotage. The hidden cause was a Censor Gate that made a clean flood decision possible only by excluding unregistered people and labor, combined with a regulator's manual bypass.

### Slippage

A machine or person loses synchronization and repeats, skips, or delays an action. It is the physical expression of a Reading or clock toll going beyond safe capacity.

### Hour-sickness

The collection of physical and cognitive effects caused by a body toll or severe synchronization. Symptoms can include vertigo, ringing ears, metallic taste, disorientation, loss of coordination, and a missing interval of lived attention.

### Open Interval

A planned period during which local clocks and civic systems operate without the Cathedral's binding forecast. It is not an absence of danger. It is a shared decision to act with incomplete information.

## Factions

### Cathedral Directorate

The appointed body that operates and governs the Cathedral. It includes engineers, operators, physicians, jurists, and record keepers. Its actual authority exceeds its public description.

### Office of Public Reckoning

Morrowfen's small audit office. It reviews tolls, public decisions, and complaints. Mara Venn is its investigator.

### Guild of Horologists

The licensed repair guild for civic clocks and hourline equipment. The guild is necessary and compromised.

### The Loose Minutes

An informal network of unlicensed tuners, archivists, workers, and neighbors who recover discarded hour tokens and share censored information. Their methods range from repair to theft.

## Places

Use these names exactly. Do not invent a new district in a late volume.

### Morrowfen

The city. Roughly 2.4 million people on a reclaimed tidal basin between the **Sable River** and the **Morrow Sound**.

### Clockwork Quarter

The repair and manufacturing district: guild halls, workshops, apartment blocks, and yard space. Orrin's bench, the guild hall, and most small horology shops are here. Its public clock is the most accurate clock in the city and the reason its residents distrust everyone else's.

### Cathedral Rise

The civic hill: the Cathedral, the public archives, and the Assembly chambers.

### The Weirs

The low districts built around floodgates, old canals, and pumping stations. Densely populated, poorly represented, and not a single political movement. They are central to the labor and consequences omitted by the model. Named in the plural on purpose; "the Weirs" is a place, not a people, and characters who treat it as a bloc are usually about to be wrong.

### The Lower Weirs

The pumping district at the bottom of the Weirs, where the floodgates and the night pump crews are. This is where the Volume 01 Reading is filed and where Perry Vance works. Use *the Weirs* for the whole low region and *the Lower Weirs* for the pumping district.

### Weir Two

The floodgate in the Lower Weirs at the center of the opening Reading. There are Weirs One through Four; only Two is named on the page, because Two is the one whose night crew is left out of the model. It is repaired in Volume 01's resolution, and it sits upstream of the flood doors that matter in Volume 14.

### Green Span

Hospitals, schools, market gardens, and the city's largest public clock. The most politically organized district and the least tolerant of operational risk.

### Northglass

Rail yards, glassworks, warehouses, and the regional power exchange. Also home to the **Northglass flood basin**: low, engineered, and usually empty industrial land that is the only plausible place to put a large volume of river water by hand. Identifying the basin as the emergency relief site is a Volume 07 beat, and its emptiness on the night of the Returning Tide is a fact the reader learns early.

### The Sloughs

The tidal marsh and road district at the seaward edge, including the road out to Bellweather, Harrow, and Saint Perrin. Formerly called the Outer Sluice, which was renamed after the confusion in the first draft: the **Outer Sluice** is the structure, the Sloughs are the land.

### The Outer Sluice

The tidal defense works at the seaward edge. One structure, not a district. Its inspection interval is eight years, and its last full inspection is deferred in Volume 06 and never completed. Its failure on the night of the Returning Tide is the direct result of a decision shown on the page in Volume 06.

### Sluice bypass

The overflow channel and manual gate through the Outer Sluice, built for exactly this night. Using it in Volume 14 requires the Weirs' own crews to clear it by hand.

### The Undercity

Sealed pre-Cathedral foundations, service tunnels, and the administrative rooms under the reservoir. Official maps show it filled. The return line, the reservoir galleries, and the route to the Gate hub all run through it.

### The Weirs flood doors

The old tide doors in the Weirs seawall, built for a smaller tide and not tested in the decades since. Surveyed in Volume 07, found to be untested and unrecorded, and finally opened by the Weirs' own crews in Volume 14.

### Ferrywright Bridge

A low iron road bridge in the Weirs, the subject of the opening Reading in Volume 01. It survives the series. It is not the Northglass rail bridge and not the hospital trestle.

### The hospital trestle

An old iron trolley trestle carrying the Green Span hospital line over the canal. The Volume 01 climax is about its failing load starving the hospital's clock pressure. It is repaired, not destroyed, and it is not the Northglass rail bridge.

### The Northglass rail bridge

The single rail crossing of the river below the city. Named in Volume 07 as the only crossing heavy enough to move rail load. Torn out and lost in Volume 14 so the river can pass. No earlier volume damages it, and no volume destroys a different bridge in its place.

### Vale's Bench

Orrin's two-bench repair shop in the Clockwork Quarter. It is a business with a sign, a work ledger, and a lease, not a base of operations.

## Institutions and public bodies

### The Assembly

The Morrowfen Assembly: forty-eight ward delegates who control budgets, public works, and the legal use of the Cathedral. Written **Assembly** when it is acting as a body, **the Assembly** when referring to the institution generally, and a named councilor when referring to a person. Roughly a fifth of the series runs on Assembly procedure, so keep the vocabulary small and consistent: debate, division, division lost, division carried, referred to committee, read twice, entered in the minutes, discharged.

### Civic Calculation Cooperative

The founding body that built and operated the Consequence Engine: engineers, physicians, jurists, teachers, and elected ward delegates together, with no single owner. It dissolved into the Directorate within a decade. Its papers, its founding tolerances, and its meeting minutes are the deepest layer of the record, and most of them cannot be recovered.

### Continuity Office

Created six years before the opening by an Assembly majority under the banner of never again, with authority over regional water, power, and emergency scheduling. It gained inspection authority over every hourline four years before the opening, which is when Morrowfen's clockmakers first had to answer to it. It is the office that installs the command line, and it is the political home of the final human antagonist, Halden Rusk. Its continuing weapon is the emergency clause of the regional scheduling act, which no earlier reform touches.

### Continuity Minister

The cabinet post Rusk has held for the two years before the opening and widens through the later volumes. He is not the Director of the Cathedral, not an Assembly delegate after Volume 11, and never an operator. His authority is legal and administrative: he can declare an emergency, invoke a surviving continuity instrument, and appoint inspectors.

### Continuity instrument

The surviving legal basis Rusk invokes in Volume 14. It is not the Hour Compact and not a certainty permit: it is the emergency clause of the regional water and power scheduling act, which permits a declared regional emergency to be answered "by any measure the responsible office certifies is necessary." The clause predates the reforms and cannot be repealed by the Assembly alone, which is why decentralization does not end the threat. **Two on-page plantings are assigned and both must happen:** Volume 06, read aloud by a clerk over the diverted maintenance budget while the Outer Sluice inspection is deferred, and Volume 11, cited by section number by Rusk over the same Sluice minutes. Without them the climax introduces a legal instrument the night it is used, and the final volume's danger reads as a new rule instead of an old one.

### Emergency declaration

The formal act that arms the command line. It requires a named responsible office, a written finding, and a published duration. Emergency declarations expire, are contested, and are the one legal instrument every faction in the series both fears and reaches for.

## Reform-era terms

These appear late and are not to be used before the reform pressures of Volumes 06–13 make them plausible. Do not let characters say “Council of Hours” in Volume 01.

### District time council

A local body formed after decentralization, holding a share of authority over a district's clocks, its toll fund, and its decisions to call a local instrument. Membership is contested, meetings are public and often badly attended, and the councils disagree with each other on purpose. The Weirs council is the strongest and the most suspicious of centralized authority.

Three Weirs bodies exist and must not be confused:

- The **tenant councils** and **mutuals** are old. They predate the Cathedral, fight landlords, and are the Weirs' real social infrastructure. Sel Kade becomes a tenant-council representative in Volume 02.
- The **Weirs tenant council** is the standing body of the pumping district, and it is the one that consents to open the flood doors in Volume 14.
- The **Weirs district time council** is the later elected clock-and-toll body created in Volume 10. It is separate from the tenant councils, it is frequently at odds with them, and "the Weirs council" in the final volume means this one unless the sentence is about housing or rent.

### Reading room

A licensed local workshop where a local instrument can be used under public rules. Licensed Reading rooms spread through Volumes 03, 06, and 10. The first informal, unlicensed version of the idea is Teo's in Volume 02, and calling that a Reading room is exactly the error that Volume 03's licensing fight is about. They are not churches and not clinics. A room with a cracked window and a hand-cranked reserve meter is more representative of the series than a grand hall.

### Council of Hours

The public institution created in Volume 14 to govern the civic time network after the Returning Tide. It is built from rotating district delegates, operators with no command authority, medical and maintenance representatives, and independent record keepers. It publishes tolls, omissions, emergency actions, and refusals; protects a maintenance budget from emergency diversion; and holds a physical kill-switch split among three independent stations. It cannot promise a safe Reading and cannot turn a forecast into an order. Its weakness, deliberately kept, is that it is slow and can be argued with.

## Character titles

Use these exactly. A character's title is a limit on their authority, and Volume 01's legal conflicts turn on getting them right.

### Line horologist

A clockmaker trained to work on civic hourlines rather than private clocks. Orrin uses this term for his profession; ordinary residents usually say clockmaker.

### Consequence operator

A trained person who can prepare and interpret a Reading. Operators are not interchangeable: they have different specialties, tolerances, and political loyalties. Ivo Pell is the volume-one example of what an operator's authority actually protects.

### Clockwarden

## Power stages

Orrin's seven stages are named once, here, and used consistently in prose and outlines. Do not invent stage names, and do not reorder them. The order is the order they are earned in.

1. **Line sense** — hears imbalance in a machine he is touching. Earned before the opening.
2. **Aftershock** — feels the next short-term failure of a named load on one connected machine. Earned in Volume 01.
3. **Choice trace** — connected to the Loom, senses the three strongest pressure paths of a concrete proposal. Earned in Volume 02.
4. **Civic rehearsal** — with independent hourlines and other operators, maps a proposal across districts. First attempt in Volume 02, a shared method he does not control in Volume 03, stable from Volume 05. It is awarded once, in Volume 05; Volume 03 is the beat where it becomes usable, not the beat where it is earned.
5. **Counterweight ward** — alters a physical machine so a consequence is moved, reduced, or made survivable. First built in Volume 04, mature in Volumes 09–12.
6. **Distributed horizon** — helps a group combine partial readings into an inspectable public map. First attempt in Volume 06, stable in Volume 10.
7. **Open interval** — decouples the central binding forecast from local clocks for a planned period. Practiced in Volume 13, used in Volume 14.

Counterweight ward and distributed horizon are frequently confused in discussion because both are about sharing. A ward moves load through a machine. A horizon moves interpretation through people. Keep them distinct in dialogue.

## Reading notice format

The Engine can display one sparse notice. It must remain plain, physical, and limited. Use at most one notice per chapter unless the chapter is specifically about multiple machines producing contradictory notices.

```text
QUESTION RECEIVED
TRACE: [one supported result]
PRESSURE: [one likely range]
BLIND MARGIN: [what is missing]
TOLL: [one readable cost]
```

Do not use: status, skill, level, quest, XP, cooldown, health bar, currency, API, protocol, backend, runtime, token latency, or deployment. A missing interval is a time cost, not a software token. A “token” in Morrowfen is a physical brass marker used by operators and workers; it must never be used as a generic game currency.

## Prose usage

- Prefer “Reading,” “clock,” “machine,” “district,” “reserve,” “witness,” and “toll” over vague fantasy synonyms.
- Use “forecast” for the ordinary public word. Use “Reading” when the Cathedral's formal process is involved.
- Use “pressure” for likelihood and “consequence” for the result. Do not describe the machine as “predicting” unless a character is speaking imprecisely.
- A blind margin is an omission, not a hidden stat waiting to be unlocked.
- If a term is newly revealed, show it through a physical object, job task, legal argument, or consequence before giving a long definition.

- **The Water Users' Committee.** Constituted under the water act for a district where more than a certain number of properties draw on a common main. **Membership is by property qualification** — the freehold or lease of a property that draws on the main — and **there is no form on which a night worker could be put on it**, because an uncertified man who is not a ratepayer at that address has neither. A man may ask the counter for a blank on any working day, free, on no form, and **the hall cannot write a rule to stop it and does not want to.** The hall's return copy — the second sheet, not asked for by anybody and handed over by a clerk who can see you have not thought of it — must be returned with the book.

Three requests on one Monday morning and three identical cards on the mat by a quarter past two. **The hall's defence is a definition, and a definition that is true cannot be beaten by producing more evidence.** A drawer on the second floor of the Office of Public Reckoning held eleven of them by the end of the batch.

Seven issues ran; the copyist is owed eleven shillings for a fortnight; a binder of the run is in a room off Weir Lane. **A printed page has no address. It cannot be served, it cannot be refused, and it cannot be contradicted, because there is nobody to contradict.**

- **A man of fifty-one** in the works, who packed the iron seat in lead in five courses with a drift and showed the other four how and did it anyway.
- **A woman of about sixty** with a shorthand book, not in the council, who took the minute of the first tenant review for eleven shillings out of an empty tin.
- **Ostrow,** the Toll Stair chalker, fifty-four, nineteen years, scrubs at half past five and chalks at six; on the sixth of the sixth month he is told by a woman from the Office that there will be two more columns, and he puts the chamois against the empty one and takes it away again and leaves it.
- **A clockfitter out of Verge Lane,** who chalked the stair's figures on six mornings for six shillings and would not take a note, and who put the name in the new column because Ostrow asked him.
