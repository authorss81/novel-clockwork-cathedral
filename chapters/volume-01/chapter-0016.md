# Chapter 16 — Deferred, for Cost

The Continuity Office file room was on the second floor of a building on the Rise with a brass rail outside it and a clock over the rail that was eleven seconds slow, and the clerk who let them in said that the clock was eleven seconds slow and had been for years and was the Office's business and not hers.

There was one table, four chairs, and no fire, and a window that looked at the wall of the next building, and the file came up from the cellar on a board on a boy's shoulders and made the whole room smell of the cellar.

"Seventeen forty-one to seventeen fifty-eight," said Mara Venn. "The pumping district, hourline inspections, transfers and maintenance deferrals. It is a fortnight's work and a morning's read, and we have the whole morning, and I would take the deferrals first, and then the transfers, and then the inspections if there is anything left."

"Why first?"

"Because the deferrals are money and money is the only part of this that anybody in the building will defend," she said. "And I would like to be able to defend it with them."

---

The trestle was in the file under *Green Span, hospital line, canal crossing, structure*, and the deferral was on the eleventh page, and it took Orrin four seconds to find because the file was set out by date and by date it was two years old and it was in a run of nine.

> *Green Span hospital line. Trolley trestle, canal crossing. Conduit supports, east span, four fixings.*
> *Inspection, 2nd month, two years past, at annual line survey. Two of four fixings withdrawn from the castings. Span observed dropped three-eighths of an inch at the third bracket. Third bracket of obsolete pattern, body grey, casting unsound.*
> *Recommendation: renew. Estimate obtained: £2 14s 6d, inclusive of new pattern from the original drawing.*
> *Deferred. District works programme, next triennium.*

Underneath that, in a different hand and a lighter ink, the carry-forward:

> *Carried forward. District works programme, next triennium.*

And then, eleven months later, in the same lighter hand:

> *Carried forward. District works programme, next triennium.*

"Two years and two months," said Orrin.

"Two years, almost to the month, and two carryings-forward, and a bracket that Bevin Sarr told me on the Sunday night was two years away from putting a bridge in the canal." He had his thumb on the entry and he was not moving it. "It says *next triennium*. It says that twice, and it has not been a triennium since, which is the part nobody writes down. It is not a refusal. It is a thing that was put in a programme, and the programme came round, and the programme did not have it in it, and nobody has to be wicked for that at all."

He turned the page and there was a routing sheet, thin, card, four lines, and he read it before he could decide not to.

> *Copies: Guild of Horologists, Clockwork Quarter. District works, Green Span. This Office, inspection file. Public-health rota, Green Span, 1st Weds.*

The same four. The hall, the district affected, the inspection file, and a rota of volunteers' names.

"That is a coincidence of procedure," said Mara.

"That is a *routing practice*," said Orrin, "and the fourth one on it is a list of names of women, and there is a woman on a list like that — a woman who sat at a witness rail a fortnight and a day ago, and who told a clerk eight days ago that she had not understood a word of what she signed, and who had it written down, and whose name is on a card in the Archive room, four removes away from a night crew, and whom nobody has connected to anything." He turned the card over. "There is nothing on the back. It is a card. It goes round because a thing that leaves the record has to be registered, and it goes to a rota because a district that has lost people needs its health visitors told. That is the sentence. That is the whole sentence and it has been the sentence for eighteen years."

He put the routing sheet down carefully on top of the file and squared it, and then he went on turning pages, and about a third of the way through the volume he stopped dead.

Batch 21/4. Weirs series. Forty-one entries. Standing withdrawal, form GH-9, maintenance-deferred class. Countersigned for the affected district by the pumping warden of the Lower Weirs, deputy.

*S. Kade.*

Eighteen years ago, in the storm month, when she was twenty-six years old and had not been the warden of anything, and had signed a piece of paper that said the Weir Two night crew was not there.

"She does not know," said Orrin.

"No," said Mara. "She has never been told. You have never told her. I have never told her, and I did not tell her in Lake's room because you were in the room and it was not mine."

"I know."

"Orrin." Mara Venn did not raise her voice. "Say what you are about to do, out loud, the way your sister made you say it, because I have watched you for a fortnight and you are about to decide something about another woman's name in a room with nobody in it but me, and you have been making decisions about other people's names in rooms like this for three weeks."

"I am not going to tell her."

"All right."

"I am not going to tell her because she has a tenant review coming, and it is not this month, and if I put a piece of paper in front of her now that says she signed this when she was twenty-six, she will be asked about it by a man who is not me and she will not be able to answer it, and there is nothing we have that can be asked about her in a room where she is not represented."

"That is a good reason," said Mara. "It is also a reason with a door in it, and you are standing on the wrong side of it, and the door is going to be a rule, and it is not going to be the one you are expecting, and you are going to write it because of this page."

"Then I shall have to remember it," said Orrin Vale, and turned the page, and did not copy the entry down.

---

The inspection at the end was Rill's, of course, and Orrin read all three of his readings in the pumping district in a single sitting without any difficulty at all, and none of them were a fault, and the word *consistent* was on the page four times.

"Everything he writes is true," he said.

"Everything he writes is true, and he is the only man in this city who has written down, in a document, that there is no shift record for the Weir Two night crew, and it cost him a column and about a page." Mara was copying steadily into a notebook, in her own hand, because the Office would not supply a copy. "That is a better piece of paper than anything you have found, and you will not be able to use it, and I want you to sit with that for a moment before you tell me the rest of it."

"Which rest?"

"The part where you have been looking at a file for two hours like a man reading a price tag, and have not said a word about the estimate." She wrote for another line. "It is two pounds fourteen and sixpence, Orrin. For a bracket that nobody could make because nobody has had the pattern made since the pattern was current, and for the pattern, off a drawing somebody has in a drawer in Green Span."

"Yes."

"And on the twenty-first of the month, in the small hours, that bracket was out on a bridge in the rain, and two unlicensed men with a hammer and a drift got the third fixing out in three pieces, and a bearing block off a dead tram bogie went in its place, and a plate with a boy's name on the back of it went on the front of it." She stopped writing. "And that cost nine men an hour of their Sunday morning and a ward forty minutes of slack and about two hours unbudgeted, and a third fixing broken in two in a woman's fist, and two days of a clockmaker's right hand."

"Yes."

"So the estimate was right, and the survey was right, and the man who signed it was right, and nobody did anything wrong, and the first night it cost nine men an hour of their Sunday morning and a ward forty minutes of slack, and neither of those figures was in the estimate, because neither of them is a thing an estimate has a column for." She put the pen down. "Because the estimate was the bracket and the cost was everything the bracket was standing on. And there is no column anywhere in Morrowfen, Orrin, not in that file, not in the guild's, not in mine, for the difference between what a thing costs and what the thing was holding up."

---

They were in the corridor outside the file room at twenty past eleven with two notebooks full and a bad feeling between them, and the corridor was not private: there were four men going to and from the Continuity Office's front desk and every one of them would have been within hearing.

Mara said, "I want to use the deferral."

"You do not."

"I want to use the deferral and not the form. That is the whole of my position and I have had it since the seventh page and I would like you to let me state it properly in a stairwell instead of a corridor, and then I want you to tell me why you won't, and I am not going to be rude about it."

"Fine. Corridor."

"A form that removed a crew is a moral instrument." She said it evenly, and precisely, in the voice she used for reading out a document. "It is priced at two shillings the head for a year, and the price is the argument, and the argument is that a hall made money by writing men off. To say that in a chamber you have to say a hall *intended* to write men off, and a district cannot prove intent, and no office can test it, and the moment you have to allege intent you have handed the matter to a lawyer and lost the reader. It is lost, Orrin. It is lost in the way that everything gets lost that asks a room to guess at a motive.

"The deferral does not ask a room to guess at anything. It has a date, an estimate, a recommendation, a carrying-forward twice, and a signature, and it is lawful, and it concerns *money*, and there is not one member of any committee in this city who will defend an item of expenditure. It is the easiest document in the world to attack without attacking a single man, and it puts the price of a bracket and the price of a night's labour on the same table, and the table does the rest."

"Then the bracket is the case," said Orrin.

"The bracket is the *case*," she said. "Perry Vance is the *reason*."

"He is the only thing there is." He had the notebook in his hand and he did not open it. "Mara, look at what you have written in that book. You have got a woman's signature from eighteen years ago when she was twenty-six and a bracket that nobody renewed. And of the two, which one do you think somebody is going to be asked about at the end of it? The bracket has got a price tag on it and it is two pounds fourteen and sixpence and I can tell you exactly what it cost in one night, and by Christmas a committee will have the bracket nailed to a wall in a corridor and the file room will be full of men discussing a bridge, and in five years there will be a deferred-maintenance schedule in the budget with a line in it and a lecture about the danger of starving the works, and there will not be one single person in this city who has ever heard the name of the man who died."

"And if we do not use the deferral, what do we use?"

"The copybook. The nine series. The form number. The price." He was aware that he was not being calm and did not care. "We say: a hall has a book, and in the book a line, and the line is a man, and the line has been copied eleven times, and it was copied by a clerk who did not know, and it was priced by a schedule that is the hall's own property and is not ashamed of itself. That is a fact. Facts do not have to be guessed at. And it puts the Weirs in the frame instead of a piece of cast iron on a bridge nobody looks at."

"It puts a district that has no standing in the frame," said Mara Venn. "It puts a district in the frame and the district gets a lawyer and a lawyer gets a decade, and the decade is how the Long Nine has stayed unlit. And I am not saying the copybook is wrong. I am saying the copybook is *nine districts on a form*, and you spent two days last month working out what happens to a district that is on the same page as a man who drowned, and you are now proposing to put eight of them there."

Orrin did not answer that, and the silence went on long enough that two men going past the door glanced at both of them, and then Mara said:

"Right. That's where we are, and we are not going to settle it on a landing, and I am not going to pretend we settled it. I am going to say one thing more and then I am going to go and file an objection about the two minutes, because I have now got the receipt that says three, your certificate that says the Quarter is correct, a street clock that is five out, and a file of deferrals that puts a price on the bracket I replaced on the night of the twenty-first. Four documents that do not agree, and I want it in the record that they disagree, and then I am going to go home and sleep for nine hours and I am not going to think about it."

"What is the thing?"

"Use the deferral." She was already writing on her cuff. "Not instead of the copybook. First. Because the copybook is only going to be believed by a room that has already been shown what a document costs, and you cannot get a room to look at a price schedule until it has understood that a schedule is a thing with a total in it, and the bridge is how it understands, and if it takes three years to get to the copybook through the bridge then the copybook is three years late, and I would rather be late and correct than punctual and lost."

"Three years," said Orrin.

"Three years, and the ninth, and the Assembly in a room being bored by a bridge, and then somebody in that room says — what's the form number on that? — and a clerk looks it up." She put the notebook under her arm. "That is the whole of my method and I have been doing it for nine years and it is not elegant and I have never told another investigator about it because it is embarrassing. Do you want the fifth or the sixth."

"Ninth," said Orrin.

"Ninth," said Mara Venn. "Then we agree about the bracket, which is not the same as agreeing about anything, and I am going to write that down too, in my own book, in a room with nobody in it, and if you tell anybody I said it I shall deny it and be believed."

She went down the stairs. He stood in the corridor with the notebooks and the elevation of a two-pound bracket and the two-year-old date of a survey that had been right, and worked out what he was going to practise on.
