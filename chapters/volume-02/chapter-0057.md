# Chapter 57 — Pump Two Before Pump One

On the night of Monday the twenty-seventh of the sixth month, between about eleven o'clock and half past one, the second stage at Weir Two was started twice, and the second start was into a pump that was already running, and nothing in the district's control system objected to either, because the interlock is a time interlock and a time interlock cannot tell a fourth draw from a fault.

---

Item seven of the list said: *Weir Two. Night of the twenty-seventh, from eleven. Bring nothing. Stand where you are told.*

He was on the step of the pump house at about ten to eleven and Teo Arden was not there and nobody had told him who would be, and there is a line in the middle of Teo Arden's letter which says *you will find out on the day who gives the orders and it will not be me*, and that line turned out to be worth exactly what it says.

What happened at about ten past eleven is that Marta Dey, who is fifty-eight and has a bad hip and is a pumping ward's *engineer's assistant* and has held that title for eleven years in a building that has never put it on a door, came out of the office and said, in a voice that could be heard over the second stage, "You. Go and stand by the far gauge with the notebook and do not touch anything and if I tell you a number, write the number."

He wrote the numbers.

---

The interlock is on the drawing. It is a box on a 1931 sheet in the district works' file with a word and a figure in it and the word is *interlock* and the figure is **20 min**, and the leader line goes to a contact on the second stage's starter and to a clock, and the clock is on the branch.

There is no pressure interlock on that machine and there has not been one since the sheet was drawn, and the reason is on the sheet in a different hand, in pencil, and it reads: *pr. intlk. no — trips main prot. Do not fit.*

A pressure interlock on a pumping main of that age would trip the main's own protection, and the man who wrote that in pencil twenty-four years ago was correct, and nobody has ever replaced him. So the second stage is held off for twenty minutes after the first stage starts, and the twenty minutes is counted by a clock, and the clock is the branch, and the branch is the branch.

And on the seventeenth of the fourth month the Water Users' Committee put the front's night draw back on a fourth cycle, and the standing rotation the district fell back on at midnight on the thirtieth of the fifth has four draws in it as well, and **a station surveyed for two cycles a day has been given four, and a clock-driven interlock does not refuse a rhythm.** It does not know that the extra two are a committee's decision and not a fault. It knows that it has been asked to move, and it moves, and it moves at the same twenty minutes every time, and on a second cycle inside an hour it starts the second stage again.

---

At about eleven twenty the second stage started. At about eleven fifty it started again.

Marta Dey said one word, and nobody has written it down, and it was not a technical word.

At about ten to twelve the second stage's gland let its seal water go, and Marta Dey was at the machine with a spanner before it had finished running down, and Orrin Vale wrote the number she called and the number was the seal water pressure, and it was nil.

"What is it now," she said at about ten past twelve.

He put his hand on the case of the second stage's delivery gauge, which is a machine he has touched four times in his life and which is connected to the branch, and he asked one question out loud, in plain words, about a named load, which is four words long and which he has never broken in public.

**What is about to let go.**

He knew what it would cost. He had been doing it since the seventeenth of the second month and the price of a well-asked question about a named load on a connected machine was a hundred and fifty seconds the last time, and this was a smaller question and a better one and the thing that came back was a part and not a state.

*A washer. A gland follower. And the delivery, behind it, and not the delivery on the drawing.*

A hundred and five seconds. He wrote it in the shop book in the pumping ward office at about half past two that morning with the reason written under it.

And by the time he had it, the repair was being done by four other people and he was not in it.

---

**What the repair was:** a gland follower, a new washer of a size that had to come out of a box in the works' store, a belt off the second stage's drive that had to be shortened by an inch and a quarter with a file on a vice in the office, four gallons of clean water from the standpipe in the yard, and eleven minutes' work by a woman of fifty-eight with a bad hip who would not let anybody else hold the spanner, and about two hundredweight of the second stage on a chain block borrowed from the boat-builder on the canal road, which came round at half past midnight without being asked and would not take money and said a boat-builder's tackle is for boats.

**He was not needed for any of it. He was needed for the far gauge and the notebook, and then for about nine minutes at about half past twelve when the front's pressure came back at about thirty-one pounds instead of forty to forty-two, and the drop is what put the water into a cellar four streets away.**

---

**Nine Weir Lane.** That is not 14 Ferrywright Row, which is a different street, and it is not a burst, and there is no hole.

The old house has a cast-iron soil under the front rooms that was laid when the house was built and has been weeping into a puddle under the board for about sixty years, and the puddle has a channel cut for it in the flagstones by a man who knew, and it has never been more than about two inches, and the house has never been flooded because a main at forty-one pounds does not put water through a hundred-year-old crack in a soil pipe at any rate a person notices.

The second stage lost its seal water at about ten to twelve and the front's pressure went from forty-one to thirty-one and stayed there for about fifty minutes, and thirty-one is seven pounds more than a hundred-year-old crack in a soil pipe was ever designed to see, and at about half past twelve on Monday the night of the twenty-seventh of the sixth month a man of sixty-eight came out of a house at number nine with a bucket and a lamp because the sound a cellar makes when it is filling is not a sound people are used to hearing, and it is not loud.

He got four feet in about three hours. He was sixty-eight. He did it with a bucket and a lamp and he went up and down about two hundred times, and the water came to the fourth stair by about half past three in the morning, and he cleared the ground floor and he did not open the front door after eleven o'clock because a man of sixty-eight with a wet floor and a lamp at four in the morning is a man who falls over a step.

He did not tell the office. He told nobody, because there is no form on which a man in a cellar at one in the morning says that a cellar is filling, and he had asked twice, in 1951, and had been told it was a matter for the landlord.

---

The six of them were on that floor from about ten past midnight until two and about four hundred gallons of it came out of the house in buckets into the channel in the flags, and the last of it went down the channel and out, and the rest of it is still under the boards, because a bucket cannot get at a cellar floor, and the flagstones under the front rooms of 9 Weir Lane are wetter tonight than they have been since about 1931 and there is a man on the list of eleven who owns the channel and who will want it seen to and who will be right to.

**Nobody thanked Alder.** He is on the works' own maintenance line in four-inch capitals as a night porter's hours as returned and he is not a plumber and the repair on the second stage was not his and the buckets were not his. He is sixty-eight and he was on the night side of this ward before the war and he has wound the night side of Weir Two by hand since he was thirty-one and nobody has ever paid him for it.

**And nobody is going to call any of it a hand correction.** There is not a clock in any of it. There is a gland, a washer, a belt, four gallons, a chain block, two hundred buckets, and a chain in a flagstone floor, and it was done by five people in the ordinary way at night, and **the count of a clockmaker's own unrecorded hand corrections in this city remains two, and nothing tonight was a third.**

---

At about ten past three in the morning Bevin Sarr came into the pump house with a lamp and the night book and wrote it down, and she wrote it standing at the board, in the same hand the men use, and then she read it out, because that is what she does and she has done it once before, in the fifth month, in front of nine people.

> *Sixth month, the twenty-seventh. Night. Weir Two, second stage started twice, the second at about eleven fifty, the gland at about ten to twelve, the seal water gone, the front at about thirty-one from about half past twelve, back at about forty from about ten past one, and the second stage's follower and washer off the store and a belt an inch and a quarter short.*
>
> *Nine Weir Lane, cellar four feet by about half past three. A. Alder, night side, with a bucket and a lamp, about two hundred times, on his own initiative, from about half past twelve. Front door not opened after eleven for his own sake.*
>
> *Water: about four hundred gallons into the channel in the flags and out, and the rest of it still under the boards, because a bucket cannot get at a cellar floor. No form for the buckets. No form for the two hundred. No form for an hour of a man of sixty-eight who is on a line in the works' own hand and is not paid for any of it and has never been.*
>
> *The reason, in full, because it is going to be asked: the interlock is a time interlock and there is no pressure interlock on that machine and there has not been one since 1931 and the reason is in pencil on the sheet. The front has four draws in it and the station was surveyed for two, and a clock-driven interlock does not know the difference between a rhythm and a fault, and it moved when it was moved.*
>
> *Nobody in this city is going to be told to pay for a gasket. I am writing the reason down so that the reason is on a piece of paper in a room and not in a man's head.*

---

**At about eleven o'clock that morning Orrin Vale walked up the hill to the Office of Public Reckoning and sat in the one window and did not do anything at all for about an hour**, and the entry in the grey book is four lines and the first of them is the reason he went.

> *Twenty-seventh of the sixth. Weir Two, night. A hundred and five seconds, a part and not a state, asked well, which is the rule from the seventeenth of the second month working for the first time in this city, and the answer was correct and I did not use it, because by the time I had it Marta Dey had the follower off and four other people had the job, and I stood on a floor with a bucket for nine minutes and was a fifth pair of hands, which is what I was for and what I have not been for four months.*
> *So: a minute and forty-five seconds taken, a part named, and nothing spent. And the district paid for it in a cellar four streets away, and the district's share of it is in a night book in a pump house and is not in any form in any office in this city, and the only figure in it that anybody could check is the number of gallons, and the gallons are not the cost.*
> *The cost is two hundred buckets and a man's back and a front door that stayed shut, and I have got a rule of my own that says no result of mine goes out of this room without a person named who can refuse it, and there is no person named in the ledger for a night porter with a bucket, and the reason there is no person named is that he is a night porter and the column wants a toll and this was not a toll.*
> *I do not know what to call it and I am not going to invent a word for it at eleven in the morning.*
