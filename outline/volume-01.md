# Volume 01 Outline: The Ninth Tick (Chapters 1–50)

Authority: `bible/` sets the rules, `outline/series.md` sets the fourteen volumes, `outline/ending.md` sets the destination. This file routes inside Chapters 1–50 and may not invent rules. Batch cards live in `outline/batches/`.

## Volume number and span

Volume 01, Chapters 1 through 50. Five batches of ten. The volume's name is the phenomenon, not the solution: the ninth tick is never explained inside these fifty chapters.

## Central pressure

Orrin hears a ninth tick inside the Cathedral's public clock, files a question through the Question Hall about whether to open Weir Two, and receives a Reading that is technically correct, publicly celebrated, and empty of the one group of people who will die because of it. The Weir Two night pump crew is not in the model. Perry Vance goes down to close a hand valve the Reading described as already closed, and dies. The Directorate calls the Reading a success, and it is one.

The pressure that drives all fifty chapters is therefore not "will Orrin find the man who did this." It is: **a correct answer, once it has been paid for, spends its cost on whoever cannot afford to pay it — and the record of that spending is not kept.** Every escalation in this volume is a sharper measurement of the same fact. The volume closes by making the measurement public and cheap to repeat, at the price of the one man who could have kept measuring it quietly.

## Starting state

- Orrin Vale, 32, runs Vale's Bench: two benches, a lease, a sign, a work ledger, and a maintainer's license, class three, issued annually through Gerren Hask's guild hall in the Clockwork Quarter.
- He is a line horologist — an excellent one. He can diagnose a failing governor by sound, isolate a false bearing, and name the screw he replaced two winters ago. He is not an operator and has never been inside the Consequence Loom as a reader.
- He has never filed a question in his life. He files defect notes, invoices, and refusals to certify.
- He keeps a second, private repair ledger. Years of it. It records what machines actually did, as opposed to what the certificates said they did. Nobody has ever read it, which is exactly why he keeps writing in it.
- He believes a precise private diagnosis is safer than public panic, and that institutions are mostly competent adults with bad paperwork.
- Edwin Vale, his father, has been listed missing for eighteen years. Orrin's private want is proof that his father was not a saboteur or a coward. He has never been able to say which of the two he is really accusing.
- Morrowfen is a city that has never lost a minute, and a boyhood of that boast is the only inheritance he trusts.
- The class-three license may service and certify a single civic machine. It may not file a district proposal and it may not witness a Reading. Its renewal is Hask's discretion, and its renewal date is eight weeks after Chapter 1 — a clock the reader carries, not a deadline the volume reaches. Orrin thinks of it as a tax. By Chapter 8 the reader should understand it as a leash.

## Starting relationships

- **Leda Vale, 27,** his sister, an emergency nurse in Green Span. Close, watchful, and already tired of it. She knows what hour-sickness does to a body because she watches it happen in her ward. She is the first person who will oppose him, and she is right about the cost before he is.
- **Gerren Hask, 55,** guild master and his licensing authority. A familiar adult who employs Orrin's neighbours, pays on time, and has a problem that paper can solve if nobody looks. Hask's Volume 01 motive is visible to the reader within three chapters and is never explained in dialogue.
- **Mara Venn, 33,** an investigator for the Office of Public Reckoning, whom Orrin has never met. Her office audits the Toll Stair, which is a decision about a stair and therefore about nothing. Her mother died after a severe body toll during a sealed flood Reading, before the Directorate corrected the record; Orrin does not learn this in Volume 01, and she does not tell him.
- **Teo Arden, 20,** an unlicensed tuner in the Weirs and, as this volume reveals, the man Perry Vance was training. Alive throughout, elsewhere in the district on the night of the Reading, and not a suspect.
- **Anja Voss, 49,** Director of the Cathedral, three years in the post and new enough to the machine she runs that she has signed things since without knowing what the founding rules say. Named in this volume, in Chapter 4, on a signature. No scene, no offer, no bargain: her offer of access is Volume 02's closing turn and it is not spent early.
- **Halden Rusk, 58,** Continuity Minister, in the chair before Chapter 1 and in the chair at the end of the series. Present in Volume 01 as one name on a Continuity Office inspection authority in Chapter 1, and absent as a person.
- **Councilor Imani Rook, 40,** Green Span delegate, chair of the Assembly's public-health committee. Her aunt-by-blood relation to Nella Rook is canon and is not revealed in this volume.
- **Sel Kade, 44,** pump warden of the Weirs pumping district. A clockwarden's post with water attached: real authority, almost no money.
- **Ivo Pell, 36,** a veteran Consequence operator, present at the Question Hall and named at the guild hall. His Volume 02 function is teaching; in this volume he is competent, faintly contemptuous, and right.

## Starting power level

**Stage 1, line sense.** He hears imbalance in a machine he is touching. He mistakes precision for certainty. His hearing is undamaged. He has no experience of a body toll, a keeper's toll, or a filed Reading, and he has never taken a witness bond.

**Ending power level:** **Stage 2, Aftershock, and it is limited and expensive.** He can feel the next short-term failure of a named load on one connected machine or one immediate assembly. He cannot see intent, cannot see a district, cannot see anything the network has routed away, and he cannot file, cite, or defend a single thing he has heard. He pays the unrecorded keeper's toll every time, twice, and the second time it takes a minute of his attention he cannot get back.

## The rules of the machine that matter in this volume

These are the engine's actual operating rules as used here. They are the volume's engine, so the batch writer must not soften them.

1. **The question spindle accepts a concrete named action only.** No one in this volume can ask the machine whether a decision is right. Every Reading is about a thing somebody could physically do.
2. **A Reading has four parts:** trace, pressure, blind margin, toll. The blind margin is a required line, printed in a footer smaller than the operator's signature, and it is read by nobody in the room.
3. **A Reading is valid only for the conditions and records present when it was made.** It is a model, never a prophecy, and it goes on acting after everyone who signed it has forgotten why.
4. **Where the toll lands is a function of reserve, not of consequence.** The submitter names a paying district; the engine draws that district's stored clock rotation. There is no provision for the paying district's consent and no requirement that the paying district be affected. This one rule is the moral engine of the whole book, and Volume 01 is where the reader learns it. A district with a full reserve pays a rounding error. A district with an empty one pays a fraction of itself.
5. **The affected-district resident station on the witness rail has been replaceable for about twenty years by a written district standing.** The Weirs have population and no standing, so for two decades their Readings have been witnessed by Green Span and Northglass residents. Every Weirs Reading in this volume is legal and correctly witnessed by a person who does not live there.
6. **Body tolls are recorded as "calibration strain,"** with no name, no duration, and no interval, on a phrase roughly forty years old. Clock tolls are public on the Toll Stair. The difference between the two is the whole of the mystery, and it is bureaucratic rather than sinister.
7. **A private sense is not a Reading.** Orrin's Stage 2 listening has no blind margin, no record, no admissibility, and an unfiled cost. It is a lead. The volume's first great lesson is that this makes it useless in exactly the rooms where it is most needed.
8. **No certainty permit exists yet.** It is created in Volume 03, out of a Reading strike, by a frightened Assembly acting under the Director's authority. Nobody in this volume may act as though permits are normal.

## Major locations

- **Vale's Bench** and the Clockwork Quarter guild hall — the bench, the certification ledger, the shift forms, Hask's office, the wall case and the labour ledger.
- **Cathedral Rise** — the Question Hall and the witness rail, the Toll Stair, the Archive room on Cathedral Rise, and the Cathedral's public clock.
- **The Lower Weirs** — the Weir Two pump house, the Lower Weirs streets and kitchens, the tenant council's rooms.
- **Ferrywright Bridge** — the subject of the opening Reading, and a structure that survives the series.
- **The Green Span hospital trestle** — the old trolley trestle carrying the hospital line over the canal, whose failing load is the volume's fuse and whose repair is the volume climax.
- **The Assembly's public-health committee room** — where the toll-ledger demand is blocked in Chapter 10 and filed about three chapters later.
- **The guild hall's inspection desk** — where Hask's inquiry is quietly closed by a Continuity Office inspector who is not a villain.

## Major factions

- **The Guild of Horologists** — the licensed repair guild, necessary and compromised, and the institution that contracted the omission in the first place.
- **The Cathedral Directorate** — twelve appointed operators, physicians, jurists, engineers, and record keepers. It controls access, licenses operators, and can seal a question, and in this volume it does all three to itself first.
- **The Office of Public Reckoning** — a small audit office with authority over a stair and a written right to be ignored. Mara Venn's office, and the first institutional home for evidence that the city pays for Readings in human time.
- **The Weirs tenant council and mutuals** — old, ragged, and the real social infrastructure of the pumping district. Sel Kade's post is a clockwarden's post with water attached and is not the tenant council.
- **The Assembly's public-health committee**, chaired by Councilor Imani Rook — the body a toll-ledger demand has to reach, and the body that blocks it first.
- **The Loose Minutes** — unlicensed tuners, nurses, conductors, and archivists who recover discarded hour tokens and copy ledgers. Coarse, effective, and divided about theft.

**Not in the list above, and named here so the omission is deliberate:** the Continuity Office is an inspecting authority in this volume and not a faction on stage. It has inspected every hourline for four years, it can close an inquiry, and its inspector's signature is on a form in Chapter 1. The Continuity movement does not appear in Volume 01 at all. Keep the three terms apart: the movement is a faction, the Office is the executive office, and the Minister is the post.

## Escalation sequence

**Step one — the ninth tick and the regulator (Chapters 1–3).**
Orrin certifies the Cathedral's public clock and hears a ninth pulse arriving through the hourline from below the city, in a machine that is correct. **The next morning** Gerren Hask brings a damaged Long Nine regulator to Vale's Bench and asks for it to be quietly destroyed, and it is not damaged: it was stopped mid-travel on purpose, with a tool, eighteen years ago. The phrase on the guild's shift forms that Orrin starts noticing is *non-certified standby* — a category in which work that keeps a machine running is recorded as work that does not exist. The ninth tick is confirmed against a real failure at Weir Two, where Orrin meets Perry Vance in daylight and likes him.

**Step two — the Reading and the death (Chapters 4–5).**
The question is concrete: open Weir Two now, or hold, and what does it do to Ferrywright Bridge. The gate opens, the bridge stands, and the toll is three minutes of the Clockwork Quarter's clocks — including the clock Orrin certified two days earlier, which is the paper that will later make him answerable. The blind margin reads *unregistered night work is not represented.* By nightfall Perry Vance is dead of a preventable accident, and the Directorate's notice calls the day a success. Two people are injured. The published cost of a saved bridge is three minutes and one name the city has never heard.

Vance's death is staged in one sequence and the sequence is fixed, because it is the inciting event of the whole series: **the head passes after the gate opens; the opened gate and the running second stage drive the hand valve open again; nobody is on the board, because the board does not have a crew on it; Vance goes down on his own initiative to close a valve he had shut himself in daylight the day before; the pump that should have stopped does not stop.** Orrin is at the pump house before all of this and is refused at the door, and the refusal is the point. He felt the failure hours earlier and he had no license, no proposal, no shift time, no name, and no way to make a district admit a guild man on the strength of a feeling. He is not the cause and he is not blameless, and Chapter 5 says both of those things out loud.

**Step three — the evidence problem (Chapters 6–10, batch 0001).**
The killer of the volume is not the machine and not Hask. It is that Orrin's experience is not a fact he is permitted to submit. He takes a keeper's toll and loses a minute he cannot account for. He meets Teo Arden over a body and keeps two things back. Mara Venn opens an investigation because a street clock in his district is five minutes slow on a three-minute toll, and because the clean certificate that put the toll on his district's clocks is in his handwriting. The Office of Public Reckoning accepts an objection on the toll receipt, and the request for the Weir Two night roster is referred upstairs, to a sealed room that is not open to petition. What Mara finds instead is a **form number**, and the form number is a guild form number: the omission was contracted, priced, and certified by people whose licenses depend on it. The Archive of Unlisted is named in this volume and stays shut.

**Step four — the paper war (Chapters 11–20, batch 0002).**
Hask's cover-up becomes procedural rather than personal: an inquiry arrives, Continuity Office inspector Tobias Rill closes it for the guild because the guild's hours and the Office's inspections are in the same chain of paper, the guild's labour ledger is copied, and the Weirs produce a counter-record of their own hours on a board in a pump house. Imani Rook files the toll-ledger demand herself about three chapters after blocking it. The volume's Stage 2 failure lands here: Orrin reads a failing Green Span hospital pump correctly, cannot get the repair certified because certification requires a license and the pump's district cannot ask for one, and watches a warning he was right about do nothing. The hospital trestle is introduced here as a structure whose failing load is quietly eating the hospital's clock pressure. **The renewal of Orrin's class-three license falls due eight weeks after Chapter 1, and it does not fall due inside this movement.** What arrives here is the renewal's *paperwork*, not the renewal: the notice, the form, the interview nobody is obliged to hold, and Hask's discretion, which he uses to make the leash felt long before anyone thinks to suspend him. Chapter 12 is where the slack goes out of it, and the batch 0001 card's rule stands — the leash is slack in Chapter 10 and must never be described as imminent. Note also that the volume's ending is a **suspension**, not a renewal decision: whatever the date does, the suspension in Chapters 44–49 is Hask acting ahead of his own clock, and a later volume may find the renewal still running.

**Step five — the midpoint repetition (Chapters 21–30, batch 0003).** See below.

**Step six — the consequence (Chapters 31–40, batch 0004).**
The water schedule that Chapter 25 made binding is unappealable, so the repair work acquires a deadline and a constituency. Clock slips cross the Quarter and the Weirs. Sel Kade's diverted pump reserve, known since Chapter 9, becomes a **documented diversion with a schedule and a named beneficiary** and is defended as a defence of jobs, which is the point: her choice is understandable and structurally unjust. The Loose Minutes steal hardware back and cost a tenant block something. Hask's ledger is copied wider. **The Directorate's counter-move is institutional rather than personal**: a question is filed against Orrin by the guild, a sealed classification is applied to the Weir Two pump records, and the whole business is reclassified as deferred maintenance with a published schedule and a budget line, which is how an institution absorbs a scandal it cannot answer. No offer is made to Orrin by anyone. He goes to the Director's door on his own, is not admitted, and does not tell anybody he went.

**Step seven — the storm and the settlement (Chapters 41–50, batch 0005).** See climax and resolution. **Batch 0005 must contain a repair, training, or recovery chapter before Chapter 48**, because the series rule is that a volume settling on a political decision needs working hands on the page inside the same ten-chapter batch. Chapter 41 is the slot.

## Midpoint reversal — Chapter 25

A second and larger public Reading, on the Weirs' own water schedule, repeats the opening's structure at ten times the scale. It is properly filed, properly witnessed, technically correct, and approved by people who have already read the blind margin once. The water it saves is worth more than the bridge was. The people it omits are the same people, and they are by now organized, informed, and furious, and they are in the room, and they are still not on the rail.

The Cathedral continues to call the omission an acceptable blind margin. Nobody in the room is stupid. Everybody is behaving reasonably inside a rule that was designed to let this happen. The failure of this book is not ignorance in Chapter 4. It is repetition in Chapter 25.

After Chapter 25 the shape of the volume changes. Before it, Orrin is a man trying to prove something happened. After it, he is a man whose proof has been published, twice, in the correct form, and has changed nothing — which is worse, and is the ground the final four movements stand on.

## Volume climax (Chapters 44–49)

A storm. The Green Span hospital trestle's failing load has been starving the hospital's clock pressure for most of the volume, and a ward's medicine schedule depends on a clock that is losing its grip. Repairing the trestle returns that pressure to the hospital. The Weirs' water schedule needs the same reserve, and there is one reserve, and two districts, and no Reading that can settle it, because settling it privately is precisely the thing that killed Perry Vance.

The team saves the hospital by exposing the trestle's load in daylight, in public, with the arithmetic printed where anyone can check it. What they cannot do is return the Weirs' water schedule without taking a **visible, named outage**: hand-wound clocks, a posted timetable, a stated number of hours during which the pumping district's water is somebody else's problem.

Orrin's instinct is to fix the machine privately, at night, with a solution that works and cannot be objected to because nobody hears it. The volume's climax is the moment he does it publicly instead, names the outage, posts the hours, and puts his own name under a number he has no standing to certify. The Weirs are furious and correct: the person who spent their hours named it correctly and is not the person who will live by the timetable. The trestle is repaired, not destroyed. The hospital keeps its time. The Weirs' water schedule stays broken for a stated and posted interval.

His reward is a suspension, and the mechanism of the suspension is the volume's whole argument: the guild suspends him for certifying a local toll he had no license to certify. He is punished for putting his name on a number nobody else would sign.

## Concrete resolution

All seven of these must be on the page in Chapters 48–50. A volume does not end on a mood.

**Note for the batch 0005 writer:** the climax runs 44–49 and the settlement 48–50, so the two ranges deliberately overlap at 48 and 49, and three chapters is not enough to give all seven items a scene each. Distribute them rather than queueing them: items 1, 3, and 6 are the machinery of the settlement and belong together in the chapter where the guild's decision and the suspension are read out; items 2 and 4 are districts acting on their own terms and can be carried in scenes already doing other work; item 5 is a line in a committee room and can ride inside a political chapter; item 7 is the ninth tick and belongs to Chapter 50 alone. Chapter 50 is the Toll Stair image and is not available to any other item.

1. **The Weirs receive a repaired pump** — Weir Two's second-stage pump, physically fixed and maintained by name — **and a public toll ledger with two kinds of column.** The minutes-and-district columns are old: the Toll Stair has carried those for forty-three years, which is why three minutes was legible to everyone who cared to look. The **new** columns are the reform's first provision and the sentence everything in this volume flows toward: for every body toll, **a name and a duration.** The Directorate has only ever written *calibration strain* in those spaces. The first entry under the new columns is **Perry Vance**, and the name is written and the duration is left blank, because no instrument in Morrowfen recorded the interval that shift lost and the volume will not invent one. The blank is the point, and the book says so out loud rather than treating it as a mystery to be solved later.
2. **A standing protection from silent shifts** in the pumping district while the ledger is audited. Temporary, and the reader is told it is temporary.
3. **Gerren Hask loses the right to certify silent shifts** and must publish the guild's labour ledger: eighteen years of hours that were never worked, and a **liability annex** that names the night of the Long Nine as the night the hours stopped. The 212 is the public figure the annex is about; it is not an entry in a shift book, and it is never added to any other number. He is not destroyed. He is a repairer under public oversight, which is the volume's view of justice.
4. **Sel Kade keeps her pump role and submits to a tenant review** of the reserve she diverted. The review is not a punishment; it is a process she did not have before, and she participates in designing it, which is why she is still a character in Volume 02.
5. **Councilor Imani Rook files the toll-ledger demand herself**, having blocked it, and is explicit that she changed her mind because of arithmetic rather than sentiment.
6. **Orrin's class-three license is suspended**, removing his right to certify a machine's local toll. Vale's Bench stays open on a lease and a reputation, not on a license.
7. **The ninth tick remains unexplained.** He counts it again in the last chapter and writes it down and gets no further than he did on the first page.

## New question for the next volume

Perry Vance was not an anomaly. If the machine can leave out one night crew on purpose — if the omission is a form number, a signature, and a price — then how many other people are missing from the civic model, and why does the Directorate keep a sealed room, under the Cathedral, full of the names of the people it stopped counting?

## Final chapter image

The Toll Stair, freshly chalked, with the ledger's two sets of columns chalked out in full for the first time. The minutes column is filled the way it always has been: **3**, and beside it **Clockwork Quarter**, in a district's own hand, because that is the toll the city has always been willing to publish.

The two new columns beside those are empty of everything except one name. **Vance, P.** is written in the column where a person goes, and the column next to it, where the duration goes, has nothing in it at all — because no instrument in Morrowfen recorded how much of that shift the Reading took, and Orrin will not put a number there that he invented. The blank is the reform. The blank is also the thing nobody can fix, and the volume has already told the reader that some families know what was taken and some never will.

A stranger stops on the stair to ask what the chalk means, because the Toll Stair is the one place in Morrowfen where people stop to read things. Orrin says *three minutes*. The stranger asks the question the whole book has been refusing, which is *from whom?* — and for the first time in fifty chapters there is a name to give, and it is two words long, and the man who could have written the number next to it is standing on the other side of the stair with a chalk in his hand and nothing to put there.

Behind him the Cathedral's public clock strikes the hour with the best timekeeping in Morrowfen, and he counts out of long habit, and hears nine, and it is still, exactly, unexplained.

## Volume guardrails

- **Anja Voss is named once, in Chapter 4, on a signature, and does not take a scene, an offer, or a bargain in this volume.** Her offer of access is Volume 02's closing turn. Nothing in Chapters 31–40 gives her one.
- **Halden Rusk is in the chair from Chapter 1 and never becomes a character in Volume 01.** He is one name on one inspection authority in Chapter 1 and nothing else. The reader should know the name before Volume 04 and should not meet the man until then.
- **No certainty permit, no district time council, no Reading room, no local instrument, no Council of Hours, no Civic Time Keeper.** Volume 01 is a book about paper.
- **Orrin never witnesses a Reading and never files a district proposal.** He files as a private citizen charging the district he lives in.
- **The paying district appears on the filed question and on the Toll Stair receipt, never on the Reading notice.** The notice's toll line reads as a quantity and an interval, which is why nobody in the Question Hall can see at a glance that a district with no stake in the answer just paid for it.
- **Perry Vance is not Teo Arden.** Vance is dead by the end of Chapter 5. Teo is alive, was elsewhere in the district that night, and is Vance's apprentice. The reader must never be invited to confuse them.
- **Vance is not a martyr.** He is a competent, unlisted wage-earner finishing a shift, and he would be embarrassed by any reading of himself as a symbol. His last act is closing a valve because it was open.
- **Orrin is not the cause of Vance's death, and the absence of cause is not the point either.** The sequence is fixed in the escalation section above. His guilt is real and it is about having no instrument that turns what he knows into an action somebody else can take.
- **The two death numbers never touch.** Two hundred and twelve is the Long Nine, eighteen years earlier, and is the public figure the guild's liability annex is about, not an entry in a shift book. Twenty-three is the Returning Tide in Volume 14 and must not appear here in any form.
- **The Censor Gate, the return line, the Dark Reservoir, the First Hour, the binding plates, and the command line are not named in this volume.** The volume may show that something physical carries load away from the public display; it may not name the machinery that does it. Volume 01 gives the effect, not the mechanism.
- **Rusk is not one of the six.** Nothing in this volume may suggest otherwise, and no one in this volume has standing to be asked.
- **Edwin Vale is not vindicated, indicted, or explained.** His face is in an uncaptioned crew photograph, a stopped regulator is in a drawer, and a name is on a wall. That is all Volume 01 has.
- **Nella Rook is not named in this volume.** She is introduced in Volume 03. Green Span's hospital is reached through Leda and the trestle.
- **No chapter ends mid-sentence, mid-action, or on a manufactured cliffhanger.** A Volume 01 ending may be a decision, a cost, a discovery, or a refusal.
- **One Reading notice maximum per chapter**, and only one notice in the whole of batch 0001, in Chapter 4.
