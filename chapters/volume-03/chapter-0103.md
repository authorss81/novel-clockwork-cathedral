# Chapter 103 — The Die Drawer

On Thursday the nineteenth of the first month Orrin went to the guild hall on a job he could have sent a boy with, because the job was a pretext and the drawer was the reason, and he admitted both to himself on the walk up Verge Lane, which was further than he usually got with himself before ten.

The hall let a man in off the street. It had let him in for eleven years, and the counter clerk still took his name and still wrote the time of his visit in the day book, and none of that had changed since the first of the sixth, and he had never once got used to it. There is a particular way of being welcome in a building you are not licensed in, and it is not embarrassment. It is the sense of being counted.

The hospital branch that morning was three hours and four minutes and twenty seconds out of the district reference and gaining about fifty seconds a night. He knew the figure because he had done the working on the back of the rate notice Mara had left: two hours and one minute on Friday the fourth of the eleventh, seventy-six nights at fifty seconds, one hour three minutes and twenty seconds added, three hours four minutes twenty seconds. He did not say it on the stairs. He carried it the way Leda carried a ward's slack, as a number to be said in a room and not on a stair.

The licensing floor was quiet. The monthly list was on the wall, the counter polished, the die drawer behind it open because a first-class holder was cutting a token for a Quarter clock that had come back with surplus at the return.

The holder was a small man in a good coat with a turret movement in a wooden crate, and he had brought it in a handcart, and he stood at the counter while the officer did the weighing and did not say anything at all, which Orrin understood to be the correct thing for a man paying two shillings to be in that room.

The drawer had nine district punches in it in a row, each in its loop, each oiled, each with its letter facing out, and blank space at the end where a tenth could go and had never gone. The officer set the movement on the plate, weighed the surplus, entered the weight in the plate book in a small upright hand, chose a punch by the letter, and cut. It took him about nine seconds. The brass offcut came away in a curl and went into the bin under the counter, and the token went into a tray, and the tray went behind the counter, and nobody in the room said a word about it.

"Two shillings," the officer said, and the man paid and took his receipt and went, and the good coat went out into the rain.

Orrin waited his turn and watched all of it, and then watched the drawer go back in, and then waited the better part of an hour for the drawer to come out again, which it did for a man who wanted to see how many punches there were and who had been in the queue since half past ten.

"Morning, Vale," the officer said. He was a man of about fifty, licensing officer for eleven years, necessary, correct, and the only person in the city whose job was to say what a token was made of. "Your licence still suspended?"

"Suspended on the first of the sixth, term unaltered, expires Sunday the nineteenth of the third month," Orrin said. "I am not here to ask about it."

"You are stood at my counter at a quarter to eleven on a Thursday looking at a drawer."

"Yes."

"What are you here to ask about?"

"The drawer. How many punches, how many cut, how it is lawful."

The officer wiped his hands. He had a cloth in each hand and he used them in turn, which was a habit and not a signal, and Orrin had watched it for eleven years and had never known which.

"Nine punches. Q, W, G and six others. Blank space is blank space. The last return cut two hundred and sixty-one, took back two hundred and six, sold nine hundred and forty. The law says a token may be held, cut, sold and bought, and the law says what it is made of and nothing else. Twenty-two millimetres, three thick, rim, hole, letter, figure, year and die. That is the whole of it. The form is about 1883 and it has no column for what a token is for, because in 1883 nobody needed one."

"In 1883 a token was a stopcock with a hole in it," Orrin said. "Nobody needed a column because a man put it in and the clock either went or it did not, and he could see which."

"That is a true sentence," the officer said, "and I have no way of putting it in a form, and both of those facts are mine to carry and neither of them is anybody else's problem before eleven o'clock."

"And the harm?"

"The harm is not in my drawer," the officer said, not unkindly. "A token spent in the clock it was cut from holds a wheel off a dead escapement for a stated time. That is the rule. What happens after — a Quarter clock sharing its rotation with the rest of a line, the network giving it to the next clock behind — that is not cutting, that is physics, and physics is not licensed. I am right about the law and I know I am wrong about the harm, and there is no form on which to be right about both."

"You have said that before."

"I have said that to four people in eleven years, and I have said it in writing twice, and both memoranda are in a file that is not open to the public, and I have never been asked to write it a third time." He put the cloth down. "Mr Vale, I have nine punches and a blank space and about six weeks a year where I can do nothing at all but sign my name against a fee, and I would put a column in that drawer for the purpose of a token tomorrow if the hall would print a form for it, and the hall will not print a form for it, and the reason the hall will not print a form for it is that nobody ever brought one of these to me and said what it was for."

He stopped, as if he had heard himself, and picked the cloth up again.

"Put your job on the counter," he said. "And pay the counter fee, because the fee does not know whose licence is suspended."

Orrin put the printer's job on the counter, paid the counter fee, and asked the question he had come to ask. "If a man puts a Quarter token into a Weirs clock, whose minutes are they?"

The officer did not hesitate, because he had been asked it before and had answered it the same way every time. "The clock's. The reserve meter does not know where the brass was cut. The law does not know either, because the law never asked."

"And if the token is false?"

"Then it is a phantom minute, and a phantom minute in the network is slippage, and slippage is a machine repeating or skipping a movement. The assay office on this lane will weigh one free. Nobody brings them."

"Have you ever seen a false one?"

"Twice," the officer said. "Both times a man brought one back with the brass in his hand asking whether he had been done out of something, and both times I sent him to the assay office, and both times he came back a week later with the same question and the same brass, because that is what a man does when the answer costs nothing. I have never weighed one myself. I do not have a beam and it is not my office, and I would want it in writing before I touched an instrument I have no standing to touch."

Orrin nodded and went out with the receipt in his pocket, and nobody thanked anybody, and the hall's clock struck eleven.

He took the tram down to Green Span in the afternoon because Leda had left word at the shop the day before that the ward's medicine times were going wrong and wanted a clockmaker's eye and not a clockmaker's answer, which was a sentence with a clause in it that he had thought about for about an hour and a half.

The ward was warm and loud, eleven patients, three staff, the free master on the landing ticking clean and the line clocks three hours out and still stepping. In the nurses' room a chart on a board carried the phrase *calibration strain* against a night sister's name with no duration beside it. Orrin looked at it once and looked away, because the empty duration column beside Vance's name in the pumping ward's glass case was a statement and this blank was a different building's different blank, and the two were not comparable, and he had never in his working life put one blank beside another and was not going to start.

Leda was at the bracket clock with two watches round her neck. She was twenty-seven and tired in the way nurses are tired in January, which is a different tired from December.

"Say it," she said, without preamble.

"Three hours and four minutes and twenty seconds, gaining fifty seconds a night," Orrin said, in the room, in front of the people whose hours they were. "A hundred minutes is a hundred and twenty nights. The ward's whole slack on the best night of the year is forty minutes. Two hundred minutes is five times the whole of the ward's slack, and two tokens cost nine shillings, and the ward has no fund, and the branch has been stepping for about two hundred and seventy-three nights and two tokens last two hundred and forty."

A student nurse wrote it down. Nobody thanked him, and Leda did not look for it.

"Is that the hospital branch or the ward's?" the student asked, and got it right a moment later without being told, which told Leda something about her that Leda did not say out loud.

"And the medicine?" he said.

"We give it on the free master and write down the line clock beside it," Leda said. "We cannot guarantee every dose on time. We can publish the missing intervals and tell the wards which patients are most urgent. The hospital's reduced efficiency is a real cost, not a hidden line item. That is what we do with a number we cannot spend."

"Where do you publish them?"

"On the board outside the Weir Two pump house, in the same hand as everything else on it, at the change of shift." She put a watch on the mantel and took it off again. "Eleven people a day read that board. The number reaches the ward about four hours after the ward produces it, which is slow, and slow is a cost, and it is the cost of not having a place to put the number that is ours."

"Four hours is a dose."

"Four hours is a dose," Leda said. "Which is why I would rather have a number late and honest than a number early that a body had to be argued into. You will notice that is not an argument for the ward. It is an argument for the board."

He checked the bracket clock's beat, set it as true as it would go, and refused to certify anything, because he had no licence and the offence of the autumn was spent and was not to be repeated as a joke. He wrote the work in the ward's own book with the fault observed, the work performed, and the recommendation, and left the recommendation short, because there was no column for *I am not sure* and never would be.

He put his hand flat on the ward's book to keep the page from turning while he wrote the date, and the student nurse asked him, quite directly, whether a man with a suspended licence ought to be writing in a ward's book at all.

"No," he said.

"Then why are you?"

"Because the fault is in the movement and the movement is the medicine and there is nobody else in this city with a licence who would come and do it for nothing," he said, "and because if I stop, then the next time this is a question, the answer will be that nobody came. Write that down if you like. I would rather it were on the page than in a corridor."

On the walk home he thought about the drawer with its nine punches and its blank space, and about a licensing officer who was right about the law and wrong about the harm and had written it down twice into a file nobody could read, and about a ward with forty minutes of slack being asked to measure itself against a hundred, and about a chart on a board with a word on it and no number after the word.

At the bench that night he wrote the drawer in the grey ledger, and the hospital figure, and the chart with its blank he had not filled and would not.

*Drawer: nine punches, blank space. Law is what it is made of. Purpose is a hole in a form about 1883. Keeper at assay weighs free, four minutes, half a grain. Officer knows the harm and has no form for it.*
