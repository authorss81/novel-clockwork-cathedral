# Chapter 104 — The Beam on Weigh Lane

Sunday the twenty-second of the first month Orrin stayed away from the assay office, because no counter sits on a Sunday in Morrowfen, and the reason has nothing to do with the day. A counter that cannot shut is a counter that cannot be found either, and the whole argument for a closed day is that the person behind it is not discoverable when he is not there, which is the same reason a man who has a claim waits until a building is shut.

He spent the Sunday mending a school clock for Monday and reading the fifth page of the row book that Teo had left in the shop and taken back again, which was about the maintenance schedule: six names, an empty seventh line, and eighteen shillings and sixpence a week of which nobody could pay the eighteen shillings. The seventh line was empty because a master had pinned a strip of form inside a labour book saying he had read all of it and would not say, and Orrin understood the empty line better now than he had in December: some instruments cost more than the district that needs them, and the cost is not an argument against them.

He took the school clock apart on the kitchen table at four in the afternoon with the curtains shut and the good light off, and the movement was so simple that a boy of fourteen could have done it, and the boy of fourteen in that classroom would have had to stand in front of thirty people and admit that the district could not pay him, and there was no column for that either.

He put the school clock back together at half past six, ran it on the mantel until nine, and wrote the fault in his own book on the bench: *maintenance, one hour, no parts, no charge, and the children are the only reason this shop will be open in ten years.*

Then he went and looked at the schedule again, because a Sunday is the only day in the week when a man in this city may sit and look at a piece of paper he has no power to change. Six names, six men or women, each in a trade, each able to hold a watch to a rail and say a clock is wrong to within a minute a day. A seventh line, empty, which would have been a fourth trade, at the same eighteen shillings and sixpence a week, which nobody could find for the first six, let alone for a seventh. So a boy of fourteen stood in front of thirty children and said that the district was very sorry, and there is no form on which a district says sorry to thirty children and gets to stop saying it.

He wrote all of that in the narrow book in pencil, in the order he had thought of it, because it was a sequence like any other, and then he read it back and could not find a single line in it that any person could act on before Friday.

On Monday the twenty-third Mara Venn came to the bench at nine with a folder under her arm and a look that said she had been up since six with columns.

"I have had a letter," she said. "From Hale. Broker. He writes to the Office asking whether he may be told the drift of the Weirs' clocks, with a date on it. He wants a figure he can put on his rate card without copying the City's numbers wrong."

"Will you?"

"No." She set the folder down and squared it. "The Office has no standing to give him anything. The drift is on the ward board outside the Weir Two pump house and any man may read it. About eleven people a day do. He is one of them if he walks, and if he is not one of them then a figure given to him in a letter is a figure in a hand that cannot be checked, and the whole of what makes his card work is that anybody can walk to it."

"And if somebody in the Office thinks the Office ought to answer him?"

"Then somebody in the Office will be asked by a man in a pitch whether the Office answered, and if the answer is no, the Office said no to a question nobody had standing to make, and if the answer is yes, the Office gave away a number that goes on a board." She turned the folder round. "That is the whole of my job and it is not an interesting job, and I have wanted to say that sentence to somebody for about nine years."

She put her hand flat on the folder. "I am auditing the market. Not the men. The arithmetic. For a set of households the market may be the only instrument by which they can buy back time official tolls took. I am going to lodge it with the working whether or not anybody wants it. It will cost me the post. An office which audits cannot also be the office a person appeals to. I have known that for nine years and I have never once been able to think of a way round it that is not a lie."

"It is two hundred and forty households in four streets, the price, the number of tokens bought by each, and the drift on the branch they are on." She said it flatly, the way she said everything that cost her. "It is not an accusation. It has no man in it. It is a count of what people did with their own money in a place where nothing else was for sale, and when it is finished it will be true, and it will be useless to everybody except the people in those four streets, and that is a thing I have decided to be the correct use of a clerk."

"Will the four streets be named?"

"No. The four streets are a number in a table and the table is the lodging, and the lodging is public, and a public table with a number in it and no street in it is the one form of this that I am certain of." She closed the folder. "If I name them the audit is a complaint about a place. If I number them the audit is a count of a thing, and a thing can be argued with by anybody who has got four and six and a calendar."

Orrin said nothing, because there was nothing useful to say to a woman who had just told him the price and the date in one sentence.

On Tuesday the twenty-fourth the arcade was full. Orrin went at ten and stayed till twelve, not buying, watching. The crowd on an ordinary Tuesday was the market's own argument: porters, conductors, two printers, a laundress with a basket, nurses, a chandler, nine or ten small employers who had discovered that a hundred minutes was a wage. Money changed hands openly. Nobody hid. Hale sold eleven tokens before noon at four shillings and sixpence, each with its district letter showing, each wrapped in paper with the price written on it.

A woman argued that the price had been four shillings last Friday. Hale showed her the rate card and the City's daily sheet underneath it and said the price was the price because the drift was the drift, and she bought one anyway and put it in her glove.

"She is right," Hale said, when she was out of earshot. "It was four shillings on Friday and it is four and six today and it will be four and three next month, and I do not set it. I put two sheets on a board with a date on the top and I take the difference between them and I do not argue with either." He squared a tray. "A man who will not tell you the price is a man who is keeping something. A man who will not tell you the method is the same man."

"How many in the arcade today?" Orrin said.

"Six pitches, four trading. I have the only sign and about a third of the brass." Hale looked down the row. "The other two-thirds is the printer's man, who does not need a pitch because he has a counter and a printer's boy, and the six weeks when the hall was cutting freely, which is over, and a man in Weir Lane with a barrow, who I would rather did not exist and cannot report, because reporting him means producing a list, and producing a list is the thing I sell."

Orrin left at noon and walked to the Public Assay Office on Weigh Lane, which was a beam, a counter, and standard weights to half a grain, a public counter, free, about four minutes a weighing.

He had been in there twice in his life, both times with a job in his hand and no reason to look at anything else in the building, and both times he had noticed the same thing on the way out, which was that a public counter is a place a person may go with nothing at all. You bring nothing. Nobody asks what it is about. You are not a customer, because nothing is bought, and you are not asking for anything, because nothing comes away. He had not in all his years of mending clocks met one person who had noticed that, and the only reason he had noticed it was that somebody had put a beam in a public room and charged nothing for the use of it.

The keeper was a man of about sixty who had weighed a token nine times in nineteen years and every one of the nine was somebody bringing one back. He told Orrin so while he calibrated the beam, because keepers tell the history of their instrument to anybody who stands still long enough.

There was one other person in the office, a woman paying a water bill at a different counter, and she was done in ninety seconds and left, and Orrin understood that the office ran on that kind of errand and that the whole of its public life was ninety seconds long and repeated two hundred times a day.

"Nobody brings them to be told what they weigh," the keeper said. "They bring them to be told they are real. Six that weigh one thousand eight hundred and sixty grains are real. Three that weigh one thousand seven hundred are not, but I have not seen those three yet and I am telling you the figures from the book."

"Whose figures?"

"Mine. Nineteen years ago, in the first month, a man came in with a tray of nine and asked me to weigh all of them and then to tell him which were which, and I weighed all of them and told him which were which, and he went away with the six and I never saw him again." He set the beam to rest and it rested. "I have thought about that tray a good deal. He was not a criminal. Criminals do not want the truth at that price. He was a man with nine tokens and a stall and a question he had not asked anybody else."

"Will you weigh one of mine?" Orrin said. He had no token of his own, so he borrowed the printer's description instead: Q, a hundred, year and die on the rim. "In theory."

"In theory, four minutes, free, anybody may bring one." The keeper set the weights out. "A beam of mine will tell a true token from a false one at a cost of nothing to whoever brings it, and I have not had one brought to me in nineteen years that was not on its way back to somebody who had already been sold it. That is this office entire."

"How long would it take the Quarter to learn the habit?"

"That is not a question this office can answer. It is a question about four hours a person has to give up, and there is a great many people in this city with four hours and not one of them knows there is a beam." He put the tweezers down. "I have written to the Office twice. Nineteen years, twice. Both letters are in a file and neither of them is a form, and a letter that is not a form is a letter, and I am sixty-one and I am going to go on weighing water rates and the occasional spoon."

"Has anybody ever come in on the strength of a letter?"

"One man," the keeper said. "In the eleventh year. He had a token in a paper and a card with a street written on it, and the card was his own, and he wanted to know whether the brass was good. It was good. I told him so, and he put it back in his pocket and went out and did not spend it, and did not ask me a single question about what to do with it, and I have thought about that man more than about the tray."

Orrin watched the beam come to rest on nothing, which was the point: the beam was true whether or not anybody used it.

On Wednesday the twenty-fifth he wrote the assay in the grey ledger, and the crowd, and Mara's letter, and under them a line about Ivo Pell, whom he had not seen since September and would not see in this city, because Pell had left a method and a price and a sentence about disconnection and the city had kept all three.

*Pell, in a document: three clocks, one question, four minutes, and what it is not. Unpaid. The method is on two sheets printed Sunday twenty-sixth December at eleven. That is the only place he is in this week, and he is not in the room.*

Nobody thanked anybody in the assay office and nobody thanked anybody in the arcade, and the keeper did not look for it and had never once looked for it. He had weighed nine tokens in nineteen years, and he was about to weigh the tenth, and the cost of the tenth was nothing at all.

The four hours it would take a district to learn the habit were four hours a person had to find and give, and there is no column anywhere in this city for a person finding four hours, and there has not been one since the form was printed.
