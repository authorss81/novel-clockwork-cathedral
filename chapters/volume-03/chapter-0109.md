# Chapter 109 — The Weight of a Hundred Minutes

On Thursday the tenth of the second month Orrin took the printer's Q token, wrapped in proof paper with the printer's own permission, to the Public Assay Office on Weigh Lane and asked to have it weighed, because a figure a person can check with a beam and a price list is a figure a person can argue with.

He had asked on the Wednesday and been told to bring it any morning, and had been told it was free, and had been told both of those things in a tone that made it clear he was the first person in nine years to walk in and ask what a token was made of. He put the token in his coat pocket in a paper twist, not in the roll, because a roll is for tools and because he had found that he did not want it near a screwdriver all morning.

The printer was Mr Coss and there was a Mrs Coss in Bellweather Road and neither of them was related to the other, and Orrin had been getting them muddled in his own head since the twenty-sixth, and so he wrote *printer* in the narrow book on the Thursday night and put the surname nowhere at all.

The keeper was behind the counter, beam polished, standard weights to half a grain laid out in order. About four minutes a weighing, free, anybody might bring a token and be told what it weighed. The keeper had weighed nine in nineteen years and every one was somebody bringing one back, and Orrin would be the tenth.

There were two other people in and neither of them was there for brass. A man with a bad boot and a boy of ten, paying a water rate at the counter, done in a minute. The office ran on that sort of errand, two hundred times a day, ninety seconds each, and the whole of its public life was that repeated ninety seconds, and somewhere underneath it there was a beam that had been true for nineteen years and had been asked to do a different job nine times.

"Name?" the keeper said, opening the book.

"Vale. Clockmaker. Not selling, not buying. Weighing for a customer, with his leave."

"Not selling and not buying is on the form. The leave is not on the form, because the form has no box for it and did not need one when it was printed." He held his hand out. "Up you come, then."

He took the token with tweezers, set it on the pan, and built the weights up, and waited for the beam to settle, and Orrin watched it do the thing beams do, which is to hesitate and then stop arguing. One thousand eight hundred and sixty grains. The keeper wrote it down, showed Orrin the entry, and then wrote the district letter and the die number beside it in the same line, so that the number in the book belonged to a specific object and not to a category of objects.

"Real," the keeper said. "Six that weigh one thousand eight hundred and sixty are real. The book says so. What it does not say is what it is for, because the law never asked."

"You would want the die number."

"Always want the die number. A weight on its own is a rumour. A weight with a die and a district letter on it is a Tuesday's work." He turned the page round and let Orrin read it. "Nineteen years, ten lines, and I could put a name to nine of the men and none to the brass."

"And false?"

"One thousand seven hundred. Three that weigh it would be false, and I have not seen them, and I am telling you a figure out of the book because there is a column headed for a false weight with three entries in it and I have never once been in the same room as the man who wrote them." He put the tweezers down. "That column is why I have never told anybody the true one cold. If I say a false one is seventeen hundred and I have never seen one, that is a fact. If I say a false one is seventeen hundred and stop there, that is a lie, and I am sixty-one."

The beam sat between them, resting, and Orrin asked the question he had come to ask, and asked it in the way he had learned to ask things in rooms like this one.

"The column for a street beside your nine brass figures," he said. "On the counter behind you. What is it?"

The keeper turned round and came back with a sheet, and put it face up on the counter, and said nothing at all about it, which was how he said it was a thing he had decided to be able to show.

It was a large hand nobody in the arcade could read, not Hale's and not Teo's and not any hand that had been taught. Nine brass figures down the left, then a ruled column, and the column had a heading over it that was two words, and four entries in it, and the four entries were doors.

"That is mine," the keeper said. "Nobody in this arcade can read it and I have never let a man in this arcade read it. It is a list of streets and a list of doors, and it is the only list in this city that says where the brass goes, and I have kept it for nineteen years because the nine figures tell me what a token weighs and the column tells me whether anybody ever comes back."

He turned it about ninety degrees and gave it back square.

"Nine tokens, nineteen years, one tray. Six of the nine were real and the man took all six away with him and I never saw him again. Four of those six are in that column, in doors, because four of the six came back past this counter on their way to something. One of the four was a man in the eleventh year with a street on his own card, who came once, was told the brass was good, and went out with it still in his pocket. One of the two that are not in the column went into a market on a Thursday and has not come past here since. That is six of the nine. The other three are the false weight, and I have never had one of them in my hand." He put his thumb on the edge of the drawer. "Eight of the nine have an ending I can name. The ninth has a market. That is the whole of what a free beam in a public room can do in nineteen years, and I have written all of it down."

"And the man who wrote the four doors down?"

"I did," said the keeper. "From the counter, from four of the six, in the year a man of twenty stood in a room in Bellweather and told me that a list of addresses is a list of routes, and then told me the better half of that, and did not tell me to write it in a book and I wrote it in a book anyway." He put the sheet away under the counter, face down, and shut the drawer. "He has not been thanked for it. I have not sent him word that I have written it down, because a man of twenty who has said a thing once in a room has not asked to be told about twice."

Orrin did not copy the doors. He stood at that counter for a full minute and did not copy them, and the reason was not integrity and it was not squeamishness. It was that a list of four doors written down by a man on a Wednesday is a list of four doors that can be found, and the man who wrote it had not been asked to be findable.

He wrote the weight in the grey ledger with the date and the four minutes and the half grain, and the fact that the weighing was free, and the fact that a man had asked a keeper a question on a Thursday and been told the truth about the column under the counter.

On Friday the eleventh he walked Pindar Row and found Hale's pitch busy in the middle of the day's trade, tokens moving at four shillings and sixpence, the rate card chalked fresh with the drift.

The whole of the market's economics were on that board in four lines, and any person in the arcade could check every one of them with a calendar and a price list. A night of drift about half a penny. A year on one clock five hours four minutes ten seconds. Three tokens thirteen shillings and sixpence. A hundred minutes a hundred and twenty nights. Four figures, no adjectives, on a board, in public, in chalk, where a child could add them up on a wet window if the child wanted to.

A man tried to sell Hale a token with no hole. Hale refused it without touching it, politely, in front of six people, and said the hole was the law: no hole, no rim, no letter, no token. The man went away. Nobody thanked Hale for refusing, and Hale did not look for it, and the six people watched a rule be applied to a counter and could not have told you afterwards which rule it was.

Orrin stood at the end of the row and counted the pitches, six of them trading, and thought about the six. Nine years ago there was one die drawer with nine punches in it, and now there were six counters in a glass roof, and nobody had voted on it and nobody had to, and the only man in the city who could tell the true weight of a token had ten lines in a book and four of them were doors.

He had come to the arcade on the seventeenth of the first month to look at a market. He was leaving it on the eleventh of the second with two facts he had not gone in for, one of which was that a free beam in a public room is not used by anybody who is not already suspicious, and the other of which was that the most careful list in the city is kept by a man who has no right to keep it and no way to act on it, and is put away under a counter every night in a drawer with a lock on it that Orrin watched him lock.

On Saturday the twelfth he returned the token to the printer with the assay slip, and charged nothing for the weighing because the weighing was free, and wrote the return in the book, and the printer read the figure and said that the clock was going properly and that the boy had said you sent it back in paper, and Orrin said he had, and the printer said so it goes, and shut the door on the shop side of it.

*Q100, 1860 grains, real, weighed 10th second, four minutes, free. Keeper's tenth in nineteen years. Doors not copied. Count of hand corrections two.*

That evening Mara came in with her audit sheets under her arm, columns of households, minutes, prices, the working showing through the top sheet. She looked thinner than in January.

"For about two hundred and forty households in four streets the market is the only way to buy back time tolls took," she said, quietly, so the empty shop made it louder. "No instrument can do it lawfully. The reason is a hole in a form about a hundred and forty years old. I am going to lodge it with the working and publish it. Nobody is going to thank me and I am not going to hide behind four staff and a window."

"Is the working finished?"

"The working is finished. The lodging is not. Publishing it means it is a document with a date on it, and a document with a date on it can be asked about by a person who is not a committee, and I have spent nine years making sure that the Office is the body a person asks and not the body a person hides behind." She squared the sheets on the counter. "The day it is lodged, every person who works in this city can say the market exists and say it correctly and can be checked. And the day after that, somebody will ask me why a clerk of the Office thinks the market should keep existing, and I will have to answer that as a person and not as an office."

"And you will."

"I will," Mara said, "and it will cost me the post, and there is no fund for the post, and I have not told anybody that but you."

"When?"

"When it is ready. Not this week. This week ends on his book."

She went out with the sheets under her arm, and he stood at the bench with the assay slip in his hand, and the last thing he did that Saturday was not in the ledger at all. It was in pencil on the back of the slip, where the printer would see it when the token came back in its paper.

*It is real. That is the whole of what the beam knows, and it is worth the four minutes it took, and it is not worth what a man will pay for it.*

On Sunday the thirteenth he did no work that needed a counter, and the arcade was shut and the assay was shut, and he said the run out loud before he wrote it down, because the last time he had a day wrong in his own head it cost him a sequence of forty-one seconds he had not known he had lost, and a market that appears to trade on a day it does not trade on is a market nobody can be believed about: Thursday the tenth, Friday the eleventh, Saturday the twelfth, Sunday the thirteenth.

The reason for the Sunday was chalked on a board over the shuttered pitch in Hale's own hand, in the same chalk as the rate card, where anybody on the row could read it standing in the aisle.

*Shut Sunday. A market which runs on a Sunday has no day to close on.*
