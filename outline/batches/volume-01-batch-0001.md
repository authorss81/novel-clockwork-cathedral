# Volume 01 Batch 0001 Outline: Chapters 1–10

Batch number: 0001
Chapter range: 1–10
Volume: 01, The Ninth Tick
Planned word range: roughly 26,000 to 31,000 words across the ten chapters, using 2,200 to 3,200 for ordinary chapters and letting the short structural chapters run short. Do not pad to a count and do not split a complete scene to fit one.

## Batch goal

Establish Orrin Vale's ordinary work, his license, his private ledger, and his father; hear the ninth tick; get Perry Vance alive on the page in daylight; run one correct Reading that costs three minutes and one man; then make the reader watch a competent man try to prove what happened to people who do not appear in any record he is allowed to file. The batch ends with a small, earned institutional gain, two real losses, and a political refusal that names the volume's next movement.

The batch's genre promise, in one sentence: **an answer this good is not free, and the receipt does not say who paid.**

## Starting condition

Autumn, about nine days. Chapter 1 is day 0, the Reading and the death are day 2, and Chapter 10 falls a week after the death. Orrin has a bench, a lease, a license, and no standing. He has never filed a question. Nobody in the Cathedral knows his name, which is exactly why he is the right person to be ignored by it, and the wrong person to be believed by it.

## Starting relationships

Orrin and Leda: close and worn at the edges; she is the person he lies to by omission and she has not noticed yet.
Orrin and Hask: fifteen years of licensing, twelve years of good invoices, and one afternoon of damage.
Orrin and Mara: strangers. They meet in Chapter 7, in an office, over a paper.
Orrin and Teo: strangers who have just lost the same man from opposite ends of it. They meet in Chapter 6, over a body, and the body is Perry Vance, not a machine.
Orrin and Anja: never in the same room. Her name is on a form.
Orrin and Rusk: never in the same room. His office is on a form.

## Batch shape

- **Beginning (Chapters 1–2):** the ordinary work, the leash, the ninth tick, the regulator, and the phrase *non-certified standby*.
- **Escalation (Chapters 3–4):** the ninth tick confirmed against a real failure; Perry Vance alive and likeable; the concrete question; the Reading; three minutes; the blind margin nobody reads.
- **Midpoint (Chapters 5–6):** the death by the end of Chapter 5, as canon requires; the keeper's toll; Teo; the private ledger with a name in it; the child on the tram.
- **Escalation to climax (Chapters 7–9):** Mara, the objection, the Archive refused, the form number, and then the slippage crisis that ends with Orrin doing the volume's first public act of work.
- **Climax and aftermath (Chapters 9–10):** Chapter 9 is the batch climax and it is a repair, not a fight. Chapter 10 is the aftermath and a defeat: the demand for a public toll ledger is refused and referred.

## Batch midpoint (Chapters 5–6)

The inciting event of the whole series completes here. Perry Vance is dead by the end of Chapter 5 and the city has already called the day a success. Chapter 6 is the batch's turn: the machine's cost stops being Orrin's private certainty and becomes an absence on a street. He loses a minute of his own attention and cannot say whether what he heard at Weir Two was the pump or the man. He meets Teo Arden and keeps two things back, and the reader watches a man who wants to be a hero fail the first test of adulthood in the book: he does not tell the truth to someone who is owed it.

## Batch climax (Chapter 9)

A slippage cascade crosses the Lower Weirs and touches the Green Span hospital line. Orrin is the only person who can find the failed assembly and he is the only person in the room who cannot lawfully certify what he finds. He does not run a Reading, cannot file a proposal, and has no licence to certify a toll. He states, out loud and in advance, what he is going to do, what he does not know, and what it will cost — and then he does the repair in company, with Teo's crew and Leda's count of what her ward can absorb, and the hospital trestle's failing load is exposed in the open. The clocks hold. It works, it is earned, it is watched, and it costs him another keeper's toll and the beginning of permanent damage in his right ear.

This is the first time in the series that Orrin's ability is useful *because* other people are present, and it is the template for the ending. He is not rewarded with power. He is rewarded with company, and the reader is given the shape of the whole book.

## Required character changes

- **Orrin:** from a man who believes a private precise answer is safer than a public vague one, to a man who has watched a public precise answer kill someone and cannot produce a fact. He ends the batch keeping a ledger that no institution can read and a rule he has not yet broken: no answer of his is used again without its blind margin and its toll named in public.
- **Mara Venn:** from an investigator who thinks he is careless, to an investigator holding the one useful object in the case, a three-minute toll receipt charged to a district that was neither affected nor consenting. She has a professional stake by Chapter 9 and no personal disclosure whatsoever. Her mother is not mentioned.
- **Teo Arden:** from a young man who has lost his master to a stranger's machine, to a young man who has watched that stranger refuse to lie to a district and cannot decide what to do about it. He is not softened and he is not recruited. He is suspicious on purpose, and the suspicion is correct.
- **Leda Vale:** from a sister who disapproves in general, to a woman who has counted, in a real ward, exactly how much slack a hospital has and is therefore the reason Chapter 9 works. She stops arguing about Orrin's character and starts arguing about method.
- **Gerren Hask:** from a familiar authority with a small unexamined favour, to a man whose reader can see the shape of his crime and who is never asked about it on the page. His cover-up is visible by Chapter 3 and stays invisible to everyone in the story for forty more chapters.

## Required power changes

- **Stage 1 to Stage 2, Aftershock, involuntary and then deliberate.** The first arrival is unwanted, unwitnessed, and wrong: in Chapter 5 Orrin feels a load that is not in the machine he is touching, and the moment tells him nothing except that something is pulling against a hand valve in the dark. The second arrival is in Chapter 9: he names a load, states the question, and gets a usable answer about one assembly.
- **The keeper's toll, twice.** The first is the lost minute in Chapter 6. The second is in Chapter 9, larger, because he pushed, and it costs him fine motor control in his right hand for two days and the beginning of lasting damage in his right ear.
- **What does not arrive:** no Choice Trace, no Loom access, no filed Reading, no admissible evidence of anything he has privately heard, and no ability to scale. He is the same man at the end of the batch as at the beginning, with worse hearing and a longer notebook.

## Required world reveals

Delivered in this order, each through an object, a job, or a conversation rather than an explanation:

1. An hourline carries mechanical load and strain, not information, and a public clock can be correct and still be carrying something that is not its own.
2. The maintainer's license, class three: what it permits, what it forbids, and that its renewal is one man's discretion. The leash, felt by Chapter 8.
3. A Reading's four parts, and that the blind margin is a required printed line in a footer smaller than the operator's signature.
4. The witness rail's three stations, and that the affected-district resident station has been replaceable for twenty years by a written district standing — so a Reading about the Weirs is witnessed by a Green Span resident, lawfully, every time.
5. **Where the toll lands is a function of reserve, not of consequence.** The submitter names a paying district; the engine draws that district's stored rotation; nobody's consent is required and the paying district need not be affected. This is the batch's governing rule and the moral engine of the series.
6. Clock tolls are public on the Toll Stair. Body tolls are recorded as *calibration strain*, with no name and no duration, on a phrase about forty years old.
7. The Continuity Office has inspected every hourline for four years, which is the year clockmakers started answering to strangers.
8. The Archive of Unlisted exists, is sealed, is not open to petition, and is not empty. A request for the Weir Two night roster is referred to it and refused in one sentence.
9. The omission is a **form number**. It is a guild form. It has a price, a signature, and a filing convention. The thing that left Perry Vance out of the model was bought, scheduled, and certified.
10. A private sense is not a Reading: it has no blind margin, no record, no admissibility, and a cost nobody reimburses.

## Rule and system guardrails for this batch

- **Keep the two kinds of toll apart in every line, every ledger entry, and every demand.** A **clock toll** is minutes and a paying district, and the Toll Stair has carried that for forty-three years. A **body toll** is an interval of a living person's attention, coordination, or memory, and it has no column at all anywhere in Morrowfen. Chapter 10's demand is for **name and duration columns for body tolls**; the three clock minutes are already public and are only being asked to have the paying district printed beside them. Do not write a body toll into a minutes column or a district into a name column, and do not have anyone in this batch demand something the Compact already provides.
- **Exactly one Reading notice appears in the entire batch**, in Chapter 4. Do not paraphrase it in another chapter. Later chapters may refer to its four parts in dialogue.
- **The paying district appears on the filed question and on the Toll Stair receipt, and never inside the notice.** The notice's toll line is a quantity and an interval. Nobody in the Question Hall can see at a glance which district just paid.
- **Orrin files as a private citizen and pays from the Clockwork Quarter's reserve.** He may not file a district proposal, may not witness a Reading, and may not certify a district toll. He certifies nothing at all in Chapter 9.
- **No certainty permit exists.** No Council of Hours, no district time council, no Reading room, no local instrument, no Civic Time Keeper, no permit, no sealed-question procedure available to him.
- **Anja Voss is named once, in Chapter 4, on a signature, and takes no scene, no offer, and no bargain.** Halden Rusk is named once, in Chapter 1, on the same Continuity Office inspection authority as Tobias Rill, as the authority that appoints the inspector, and takes no scene and no action. Neither name may recur on a second document in this batch.
- **Nella Rook is not named.** Green Span is reached through Leda and the hospital trestle.
- **The Censor Gate, the Dark Reservoir, the First Hour, the return line, the binding plates, and the command line are not named.** Chapter 1 may show a load arriving from below. That is all.
- **Perry Vance dies in Chapter 5 and is a working man, not a symbol.** Teo Arden is alive, was elsewhere in the district that night, and is Vance's apprentice. The reader is never invited to confuse them.
- **Ada Vance is thirteen and is mostly observed.** She appears on the six o'clock tram. She may ask one short question. She does not argue a position.
- **Two hundred and twelve** may appear in Chapter 2 as the public figure the guild's liability annex is about. **Twenty-three** must not appear anywhere in this batch, in any form.
- **The Chapter 2 crew photograph is uncaptioned.** It names no station, rank, or post, and in particular it does not place Edwin Vale at the operator station of the witness rail. That is Volume 04's escalation and Volume 08's reversal.
- **The second hearing of the ninth tick is Chapter 3 and the last in this batch.** Chapter 5 is a different sensation — a load, not a beat — and must not be written as a repeat.
- **The right-ear damage from Chapter 9 is a condition, not the series' final cost.** Volume 08 and Volume 14 each take something larger, and both must still be available when those volumes are written.
- **No chapter ends mid-sentence or mid-action.** Endings are decisions, costs, discoveries, or refusals.

## Chapter cards

### Chapter 1 — "The Extra Beat"

**Type:** daily work, repair, discovery.
**POV:** Orrin.
**Location/time:** Clockwork Quarter, Cathedral Rise, and back to the Quarter, in one wet autumn day, dawn to last bell.

**Immediate goal:** Certify the Cathedral's public clock, collect the fee, and get through the rest of the day's list — a work clock at a ropeworks, a hospital timer, a school bell, a market scale — without thinking about it.

**Resistance:** Four small resistances and one real one. The foreman at the ropeworks rounds a worker's shift down by a minute and calls it a rounding error, and the only instrument the worker has for a minute the works has kept is a brass hour token, which is a punched record of minutes owed, not a payment; the foreman gives him one and takes the minute, and the worker takes the token, and Orrin, holding the work clock, says nothing, because he is not the man's employer and because saying it would cost the man his hours. A Continuity Office inspection authority has been left at the bench for signature, and it carries two names: **Tobias Rill**, the inspector who will do the work, and, in the line that states the authority appointing him, **Halden Rusk**, Continuity Minister. Rusk's name is the office's warrant, not a second instruction, and the reader should register that the man who appoints Morrowfen's inspectors is on the paper without either of them ever standing in this shop. The form asks to inspect the Quarter's hourlines, which four years ago nobody asked to do. On Cathedral Rise, a clerk treats a class-three maintainer as furniture and gives him a counter and a stool instead of a name. And the clock itself will not resolve: it is correct. His timing gauge says correct. Eight beats, and then, arriving through the frame and the hourline and not belonging to the clock at all, a ninth pulse — faint, low, from below, with the sound of water coming back in a pipe.

**Information revealed:** What a class-three license may and may not do, in Orrin's own understanding of his own leash. What an hourline is, physically. That the Continuity Office has inspected every hourline in the city for four years, so a stranger with a form and a question about hourlines is now a normal event in a trade that used to be private. The economy of a lost minute and the hour token that records it.

**Action or decision:** He certifies the Cathedral clock as within tolerance, because it is within tolerance, because that is the truth and because his name on that certificate is worth more to him than a theory. It is a clean certificate, signed and dated today, and it will be the paper that makes him answerable in Chapter 7. Then he writes the ninth beat in his own private ledger, in the margin, under a date, and does not tell anybody.

**Emotional change:** Professional certainty, one small humiliation, and then a private unease he has no word for. He ends the day more alone than he started it and considers this a fair trade.

**Power or resource change:** Paid work, one day's fees, ordinary fatigue. No Stage 2. His hearing is undamaged at the end of this chapter and the reader should be able to feel that as a resource.

**Continuity fact to preserve:** The ninth tick is first heard here, in the Cathedral's public clock, and it arrives from below. The certificate Orrin signs this morning is **clean, in his own hand, and dated two days before the Reading** — not a defect note, and never referred to as one again. **Tobias Rill and Halden Rusk are both named on the inspection authority in this chapter, and neither appears in person.** Rusk is the appointing authority and nothing more; he takes no action, sends no instruction, and is not referred to again in this batch. This is the reader's only sight of him before Volume 04.

**Ending type:** discovery.
**Next-chapter pull:** Hask has left word at the bench: come and see me about something that does not go on an invoice.

### Chapter 2 — "Non-Certified Standby"

**Type:** character and relationship work; investigation.
**POV:** Orrin.
**Location/time:** The Clockwork Quarter guild hall and Vale's Bench, the next morning.

**Immediate goal:** Get the anomaly into the ordinary channel. A defect note is a normal thing to file; it would send the Cathedral an engineer, cost the guild nothing, and let Orrin be right on paper.

**Resistance:** Gerren Hask. He is warm, unhurried, entirely reasonable, and does not say no to anything; he simply describes what a defect note would cost, in fees and in attention, and what a line on the certificate would do to the Quarter's reputation as a supplier of clockwork. He also says the true and unhelpful thing, that the Cathedral's clock is right, and that a building which keeps its own accounts does not like being told what it is carrying. Under Hask's elbow, open, because Hask is a man who reads while he talks, is the guild's labour ledger at a page of shift totals where one line is set in a different hand and reads *non-certified standby.* **Ivo Pell is in the hall**, waiting on his own quarterly business with the Cathedral, and is introduced here, at the guild, as canon requires: twelve years an operator, competent, faintly contemptuous of the trade he left, and the only man in the room who will say to Orrin's face that a bench man's ear is not an instrument. He is not a mentor in this chapter and offers no lesson. On the hall's wall is the Long Nine case: a guild crew photograph, glass, foxed, uncaptioned beyond a date and a shift number. Edwin Vale is in it. Nobody points him out and Orrin does not ask.

**Information revealed:** Hask's motive becomes legible to the reader without being said: that ledger line is a crew that did not exist, and if the crew did not exist then the hours it worked are hours the guild was paid not to pay, and if those hours connect to eighteen years ago, the guild's licenses connect to a night two hundred and twelve people did not survive. Hask is not hiding a monster. He is hiding a number that ends careers, including his own. Ivo Pell's presence establishes that operators and clockmakers are different trades with different standing, and that the Question Hall is not a room Orrin can simply walk into.

**Action or decision:** Hask brings out a regulator, damaged, and asks Orrin to reduce it to scrap and not to enter it in the books. Orrin says he cannot scrap a regulator with a cut pin. Hask points out that he can, and can do it without recording it. Orrin takes the regulator home. He files nothing. He has now asked for nothing in public and kept something dangerous in private, on the first morning after hearing a thing he cannot explain.

**Emotional change:** The first time a person with power asks him to make a machine disappear, and the first time he declines by keeping rather than by arguing. He is compromised and it feels like prudence.

**Power or resource change:** His craft is on display — the regulator is not damaged, it was stopped mid-travel on purpose, with a tool, and the pin teeth are engaged. He recognises the signature of a hand-thrown stop from his bench. This is expertise, not magic, and it should read as competence.

**Continuity fact to preserve:** The Long Nine regulator is now in Orrin's possession and unrecorded. *Non-certified standby* is the phrase the reader will hear again. Ivo Pell is named at the guild hall here and appears again at the Question Hall in Chapter 4. **Edwin Vale appears for the first time as an unnamed face in an uncaptioned crew photograph.** The caption must not name a station, a rank, or a post, and in particular must not place him at the operator station of the witness rail: that is the Volume 04 escalation and the Volume 08 reversal, and giving it away here costs both.

**Ending type:** cost, quietly incurred.
**Next-chapter pull:** A call comes from the Weirs: a certified clock in the Lower Weirs is running fast, there is a pump fault under it, and the guild is sending the man who certified the Cathedral this week, because he is the cheapest person who will come.

### Chapter 3 — "The Hand Valve"

**Type:** repair, investigation, relationship.
**POV:** Orrin.
**Location/time:** The Lower Weirs, Weir Two pump house, daylight. The head is expected to crest late tomorrow afternoon; tonight it is rising hard and Orrin's guess is that Weir Two's second stage will run past midnight if it keeps rising.

**Immediate goal:** Find out why a certified clock in the Weirs is four minutes fast, and stop the pump fault that is underneath it before a shift starts.

**Resistance:** The pump is not failing. The pump is doing exactly what its logic says, and its logic is correct, and the thing that is wrong is that the logic has no idea a valve in the pipe is open, because the valve is opened by a person on a night shift that no instrument in the building has ever registered. Orrin's own instinct works against him here: he hears a fault, forms a single explanation, and is wrong for several hours in a small way. The water is up. There is no budget. The district's own people are better at the work than he expected. And **Bevin Sarr**, thirty-three, second on the Weir Two night pump crew, is the first person in the book who writes down what actually happened on her shift — on a board by the pump, in chalk, by hand, because the guild's line for that work is *non-certified standby* and a board is at least a record somebody made.

**Information revealed:** How a district can be legally on time while its residents lose sleep: the reserve comes from somewhere, and in the Weirs it comes from hours nobody is paid for. The Weir Two stop logic keys off a shift clock nobody certifies, which is why the guild cannot say who was in the building at three in the morning, and why Bevin Sarr keeps a board. The rising head, the crest, and Ferrywright Bridge as a structure with a name and a load. Perry Vance, forty-one, twenty years on the night pump crew, unlicensed, the man who closes things by hand and complains about it, who mentions his daughter's six o'clock tram and does not look sentimental about it.

**Action or decision:** Orrin tells Vance the pump will run past midnight if the head keeps rising. Vance goes down and closes the hand valve and comes back up and says the board says that valve was already shut. It was open. Orrin understands, standing in a pump house with wet boots on, that a machine can be perfectly informed about a valve that is shut.

**Emotional change:** He meets the man his calculations are about and likes him, and the liking is what makes the next two chapters possible.

**Power or resource change:** The ninth beat again, this time through the pump house's own line, and this time leaning on something he cannot name or locate — it gives no picture, no night, and no word, and there is no instrument that will tell a man where it leans. **This is the second hearing of the ninth tick, and it is the last one in the batch**; Chapter 5's arrival is a different sensation and must not be described as a repeat of it. **Delivered register, fixed by the prose and not to be re-derived: the ninth beat is a sound with a count in it and a lean in it, it is not a prophecy, and it costs nothing. Aftershock arrives for the first time in Chapter 5, is a load and not a beat, carries a picture of a named load's next failure, and costs a keeper's toll every time.** Still Stage 1 by his own understanding at the end of this chapter. He has no name for what he is doing and no instrument for it.

**Continuity fact to preserve:** Weir Two, the hand valve — **shut here in daylight, and driven open again in Chapter 5 by the opened gate and the running second stage, which is the whole mechanical reason Vance goes back down** — Ferrywright Bridge, the rising head, the crest at late tomorrow afternoon, and the fact that Perry Vance is alive and specific at the end of this chapter. Bevin Sarr is alive, on the board, and not in the building on the night.

**Ending type:** earned hook, built on a decision rather than a threat.
**Next-chapter pull:** There is exactly one machine in Morrowfen that can tell him what happens if he opens Weir Two now, and he has never filed a question in his life.

### Chapter 4 — "No Unseen Cost"

**Type:** political and institutional pressure; the batch's genre promise delivered.
**POV:** Orrin.
**Location/time:** Cathedral Rise, the Question Hall and the Toll Stair, one day, late morning to late afternoon. The crest is expected tomorrow afternoon; Orrin's window is the four hours before the head gets there.

**Immediate goal:** Get a concrete question into the machine: *if Weir Two is opened now, does Ferrywright Bridge stand until the head passes?*

**Resistance:** Four resistances, each of them a rule rather than a villain. He may not file a district proposal, so he files as a private citizen, which the spindle accepts and the hall makes unpleasant. A question must name a fee and a paying district, and he has no district affected by the question and no authority to spend one — so he names the Clockwork Quarter, the district with the fattest reserve and the least right to be charged, and notices himself doing it and keeps going. **The paying district goes on the filed question and onto the Toll Stair receipt. It does not go on the notice**, which is one of the reasons nobody in the room can see at a glance that a district with no stake in the answer has just paid for it. The witness rail requires three identified witnesses: Ivo Pell at the operator station, a record keeper, and the affected-district resident. The Weirs have no standing, so the third station is filled by **Alma Festinger, 56**, a Green Span resident who signs because she is a dutiful woman on a public-health rota and who has no idea what a district standing is or what she has just been used for. This is so ordinary that nobody remarks on it. And Pell, twelve years an operator, is competent, unimpressed, and unwilling to record anything that is not properly asked and properly witnessed.

**Information revealed:** The four parts of a Reading, once, on the notice. The blind margin is a required line, printed in a footer smaller than the operator's signature. The witnesses wear wrist bonds and a reader is told, casually, what the bond is for. Above the counter, carved in stone: *No unseen cost.* The Director's name on a signature, and nothing else. A Reading is valid only for the conditions and records present when it was made, which Pell says in the tone of a man who has said it a thousand times and once means it. Alma Festinger's name is written in the record and she should be written into the prose as a person rather than a station, because the reader will meet her again in a gallery in Volume 06 and will be asked whether she knew.

**Action or decision:** The Reading runs. The gate opens. Ferrywright Bridge stands.

The notice, in the form the world uses:

```text
QUESTION RECEIVED
TRACE: Opening Weir Two now holds Ferrywright Bridge through the crest.
PRESSURE: Weir Two's second stage will not stop before midnight, and the hospital's night clock slips with it.
BLIND MARGIN: Unregistered night work is not represented.
TOLL: Three district minutes and a witness interval.
```

**Emotional change:** Vindication and disgust in the same breath. He was right, the room is satisfied, and three people in the hall are quietly pleased in a way that will read to him later as obscene. He has just spent his own district's accuracy to buy a bridge for people who were not asked.

**Power or resource change:** He spends money, civic time, and his standing to see, at close range, the three things he is not permitted to do himself: submit a question, sit at a station, and file the result. He gains no power and one large piece of understanding.

**Continuity fact to preserve:** Three minutes, charged to the Clockwork Quarter, are the batch's tracked number. Ferrywright Bridge is saved. The omission is named in the language of a category, not a person. Anja Voss is named in this chapter and takes no part in it. **The notice does not name the paying district**; the reader learns who paid from the question in the room and the receipt on the stair, and must never be shown the district inside the notice text. The PRESSURE line is not a copy of the canonical example in `bible/power-system.md` §9 and must not be paraphrased into it anywhere later in the batch. Note that §9 is an *example* of the world's form, not a blank to fill in, and that this world's notices print their band flatly: "will not stop before midnight" is how a notice here reads. §3's language about probabilities describes what the band actually contains behind the sentence, not a hedge the notice is obliged to print. Keep the four lines, their order, and the plain declarative voice. Do not add hedged or probabilistic wording to make the notice sound more careful, and do not give it a fifth line.

**Ending type:** discovery, with a cost already incurred.
**Next-chapter pull:** At the Toll Stair the public display reads three minutes and nothing else, and Orrin walks out past a Quarter that is now three minutes behind every clock in the city, knowing the true figure is larger and cannot be written down.

### Chapter 5 — "The Pump That Should Have Stopped"

**Type:** action and survival; the series' inciting death.
**POV:** Orrin.
**Location/time:** Vale's Bench, then the Lower Weirs and the Weir Two pump house at dusk, then the pump house again at night and at dawn. One long day, the day of the Reading.

**Immediate goal:** There is nothing left to repair. The Reading is done, the gate is open, the bridge is standing, and Orrin's afternoon is suddenly empty in a way that is worse than work. He wants to find what the Reading left out.

**Resistance:** He cannot. This is the chapter's argument and it is stated through the machine, not through anybody's mouth. His Stage 2 sense has no blind margin **by construction**: it can only feel strain the network already carries, so the people the Reading omitted are precisely the part of the situation he is deaf to. He can hear the water and the city and the hours of the district, and he cannot hear the one thing he is looking for. He is a man with a very good ear, listening in exactly the wrong place.

Then he goes to Weir Two, because putting a thumb on the machine is what he does instead of thinking. **The sequence of the night is fixed and the chapter must keep it:**

1. Dusk. The head is high. Weir Two's second stage is running and the hand valve Vance shut in daylight that same afternoon is open again — driven open by the opened gate and the running stage, and there is no water record of when, because the shift that would have watched it is not on the board.
2. Orrin puts his hand on the second stage. **Aftershock arrives, and it is correct:** he can feel the next failure of a named load, which is that the stage will not stop tonight. It is a real reading, and it is the best he has ever had.
3. He tries to act on it and cannot. The night watchman will not admit a class-three maintainer to a district pump station at night on the strength of a feeling, and the watchman is not corrupt and is not wrong: Orrin has no licence for the machine, no district proposal, no shift time, no name of anybody on the crew, and no number. **The most Morrowfen door in the book is a locked door, and it is locked by paperwork, and it was built by good people.** He stands outside it for a while and then goes home, which is the correct thing to do and feels like the worst thing he has ever done.
4. Night. The crew comes on. Nobody tells them anything, because nothing was filed that would tell them anything. **Perry Vance goes down, on his own initiative, to close the valve he had shut himself earlier that same afternoon**, because it is his valve and it is his shift and nobody told him the Cathedral had already decided it was shut. The pump that should have stopped does not stop.
5. Dawn. The crew has the body out. Bevin Sarr, who was not in the building and who has been writing the crew's own hours on a board for six years, is the one who tells Orrin the valve was open when the head passed. Orrin knows what the model said. Nobody else in the room does. A clerk comes down from the Rise with the Directorate's notice, and the notice calls the day a success, and the notice is not lying.

**Information revealed:** The omission is a category and not a failure of the machine. The model said the valve was closed because no registered person was ever modelled as opening it. The engine is right. The Directorate's notice is right. That is the horror and it should not be underlined. Also: the first working use of Aftershock, on one named load on one assembly, and it is correct. He asked the right question in the right words. It is still worthless, because a correct private reading cannot be entered anywhere and therefore cannot send anybody anywhere.

**Action or decision:** He gets to the assembly at dawn and puts his hand on the running stage and the answer is the same answer, and the man is already out of the water. His guilt is real and it is not negligence. He had the information and no instrument that turns information into action somebody else can take, and the only instrument in Morrowfen that does that requires a licence, a proposal, three witnesses, and a fee charged to a district, and he had none of them and so did Vance.

**Emotional change:** From the satisfaction of having been right to the first genuine understanding that being right was the injury. This is the chapter where the volume's argument enters his body instead of his head.

**Power or resource change:** **Aftershock, Stage 2, arrives on the page for the first time: he asks a narrow question of a named load on one assembly, and he gets an answer, and it is the best he has ever had.** He cannot see intent, cannot see a district, cannot see what was routed away, and cannot file, cite, or defend a word of it. His hearing in the right ear begins to ring and he assumes it is the water.

**Continuity fact to preserve:** Perry Vance is dead by the end of this chapter. His death is a preventable accident during an unsealed bypass, caused by a pump that should have stopped, a valve that was open, and a model that said it was shut. Two other people are injured. His last act was closing a valve because it was open, and he went down on his own initiative and not because anybody sent him. Nobody calls him a martyr and the narration must not. **Orrin is not the cause, and the chapter says so in his own head, once, without letting him off.**

**Ending type:** cost.
**Next-chapter pull:** The street outside the pump house, the loose tuners, and a young man who is not a stranger to the dead man at all.

### Chapter 6 — "The One Minute He Cannot Account For"

**Type:** character and relationship work; recovery that is not recovery.
**POV:** Orrin.
**Location/time:** The Lower Weirs in the morning, a Green Span ward in the afternoon, then the bench. The day after the death.

**Immediate goal:** Find the Weir Two night roster. Any roster, in any book, in any office: a shift list, a wage claim, a drinking story. He wants a name on a piece of paper so that the death on the stair has a person attached to it.

**Resistance:** The hour. He sits down to write down what he heard at Weir Two and finds a gap he cannot cross: one minute in which he was somewhere, and he cannot say whether he read the stage or imagined the reading, and every subsequent fact in that minute is a reconstruction. This is the keeper's toll, and it is not tiredness. It is the sound a small coin makes when it lands and there is no surface. He tries twice and gets two different answers, and both are plausible, and neither is evidence.

Then two people who are owed him something. **Leda**, who has worked a night shift and has come to tell him her ward lost half an hour of drug-round time on a ward clock that ran slow, and who says *calibration strain* at him in a corridor in front of a ward clerk, deliberately, because she wants to see him flinch. That phrase is the Directorate's word for an interval of a living person's attention and it has no name and no duration and no date of occurrence on the slip, and Leda is a nurse who has watched it happen to people, and she uses it as a provocation and then refuses to explain why. The two of them have the first real fight of the book: she says the district pays for the city's answers in sleep and he says somebody has to run the pumps, and they are both quoting their own mothers. He does not tell her about the ninth tick.

**And Teo Arden**, who is twenty and was elsewhere in the district that night, and who is not the dead man, and who is the dead man's apprentice, and who has been up all night and has better information than Orrin does about everything that matters. Bevin Sarr is with him, or has been, with her board. Teo's anger is not confused and does not need to be managed.

**Information revealed:** The keeper's toll, named by nobody. The health slip, in Leda's hands, recording a man's lost attention as *calibration strain* with no name and no duration — the Directorate's euphemism for a body toll, distinct from the *self-inflicted occupational strain* the guild uses for a clockmaker who listens to a machine without a witness. The reader gets both phrases in one afternoon and can keep them apart for the rest of the series. The Loose Minutes, informally: unlicensed tuners, nurses, conductors, archivists, who recover discarded hour tokens and copy ledgers. The shape of a neighbourhood's anger, which has better arithmetic than Orrin's and a better account of the night.

**Action or decision:** Orrin tells Teo that he filed the question. He does not tell Teo that he heard the ninth tick, and he does not mention the regulator. He offers the anger a target, which is generous, and he keeps the two things that would make him the object of it, which is not. He writes Perry Vance's name into his private ledger — the first name in it, and the first person in it rather than a shift.

**Ada Vance is in this chapter, not in the next one.** She is thirteen and she is on the six o'clock tram across the canal, alone, on her own route, and Orrin sees her from the wrong side of a street he could cross in nine seconds and does not. She does not make an argument and nobody asks her to. She may ask him one short question — the reader should feel the weight of a child asking a man whether he did it, and it should not be answered well — and then the tram takes her. He lets her go past. That is the chapter's last human beat before the office sends for him, and it should not be written as a gesture or a moral victory. It is a man who does not know what to say, and it is a small cowardice, and it is the last one he gets away with in the volume.

**Emotional change:** From guilt, which is broad and survivable, to the specific shame of a man who knows something and has decided, in the last half hour, that knowing it privately is the safer gift.

**Power or resource change:** Aftershock confirmed and denied. He can feel the next failure of a named load on one connected assembly. He cannot file it, cite it, or defend it, and he has just discovered that this makes it a rumour with his name on it.

**Continuity fact to preserve:** Teo Arden is Perry Vance's apprentice and is alive. Bevin Sarr is alive and is the one with the record. The keeper's toll is taken, unfiled, and leaves a hole of exactly one minute. The words *calibration strain* (a body toll) and *self-inflicted occupational strain* (a private reading) both enter the reader's vocabulary here and must never be used for each other again. Leda has now been given the thing she will oppose him about for the rest of the volume.

**Ending type:** reversal of the reader's expectation, and a refusal.
**Next-chapter pull:** At the end of the day, the office he has never visited sends for him: a wet clerk, a receipt, and a question about a street clock in his own district.

### Chapter 7 — "Three Minutes"

**Type:** investigation; the first relationship with sustained friction; the batch's first small victory and first real failure.
**POV:** Orrin.
**Location/time:** A street clock in the Clockwork Quarter, then the Office of Public Reckoning, Cathedral Rise, a day and a half after the death. The chapter opens with the wet clerk and the receipt, which is the thing Chapter 6 ended on.

**Immediate goal:** Get the three minutes into a record that somebody is obliged to answer to.

**Resistance:** Mara Venn. She is an investigator for an office with audit authority over a stair and a written right to be ignored, and she has done her homework: **the clean certificate in Orrin's own hand, dated two days before the Reading**, a toll receipt for three district minutes charged to the Quarter, and a street clock in the Quarter that is three minutes slow and required a further two to be dragged back into agreement. Five minutes, not three — two of them are what it cost to correct a clock that had to be caught up by hand, and nobody has charged anybody for them, and the chapter should let that discrepancy sit on the page unremarked. It is Chapter 10 where Orrin notices the two numbers do not agree, and a writer must not quietly correct it here to three.

She thinks he is careless and possibly dangerous. He thinks she is hiding behind procedure and possibly correct. And the deepest resistance is not between them: it is that he cannot produce a single piece of evidence. Everything he knows happened at Weir Two is a feeling in a wet building. She can check a feeling against a document. His feeling has no document, because no document of it exists, and because the one that would exist requires him to certify a district toll he has no licence to certify, and because a man's private sense is not a fact.

**Information revealed:** The Office of Public Reckoning's actual, tiny, and precisely bounded authority. The legal hook that makes the batch's small victory possible: a Reading may be charged to any district's reserve, and the Compact requires a toll recorded, but nothing in forty-three years has required the paying district to be affected or to consent. A Reading charged to an unaffected district is legal, current, and unchallengeable, and Mara can put an objection on file about it. She quotes a rule, once, about calibration strain, in a tone that means she has a reason for the tone, and does not explain it. Orrin, for his part, tells her the one fact she can check: the blind margin line on the notice, its exact wording, and its size relative to the signature. She goes and measures it.

**Action or decision:** She files an objection on the toll receipt, on the ground that the paying district was neither affected nor consenting. It is accepted for filing, which is a real thing and a small thing, and it is the first crack in the forty-three-year habit. Meanwhile, the request for the Weir Two night roster goes upstairs and is referred, in one courteous sentence, to a room that is not open to petition.

**Emotional change:** He learns that the thing he is sure of is inadmissible, and she learns that he is not the fool she assumed, and neither of them tells the other anything true about themselves.

**Power or resource change:** The keeper's toll becomes undeniable when Mara notices that he cannot account for a full minute of a conversation he clearly had. Her reaction is not scepticism. It is professional fear, because she knows what a lost interval looks like, and she is the first person in the book to look frightened for him rather than at him. He notices her noticing, and the only person who can corroborate the hour-sickness he is describing is a man who is dead.

**Continuity fact to preserve:** The three-minute receipt is now an exhibit. The Archive of Unlisted is named for the first time. Mara's mother is not mentioned and must not be. The blind margin has been measured by a hostile witness and is exactly as small as it looks. The street clock is **five** minutes out, not three, and the two uncharged minutes are deliberate.

**Ending type:** cost, and a small victory that does not cancel it.
**Next-chapter pull:** The Archive room on Cathedral Rise, its keeper, and the discovery that the omission is not a mystery of machines but a question about a signature.

### Chapter 8 — "The Form Number"

**Type:** investigation and discovery; the batch's turn in the mystery.
**POV:** Orrin.
**Location/time:** The Archive room on Cathedral Rise, then a Weirs kitchen, one day.

**Immediate goal:** The night roster. Not the sealed room's version — any version, including a badly kept one. He has begun to understand that the thing that is missing is not hidden, it is *filed*, and that a filed thing has a form and a shelf and a clerk.

**Resistance:** Sindri Lake, keeper of the public Archive room, is not obstructive, and that is worse. He is helpful, precise, and immovable, and he explains — carefully, and without any pleasure — that the Archive room on the Rise **is** open to petition, and that the room Orrin actually wants is the one that is not, and that the difference between the two is a locked door and a different set of hands. He does it kindly. The public record of Weir Two shows a maintenance line, a period, a quantity of hours, and no names, because the record's purpose is the hours. Then the refused request comes back with a routing stamp, and the reason the roster is not in the public record is named: the night crew was removed, in a dated batch, to a sealed set of rooms under the Cathedral. And then the thing that changes the shape of the story: the removal carries a form number, and the form number is a **guild** form number, in the guild's own series, with a price schedule attached, because the omission was not a failure of the Cathedral's records. It was bought, scheduled, and certified by people whose licenses depend on it. Della Marsh, the guild secretary, can be made to look up a number. She is terrified of an inquiry and will do it for a fee and a closed door.

**Information revealed:** The Archive of Unlisted is real, sealed, not open to petition, and not empty. The removal of people happens in dated batches. The category on the form is not a name. And the second signature: the same batch carries the countersign of a Weirs pump warden, which is how Sel Kade's name enters this book's evidentiary record for the first time, on a document about people being made invisible. **Alma Festinger's name is on the same batch's routing, four removes away, and nobody connects it, which is the point.**

**Action or decision:** Orrin and Mara do not break into anything. They do the work they can actually do: they find a number, they find a signature, and they establish that the omission has a paper trail with a price on it, which means it can be litigated. **The Weirs kitchen is Wenna Pruitt's room**, and she is the one who says the sentence the chapter turns on: Orrin has the paper and does not have the person. She is a tenant-council organiser who has been keeping the pumping district's own shift book for eleven years and who says, without hostility, that a district which produces the hours and cannot produce anybody's signature is not going to be believed this century. She also has a copy of Bevin Sarr's board, or a better one, and she asks the first question in the book that Orrin cannot answer: whose name goes on the form when the guild's form says there is no crew.

**Emotional change:** The mystery changes shape in front of the reader. It is no longer about a machine Orrin can hear. It is about a signature, and the signature is on a form, and forms can be made to answer. He is not the centre of the case any more, and the reader can feel him not enjoying it.

**Power or resource change:** No gain and a hard limit, stated plainly in the scene: without a record there is no claim, and what he privately heard cannot become one. His private sense stops being the engine of the story and becomes a lead that points at a document.

**Continuity fact to preserve:** The Archive of Unlisted is named, sealed, and closed in this batch; it opens in Volume 02. **The Archive room and the Archive of Unlisted must never be confused in the narration, and the confusion is the plot** — `terminology.md` makes this explicit, so the prose must have Lake say which is which. *Non-certified standby* and the guild form number are linked in the reader's mind. Sel Kade's name is on the same paper as the guild's. Della Marsh, Wenna Pruitt, and Bevin Sarr's board are now live and all are a document away from trouble.

**Ending type:** discovery.
**Next-chapter pull:** The Weirs' clocks slip out of phase, the slip crosses the hospital line, and the only reserve that could absorb it is the one that is not there.

### Chapter 9 — "What the Ward Can Absorb"

**Type:** repair and controlled experimentation; the batch climax.
**POV:** Orrin.
**Location/time:** The Lower Weirs and the Green Span hospital trestle, two and a half days after the Reading, one night into the following morning. That interval is fixed: the Reading is on the day of the death, Chapter 7 is a day and a half later, Chapter 8 is a day, and this is the following night.

**Immediate goal:** Stop the slip. The Lower Weirs' clocks begin to repeat and skip, the hospital line over the canal goes with them, and a ward's medicine schedule depends on a clock that cannot hold its grip.

**Resistance:** Four things, and the third is the chapter.

There is a cause on the page, and it is not a retroactive network toll out of the Chapter 4 Reading: the notice charged three district minutes and a witness interval, and that is all it charged. The slippage is the ordinary late arrival of a district that has been spending hours it does not have. The pumping district's reserve is gone, its water schedule is gone, and its clocks have been corrected out of unlisted night shifts for twenty years. When a shift is not on the board, the correction lands somewhere, and tonight the somewhere is the district's synchronisation.

There is no Reading available, and the reasons are the batch's own rules rather than hand-waving. **A Reading is valid only for the conditions present when it was made**, and a slippage cascade changes its conditions by the minute, so a Reading taken now would describe a network that no longer exists by the time the notice is read. **The witness rail is booked a day ahead** and there is no rail tonight. And **the fee comes off a district reserve**, and the reserve is the thing that has failed.

The only reserve that could absorb the slip is the pumping district's diverted pump reserve, and it is gone, and the person who diverted it, **Sel Kade**, is standing in the pump house defending it on the grounds that it feeds a works that employs most of the people on that street, which is true, and which is precisely the moral shape this whole book is about. The Green Span hospital line runs over the hospital trestle, an old iron trolley trestle whose failing load has been quietly eating the hospital's clock pressure for longer than anyone has wanted to look at it, and looking at it is expensive and requires somebody to be certified. And Orrin is the only person in the room who can find the failed assembly, and he is the only person present with no standing to certify a single number he finds. **He certifies nothing in this chapter.** The licence violation that costs him his trade is reserved for Chapters 44–49 and must not be spent early.

**Information revealed:** Slippage as the ordinary late arrival of a district running on hours it does not have. The hospital trestle, its load, and the fact that a district's time can be healthy and its machinery starving at the same time. That a hospital runs several clocks and that their disagreement is a staffing problem, which is Leda's professional territory and the moment the reader understands what her job actually is. And what Leda can and cannot absorb, said in numbers, in a corridor, at three in the morning, by a woman who is not being brave and is being precise.

**Action or decision:** He does not run a Reading, because it would be meaningless. He does not fix it alone, because he finally notices that the whole story is about people he did not ask. He says out loud, in front of Teo's crew, Leda, and Sel Kade, what he intends to do, what he does not know, which of his readings is a guess, and what the repair will cost in minutes that somebody's morning will be short by. Then he works: a bearing, a shim, a sacrificial plate, a night's labour and Leda's count of the ward's slack and Teo's crew on the panel because two of them did the cathedral's own dampers last year for money. It works. The clocks hold. The trestle's load is exposed in daylight, in public, with the arithmetic available to anyone who wants to check it.

**Emotional change:** The first time in the book that Orrin acts in company, in the open, with the cost named in advance. It is not forgiveness, and Leda does not forgive him, and the scene does not ask her to. What changes is that she stops arguing about his character and starts arguing about his method, which for a sister is a considerable move.

**Power or resource change:** **Aftershock, used deliberately and named out loud.** He states a question about a named load on one assembly and gets an answer, and this time somebody else believes him and acts on it, which is the first evidence in the series that the ability is a civic instrument rather than a private symptom. Cost: a second, larger keeper's toll. Fine motor control in his right hand for two days, and the ringing in his right ear moves from an event to a condition.

**Continuity fact to preserve:** The hospital trestle is now a known, named, load-bearing problem that nobody has paid to fix, which is the volume climax's fuse. A hospital clock in Green Span disagrees with the public clock and Leda knows what that means. The Weirs' morning comes up short by a number of minutes that belongs to people, and the street knows whose. **The hearing damage here is not the series' final cost.** From here on the right ear rings above a given pitch, and dulls when he is tired, and that is all. The clean edge of a single machine, and the range, are what Volume 08 takes and then Volume 14 takes for good; a Volume 14 writer must not find this already banked.

**Ending type:** earned hook — the clocks hold, and the number that is missing afterwards is a number of people's minutes.
**Next-chapter pull:** The only place in Morrowfen where a number like that can be entered, so that it is a fact and not a rumour, is an office that audits a stair. And the office has to petition somebody.

### Chapter 10 — "The First Demand"

**Type:** aftermath and political pressure; the batch's defeat.
**POV:** Orrin.
**Location/time:** The Office of Public Reckoning, then a public-health committee room on Cathedral Rise, and the Weirs, one week after the death of Perry Vance.

**Immediate goal:** Get the missing time into a public record, and the two kinds kept apart. What he is asking for first is narrow and technical: the Reading's three minutes entered on the Toll Stair with the paying district named beside them, so that the charge is visible as a charge. What he actually wants is the second thing, and he cannot phrase it in a petition: **a name column and a duration column for body tolls**, the columns the Compact's fourth safeguard has required for forty-three years and the Directorate has only ever filled with the phrase *calibration strain.* He wants a fact, not an accusation, though he can no longer tell the difference.

**Resistance:** The Office can file an objection and cannot create a ledger. A ledger is a matter for the Assembly, and a matter for the Assembly is referred to a committee, and the committee that has the Weirs' missing time in front of it is the public-health committee, chaired by a Green Span delegate whose ward is the one whose clocks disagree. Councilor Imani Rook blocks the demand, and she is not a villain, and she is right about one half of it: a published body-toll ledger would panic four Green Span wards, and she has spent her career not causing panics, and she can point at the actual arithmetic. And she is wrong about the other half in a way the reader can see before Orrin can articulate it, which is that a cost nobody can name is a cost the city will keep paying, and the wards she is protecting are protected by the omission as much as by the ledger. **Alma Festinger is in the gallery and is asked, once, by a clerk collecting names, whether she understands what she signed at the rail in Chapter 4. She says no. She means it, and it is written down, and nobody follows it up.** Orrin is not equal in that room and never will be; the Clockwork Quarter sends nobody to a committee about the Weirs.

**Information revealed:** How the Assembly actually works: debate, division, referred to committee, read twice, entered in the minutes, discharged. The public-health committee's authority and its composition. Imani Rook's competence, her real stake through her sister in the nursing service, and the reason she is a recurring character for **three more volumes after this one and not thirteen** — she is an obstacle when she protects her district by privileging its clocks and an ally when hospitals are included in the reform. And the first appearance of a phrase the reader will meet repeatedly: who pays for the record.

**Action or decision:** They file the demand anyway and it is refused and referred to committee with a date, which is a defeat with a name and a date attached and is therefore the most useful kind. Mara keeps the objection and the receipts. Orrin, alone at the bench at the end, does two small things that are the whole point of the batch: he stops writing Perry Vance as a shift and writes him as a man, and he copies the three minutes and the night's minutes side by side in his private ledger and finds that the two numbers do not agree, and cannot say why, and writes that down too.

**Emotional change:** From keeping a secret to trying to make a record, and from the private shame of the first to the public exposure of the second. He fails, in a room, in front of a competent opponent, and he now has a named institution to fail against instead of only a private one.

**Power or resource change:** None gained, and that is the point. What he gains is a rule he writes at the top of his own ledger and has to keep: *nothing of mine is used again without its blind margin and its toll named in public, in a room, to somebody who can object.* The rule is not yet tested. The volume's test of it is the hospital pump in **Chapter 19**, where he is right and cannot certify and nothing happens.

**Continuity fact to preserve:** Imani Rook has blocked the demand for a public toll ledger. She files it herself about three chapters from now, having been shown arithmetic rather than sentiment — that is a promise owed to the reader inside this volume and it must not be paid early. The three minutes are the first entry in a public toll ledger that does not exist yet. The Archive of Unlisted remains shut. Sel Kade's reserve is a public fact as of this chapter; it becomes a documented diversion with a schedule and a named beneficiary later, and her tenant review is owed by about Chapter 46. **Orrin's class-three license renewal falls due in about eight weeks**, which is why the leash is slack in this chapter and tight in Chapter 12, and it must not be described as imminent. The renewal does not fall due inside Volume 01: what comes in Chapters 11–20 is its paperwork, and the volume's ending is a suspension issued ahead of Hask's own clock, not a renewal decision.

**Ending type:** reversal and decision. A refused demand is a better ending than a small win, because it gives the volume a direction and the reader a grudge worth carrying.
**Next-chapter pull:** A sealed room under the Cathedral cannot be opened by petition, and the only paper in Morrowfen with both a signature and a price on it is the guild's labour ledger. Hask has been asked to publish it. Hask has refused. Orrin has a license whose renewal is Hask's discretion, a regulator that is in no book anywhere, and eight weeks.

## Open questions after the batch

1. What is physically carrying load away from the public display, and why does it arrive in a clock as a ninth beat?
2. Why is Perry Vance's name in a batch, and who signed the batch's other line?
3. What did Hask's regulator stop, and who threw the handle?
4. How many other batches are there, and in which years?
5. Why does a hand valve in the Weirs have no instrument on it, and how long has that been true of every valve?
6. What is in the Archive of Unlisted, what is it for, and who has been reading it for eighteen years?
7. What is Edwin Vale's name on, and what is it not on? The photograph answers none of it, which is why the reader is allowed to ask.
8. Officially, no one is counting the missing minutes at the end of Chapter 9. The street knows whose they are and there is no column anywhere in Morrowfen that can be told. Who writes the number down first, and whose name goes beside it?
9. Alma Festinger signed at the rail in Chapter 4 because she was on a rota and did not know what a district standing was. She still says she did not know, in Chapter 10, and it is written down. When somebody who can act on it finally asks her, what happens? **This question is answered in Volume 06, in a gallery, and the reader should recognise the face.**
10. The street clock is five minutes out and only three minutes were charged. Orrin has written both figures in his ledger and cannot reconcile them. **Chapter 10 is where he notices; this stays open into batch 0002, and the two uncharged minutes are a real cost that nobody has been billed for.**
11. What happens to a man who has read a machine correctly, filed nothing, been refused at a door, and been asked by two institutions to stop?
12. Orrin's class-three renewal is eight weeks away and Hask holds it. What is the guild's price for silence, and does Hask ever intend to collect it in a form that is not a form?

## What this batch must not do

- Must not explain the ninth tick, the return line, the Censor Gate, the Dark Reservoir, or the First Hour. The reader gets a load arriving from below and nothing else.
- Must not give Orrin a filed Reading, a witness station, a district proposal, a licence he does not hold, or a number that counts.
- Must not let any other character carry the plot for him. Mara brings a legal instrument, not a clue to the machine. Teo brings a better account of the night, not a revelation. Leda brings arithmetic about a ward, not a solution. Wenna Pruitt brings the question he cannot answer, not the answer. Sel Kade brings a moral problem, not a confession.
- Must not make the Office of Public Reckoning powerful. It has a written right to be ignored and the batch ends with it ignored.
- **Must not make Orrin the cause of Vance's death, and must not make his blamelessness the point either.** The sequence is fixed in Chapter 5's card. Vance goes down on his own initiative to close a valve that the opened gate drove open again. Orrin felt the failure hours earlier and was refused at a locked door by people who were not corrupt. His guilt is that he has no instrument that turns what he knows into an action somebody else can take, and Chapter 5 says so in his own head once without letting him off.
- Must not let Teo be softened, recruited, or grateful. He is suspicious of Orrin on principle and correctly so.
- Must not let Ada Vance be a symbol. She is thirteen, she is on a tram, she asks at most one short question, and Orrin does not cross the street.
- Must not end on a cliffhanger, a whisper, or a stranger in a doorway.
- Must not spend a second Reading notice.
- Must not spend the licence violation that costs Orrin his trade. He certifies nothing in Chapter 9; that offence is Chapters 44–49.
- Must not introduce, name, or hint at the certainty permit, a Council of Hours, a district time council, a Reading room, a local instrument, or a Civic Time Keeper. None of them exist yet.
- Must not confuse the Archive room with the Archive of Unlisted. The confusion is the plot.
- Must not confuse *calibration strain*, the Directorate's word for a body toll, with *self-inflicted occupational strain*, the guild's word for an unfiled private reading. Both appear in Chapter 6 and are never used for each other again.
