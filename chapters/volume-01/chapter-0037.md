# Chapter 37 — A Question Filed Against a Man

Orrin Vale went down to the licensing floor on the Friday morning of the twenty-first of the fourth month for a thing that took ninety seconds, and the thing that took ninety seconds was asking, for the fifth time in eleven weeks, whether the hall keeps a record of the hours worked at Weir Two at night.

Della Marsh was not on the counter. The man on the counter was twenty-four and had been in the building a fortnight and had the run of the rack and the run of the mat and the manner of a man who has not yet been told which questions are worth asking.

"You'd want a form," the young man said. "GH-9 is the request form and there's a card, and I can post you one if you leave an address, or you can take it now."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what we've got." He said it without any edge at all, and it was true, and it is what the hall's position has been since the first month, and Orrin had heard it in better voices.

Then Della Marsh came round the end of the counter with an inventory tray in each hand and put them down, which is what she does when she is going to stay.

"Don't," she said to the young man, and he went.

"You're the fifth person in eleven weeks to ask me that and I am not going to be asked it a sixth time on a Friday, and you have not come for it." She put her hands on the counter. "You've come because the man from the works telephoned the licensing floor on Thursday about a bracket and there is a line in a store book and every clerk in this building heard about it, and you have come to see whether anybody here has connected it to a maintainer."

"Nobody has."

"Nobody has." She looked at him for a moment. "Mr Vale, I am going to tell you the number of a form that is in your file. I am not going to tell you what it is about, because I am not able to, and I am not going to tell you who put it there. And I am telling you the number because there is a card at the front of every holder's file in this building with a list on it, and if you have not looked at your own card in six months then somebody else has, and I would rather it was you that found out from me than from a clerk with a mat and no instructions."

---

The card is a printed card about the size of a postcard with a linen rule across it, and it is not new: holders in this hall have had one since before the war, and the rule is the same rule as on the W-4, which is a thing nobody has ever thought about, which is that the hall's whole filing practice is a system of forms with a ruled list of what has been lodged against a name, going in one direction only.

Orrin's card had eleven lines in it and ten of them were his own, and the eleventh line was not.

> *No. 11 — 21 Feb — W-4 — class three, annual — Vale, O., Verge Lane.*
> *No. 12 — 19 Mar — W-4 — class three, renewal — Vale, O., Verge Lane. Fee 4/6. Endorsed.*
> *No. 13 — 14 Apr — Q — 6/4 — filed by the hall.*

That is the whole of it. Three lines and a type. The type is a letter he did not know and there is a column for the type and there are seven other types on the card, and four of them he knew: **D** for defect note, **C** for certificate, **I** for inquiry, **W** for work book, **P** for petition, **G** for general correspondence, and **A** for application, and the renewal is an application, and Orrin had read his own card eleven times in six years and had never once asked what the other letters were, because a card in a building is not a thing a man reads for entertainment.

He went and found the board.

The licensing floor has one board and it is not the notice card, and it is not a rule board either: it is a board of printed standing orders, put up by the forms printer along with eleven others about the use of this hall, and Orrin had read it four times between the twenty-seventh of the second month and the nineteenth of the third, and had read the fifth notice on it about a two-hour window on an expiry day, and had not read the rest of it, because the rest of it was about the use of the building.

**Notice nine.**

> *A question may be put against a holder by a member, or by the hall on behalf of a member, upon a matter concerning that holder's returns to this hall. A question is not read to the holder. It is not to be discussed with the holder. It is answered in writing within twenty-one days of the lodging of the number, and the answer is entered on the holder's card. A question does not suspend any right of a holder, and does not alter any term, and does not require an interview, and none will be offered. A member who wishes to put a question may do so in the ordinary way and without notice to the holder.*

Nine lines on a board in Orrin Vale's own trade, in his own hall, put up by a forms printer who has never met him.

---

Gerren Hask was on the floor at about half past eleven and he was in a good coat, and he was not on his way anywhere, and he had been there since ten, which Orrin understood to be a thing a man does on a Friday.

"Six weeks," said Hask. "You are here at half past eleven on a Friday and I have been here since ten and there is nobody in this building to have a cup of tea with. That is the first Friday since the nineteenth of the third month on which I have not had anybody to have a cup of tea with, and I am not complaining, because on the nineteenth I had you, and it was the best tea of that week."

"Master."

"Sit down. No, do not sit down on that bench, it is a bench for the examination of work books and a man who sits on it is a man who has come to be examined, and I will not have that in my building without a reason." He took a cup. "How is the year?"

"Busy."

"Everybody says busy. It is not busy, it is a year with a lot of rain in it and a great many clocks that want attention in the same fortnight." He drank his tea. "You have not come about the fee, and you are not due until the nineteenth of next year, and I know the date as well as you do because I write it in a book. So you have come about something and you are going to work round to it, and I am going to let you, because I have got nothing else and I would rather be got round to than wondered about."

---

He was not got round to. He talked for about twenty minutes, warm, and about four minutes of it was a boy.

"There is a lad on the floor, seventeen, whose father is on the district works at the pumping station, and he is quick and he is honest and he has no ear at all, which is not a fault at his age, and I have been telling him for a year that he will not make a maintainer with no ear and he will not make a *keeper* with no ear either, and he will make something." Hask turned the cup round twice on the saucer and did not look up. "I am not going to put a boy in your shop because your shop is yours and I have no business in it. If you ever want one, say so, and I will send him down on a Tuesday and I will not ask you why, and if you do not want him I will not mention it again, and I want that on the table between us now and not later."

"I will think about it."

"No, you will not, and I did not expect you to, and I have had the conversation with three men this year and I know what *I will think about it* is worth." He put the cup down. "Second thing, and this is housekeeping and there is no conversation in it. The hall opens a second work book for a holder in the middle of his term when he has filled one, and I have had two requests for early opening this month, both from men on the east side who had a wet autumn. I will not open yours. When you have filled the first one, bring it up and I will open the second, and I will not ask what is in it, and if it is the same forty lines it will be the same forty lines. That is the whole of it."

"That is very good of you, Master."

"It is the practice and I have never had to explain it once in thirty-one years and I do not propose to start." He looked up then. "Now. You have not asked me a question and I am going to let you go, and when you have a question you will come on a morning, and I will be here on the twenty-eighth, at ten, which I have done for twenty-six years, and if the twenty-eighth is a Sunday then it is a Sunday, and you will not be the first man I have had to see on a Sunday and you will not be the last."

He said the date the way he always said the date, in a room with tea in it, and it was not a threat and it was not a favour, and Orrin walked down Verge Lane afterwards with the number 13 on a card in his head and no question to put it with, because he did not know that it was a question.

---

He worked it out on the Saturday morning, at the bench, with the W-4 in front of him, and the type on the card was **Q**, and the hall's own form list on the counter had a Q in it, and a Q is a question, and the question was *6/4*, which is the hall's own numbering — a series figure and the month of the year — and it is the sixth of its kind this month, and the hall has answered five of them, probably to five other men.

The W-4 he had lodged on the nineteenth of the third month is at the licensing floor in a file, and it is two sheets, and it has a ruled table of three columns, and the recommendation column is a third of an inch wider than the others, and it has on it, in his own hand, of his own motion, on the sixteenth of the third month in a shop:

> *Weir Two floodgate and second-stage pumping station. That the maintaining hall note that the works' certified shift return for this station is not consistent with the station's own board.*

He had written that in a form because there was no column anywhere else, and it had gone into the hall in his own hand, and five days later the hall had put a question about it, and the question had been filed on the fourteenth of the fourth month, which was a Friday, and which is seven days ago, and which had been sitting in his file since then without his knowing, in a building he works four hundred yards from, on a card he has read eleven times in six years.

---

Here is what the hall's records establish, and he did it on Saturday morning with the W-4 in front of him and the card in front of him, and it took about an hour, and it is the correct use of a well-run system.

The hall keeps a certification ledger, which is a record of machines. Weir Two appears in it, as a machine, with a maintenance line and a period and a condition, correctly and honestly, and the day maintenance line runs to a figure in the region of fourteen hundred hours for the year, which is right and which is not in dispute.

The hall does not keep a record of hours for work that is not part of a certified shift, there being no such hours, there being no such shifts.

There is therefore no certified shift return for the night pumping at Weir Two. There is no such document, has never been such a document, and cannot be made into one at any price the hall would accept.

The hall's labour book, series 3, carries a line for the site in the maintenance-deferred class, batch 21/4, one crew, three nights, in a hand that is a different hand from the eleven lines above and below it, entered on a day when the ink above it has a Sunday in it, and that line has been recopied fair eleven times by a copyist who did not know what a standby was.

So: a class-three maintainer has entered in a hall form that the works' certified shift return for a station is not consistent with the station's own board. And the hall's own records establish that there is no such return to be inconsistent with anything. And therefore the statement he made is, on the hall's records, unsupported — not false, because it is not a claim about the return's contents but about its relation to a board; **unsupported**, in the way that a claim is unsupported when the document it depends on does not exist.

And the board is a painted board in a pump house. It is not a record of the hall. It is not before the hall. It cannot be exhibited to a hall, produced to a hall, entered on a hall's form, or described in a hall's form, because a form that describes a chalk board must contain a figure, and a figure of a district's hours is a district return, and a district return may be certified only by a licence above the third class or by the district that owns the reserve.

He put his hand flat on the bench, which is not a thing he does, and then took it off again.

The answer that the hall's own records support is the answer he already knows: the board is not before the hall and the return is not in the ledger and the maintainer is unable to produce either.

The answer that is true is the answer he cannot write: that the hall's labour book contains a line in a second hand, on a day when the ink above it has a Sunday in it, and that a fair copy of that line was made eleven times and cannot carry a hand, and that the hours it records are the hours his recommendation was about, and that no document in the hall contains them and no document in the hall can.

And to put that in writing, in a hall form, in answer to a hall question, he would have to state the number of the hours. Three hundred and eighty-eight hours in four months, off a board and a kitchen book, unverified, compiled by the district, and printed in the Assembly's own minutes on the twentieth of the third month, which is the only document in the city that carries it, and which says on its face that the returns are compiled by the district and are unverified.

To state it to the hall is to enter a district figure in a hall's form. To enter it as true is to certify it. **And a class-three maintainer may not certify a district figure, and the reason is the reason the Weirs have no standing, and the reason is the same reason, and it is written in the guild ordinance in one sentence and Orrin has read it in every room he has ever been licensed in.**

So the honest answer to the guild's question is a licence offence.

That is the shape of it, and he sat with it for about an hour, and it was the most completely honest thing he had worked out since the fifteenth of the first month, and there is no form in the city that will hold it.

---

On the Saturday afternoon he wrote four answers on four sheets and took them up to the flat above the bathhouse and put them on the table and read them as four documents, which is what a man does who has spent a month writing things out of a book that is not a form.

The first was the answer the hall expected, in the hall's own words, four lines, true, and it would have been accepted, and it would have said: the board is not a record of the hall, the return is not in the ledger, the maintainer is unable to produce either, and the hall is not able to assist further.

The second was longer and said that the station's board exists, that the maintainer has seen it, that a district's own count of its hours is printed in the Assembly's minutes, and that the maintainer is a class-three maintainer and has no standing to certify a district figure and has not certified one. That one was true and it told the hall where to look and it named no person who could refuse it, because he had not asked one.

The third was the true one, and it was four lines long and it said: the hall's labour book, series 3, for this site, carries a line in a hand that differs from the lines above and below it, entered on a day when the ink above it carries a Sunday; a fair copy of that line has been made eleven times; the evidence that the line was not entered as part of the works' ordinary copying is the hand, and a hand cannot be exhibited; and the maintainer is therefore unable to place before the hall the difference between a fair copy and an original, and is of the opinion that the difference is the whole of the matter.

The fourth was one line and it was the one he did not send.

> *I am unable to answer this question, and I am unable to give a reason that the hall will accept, and the reason is that the honest answer would require me to certify a figure belonging to a district, and my licence does not permit it, and I would rather be recorded as a man who cannot answer than as a man who has answered.*

---

He looked at the four sheets for a long time and he worked out what he was actually choosing between, which was not truth, because three of the four were true, and it was not the argument, because he had that by heart, and it was the rule at the top of the grey ledger, which is the only piece of his own method that has ever done anything at all.

*No result of mine goes out of this room without a person named who can refuse it.*

The first answer went to the hall. Nobody in the Weirs had been asked. The second answer went to the hall and told a hall where a document was, and nobody in the Weirs had been asked. The third answer went to the hall and put the only piece of evidence there has ever been about a night crew into a guild's file, and nobody in the Weirs had been asked, and in particular the five people whose hands and whose board it was had not been asked, and one of them is dead.

He had a week. The question was filed on the fourteenth and the answer is due on the fifth of the fifth month and it was the twenty-second of the fourth, and he had a telephone in a shop and a district four miles away that will speak to him at any hour of any day, and a woman in a kitchen who has been waiting nineteen years for somebody to need a number she already had.

He wrote on the reverse of the second sheet, and then he tore it off, and then he wrote it out again on the fourth sheet, and the fourth sheet is the one that went in the envelope.

> *Valued maintenance district, fourth month, 22nd inst. Question 6/4 put by the hall.*
>
> *Answer. The maintainer has not been able to obtain the co-operation of any person in the district to produce a record, and the reason is not a refusal. The district's own count of its night hours is the only figure that exists and it is the district's and not the hall's, and the maintainer has no standing to enter it in a form of this hall, and will not enter it without the standing.*
>
> *The maintainer further states that he wrote the recommendation to which the question refers of his own motion, in the belief that a form is a room, which it is not, and that the maintainer is aware that this has placed the district's hours in a file of this hall without any person in the district having been asked, and that he did that and would do it again and is sorry for it in the way a man is sorry for a thing that was correct.*

The envelope went to the licensing floor on the Monday morning and the clerk initialed the receipt, and the card at the front of the file got a twelfth line, and there is a copy of it in the grey ledger, and the number of the answer is 8/4, and nobody in this city will ever ask him about it.

The count of his own hand corrections is two. It was two on Friday and it was two on the Monday. The question is closed in a file and he is a licensed maintainer and he did not commit an offence of any kind, and the work book for the coming year will be opened when the first is full, and there is no inquiry and no letter and no interview, and the hanging thing is that the answer he sent said, in eleven words, that no person in the district was asked, and that eleven words are the only part of the entire business that will survive him.
