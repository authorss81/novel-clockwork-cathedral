# Chapter 120 — Where Numbers Arrive

The numbers arrived on the Friday the twenty-fourth of the third month in an envelope with a woman's handwriting on it and a printed card inside it, and nobody took anything, and there was no night porter and no raid and no man in a coat.

The letter said that the writer lived at number four in one of the four streets, that the writer had bought a hundred minutes in January from a pitch in Pindar Row, that the writer was not able to say what the brass was for, and that the writer enclosed a card because the card had four streets and four figures on it, and the writer had read the four figures four times and could not tell which of them was her own street, and would be obliged if somebody who knew would tell her. The last line said that the writer was sorry for the trouble and that the writer would not send anything else.

The card was Corvin Hale's rate card, printed at his own expense and given away free at the end of the row, and it had the price on it and the four figures on it, and under the four figures, in the same type, the four streets.

That was the whole of the route. A man had answered a question in public, in chalk, at the end of a row, on a card, and a frightened woman had asked him for one and had put it in an envelope, and the Office of Public Reckoning had opened it on a Friday morning with one window and four staff.

Mara Venn read it standing up, twice, and then carried it to the counter and read it a third time with the counter against her hip the way a person reads a thing they are going to have to be asked about by somebody who is not pleased.

"It is correct," she said. "Every figure on it. I checked it against the daily sheet and the daily sheet is printed by this office in the morning and the figures have not moved and a hundred minutes is a hundred and twenty nights at fifty seconds and that is what his card says. The four streets are the four streets. He has not made anything up and he has not lied to anybody and he has put a list of four streets on a piece of printed paper and given it away."

"Who wrote to you?"

"A woman at number four in the second of them, and she has not sent anything else, and she has signed her name, and her name is in this file and not in that drawer." Mara Venn put the card down and squared it. "Orrin. I am going to enter this. Received and noted, with the date, on the top sheet, and it is not a certification and it is not admissible, and I am going to write that in the margin in the same hand, and I am going to do it in the room and not in the drawer, and I want you to hear the reason."

"Go on."

"There is a drawer in this building," said Mara Venn, "and I put the tank in it in December deliberately and said so out loud first, because a number an office cannot act on and cannot refuse is a worse thing than a number in a drawer. That was right and I would do it again. And this one does not go in the drawer, because a drawer is where a thing goes when a person is protecting somebody, and I am not protecting anybody today. Today four streets have come into a building with a window in it off an envelope, and there is no instrument in this city that can take them out again, and if I put them in a drawer then in four years somebody will find them in a drawer and ask me why, and the answer will be *to protect them*, and it will sound like a good reason, and it will be the reason this city has spent eighteen years."

She wrote the entry standing up, on the top sheet, in the margin, in her own hand, where anybody coming into the room could read it upside down.

*Received and noted, 24th third. A printed card of a market rate, with four streets named. Not a certification. Not admissible in support of any demand. Entered and not withdrawn.*

"The Office's own figure is on the ward's return as a schedule and it is fifty-one thousand four hundred and eighteen," she said, "and it goes in no calculation of mine and I have not multiplied it by anything, and there is a range on two boards outside the Archive on Cathedral Rise which I have not copied and which nobody in this room is going to multiply, and I want that said out loud this morning because in about a month somebody will do it and it will be done by somebody who has read one of the two and not the other, and it will be in a pamphlet."

"What will be done to you?" Orrin said.

"Published, if I publish," said Mara Venn. "I will publish it with the working, in the autumn, in a document a member can carry into a lobby, and on the day after the day I lodge it somebody will ask me why a clerk of the Office thinks the market should keep existing, and I will have to answer that as a person and not as an office, and I said in January I would and I have not changed my mind and I have now got a card with four street names on it to help me."

She put the card in a drawer and the drawer was not the one with the tank in it, and she shut it, and then she opened it again and put the card on the counter on top of the blotter where anybody could pick it up and read it, and said, "That is the only privilege this office has and I am going to use it once a week and I am not going to be thanked for it and if I am I will not thank you back."

On the Saturday the twenty-fifth the same figures arrived in two other buildings without anybody sending them anywhere.

They arrived in the Weirs tenant council's rooms because the notice run had gone out on the Saturday before with the minute of the sixteenth, and a woman who had been in the room on the Wednesday had been in a shop in Bellweather Road on the Friday and had seen the rate card, and had put it in her apron and had brought it up the stairs behind a chandler's shop, and Ivy Pike put it face up on the table and looked at it for about a minute and said, "That is a list of addresses printed in type and given away."

And they arrived in a corridor on the second landing of a hospital because a porter of about thirty-one, who had been on the Friday's delivery, had said at eight o'clock on the Saturday morning, in a corridor, with his hand on a card, that the G ward had three patients from that street and the one below had four and the one on the other side of the city had none, and a ward sister of about fifty had said, "Which street," and he had said the name, and she had said, "Then that is eight patients out of eleven on a card with a column headed with a date and two watches' difference in it, and a rate card in a shop in Bellweather Road with four streets and four figures on it, and the two have never been in the same room and now they are in the same conversation at eight o'clock on a Saturday, and that is how it happens. Nothing has been copied and nobody has broken anything. I have simply been handed two true things at once and I am the only person in this building holding both of them, and I am a ward sister, and by the end of the month I will have said the number four out loud in a corridor to somebody I did not mean to say it to."

Nobody put them in the same book. That is the point, and it is the reason the twenty-seventh of the third month runs as long as it does.

On the Sunday the twenty-sixth nothing happened, and the arcade was shut, and the board over number fourteen carried the reason in Hale's chalk, and Orrin did not go to Hobden Street or to Weir Lane or to any counter in this city, and the figure on the card went on being true in three buildings without anybody doing anything at all to it.

And on the Sunday afternoon, at about four o'clock, five of them were in a kitchen nine feet by eleven on the fourth floor of a boardinghouse in Bellweather Road, and it was not a meeting of anything, because there was nothing to meet about, and there was no Assembly man and no Directorate man and no guild man in it, and a kitchen is where this city does its business when it is being honest.

There was a list on a nail. There was a gas ring. There were Miriam Coss, thirty-four, with a token on the table in front of her that had been carried up and down four flights of stairs every day since the seventeenth of the first month; Nella Rook, twenty-eight, off shift; a conductor of thirty-three called Nita Bramhall who had walked up Bellweather Road on her day off and had brought a ticket roll; Teo Arden, twenty; and the man with the lamp, who had come because the porter on the second landing of the hospital had told a ward sister, and the ward sister had told a nurse, and the nurse had rung the shop.

They disagreed inside about eleven minutes.

"It goes to somebody," Teo said. "That card, that book, the four streets. It goes to a person who may be asked, and there is such a person, and I have met her, and she sits in a building with a window and she writes things down and puts the date on them."

"That is the Office of Public Reckoning and you are describing handing a list of doors to the only office in this city that counts things," said Nella Rook. "And the twenty-six addresses in Weir Lane have a four-line undertaking on them signed by four people in October with the price of it named out loud three times, and a day book is a list of addresses, and Hale offered in February to sign four lines if anybody brought them, and nobody brought them, and that is the answer and it has been the answer for four months."

"Nobody is entitled to the twenty-six," said Teo. "Nobody is entitled to Bellweather Road either. The undertaking is not about entitlement, it is about what a person with a list does with it in his head on a Tuesday, and a man in a pitch is a person with a list, and I am not asking anybody to take anything off him, I am saying there is a difference between a list in a locked drawer and a list on a counter, and everybody in this room knows it, and the difference is about fourpence and a Tuesday."

"It is about a Tuesday," Nita Bramhall said, "and it is about a door. A list of doors is a list of routes if the door is a house and a person is standing in it, and it is a list of stops if the door is a shelter and nobody is, and you cannot write down which one it is, because the thing that decides it is not the list. That is my trade. I have carried a timetable with about ninety stops on it for twenty-one years and I could not tell you, on any single day, which stops were a list of people and which were a list of walls, and on the worst day of my life it was a list of both and I was doing it at forty miles an hour."

Four of them looked at the woman at the table with the token on it, and Miriam Coss said, "I have been in this kitchen for two hours and I have not said anything and I am going to say the thing, and the thing is that there are four figures on that card and I know which street I am standing in and I cannot tell which of the four figures belongs to it."

Nobody could tell her. That was the point and it was also true, and there was no form in this city on which a person may look up how far her own street's clocks are out, and the fact that a street is a fact about a house and not about her did not help in the least, because the card does not say which figure belongs to which house, and the card is the only paper in the city that has all four on it.

"So there it is," said Miriam Coss. "A man has printed four figures and four street names on a card and given it away at the end of a row, and I have read it four times, and I cannot tell from it which of the four figures is my street, and it is the only one of the four things on that card I cannot do anything with, and the other three are prices."

She pushed the token toward the middle of the table.

"Nine weeks and six days," she said. "I have carried that up and down those stairs since the seventeenth of the first and I have not spent it and I am not going to, and I would like somebody to say one true thing about it, and there is nobody in this city who can, and I have now asked four of you and one of you found out how late my bell is and did not charge me for it and I have not forgotten that, and it is the only thing anybody has done for me since the seventeenth of the first and it was not about the token at all."

Teo Arden put his hands flat on the table, which he does, and which he has done since he was seventeen, and said the thing that he had said in a room in Bellweather Road in the fifteenth of the first month and had not said since.

"A list of addresses is a list of routes."

Nella Rook looked at him.

"I have heard you say that."

"You have," said Teo. "I said it in January in this district and a man on a public counter in Weigh Lane wrote it down in a book because of it, and he has ten lines in that book in nineteen years and four of them are doors, and he did not ask me to write it and I did not ask him to keep it, and I have not been thanked and I am not going to be, and I have just said it a second time and I said it the first time to a room that was arguing about a laundry."

"And now?"

"Now the difference is that the second time there are five of us in a kitchen and one of us is on the card," he said, "and the second time I am saying it about a card that was printed and given away and not about a book in a drawer, and the second time it is worse, because a printed card is a thing a frightened person will put in an envelope, and she did, and it is in a building with a window in it, and I am not saying print it and I am saying *know what it is*."

The room did not agree. That is what happened in that kitchen, and nobody tried to make it agree, and there is no committee anywhere in that kitchen and there never will be, and one of them was twenty and had no title and gave no order on anything, and one of them was a nurse and one a conductor and one a woman with a token and one a clockmaker who had come because a porter on a hospital landing said four words in a corridor.

On the Monday the twenty-seventh of the third month the figures were in three buildings and they could not be un-arrived, and no instrument in Morrowfen can un-arrive anything, and the reason is that nobody took them.

A printed card at the end of a row, given away free. An envelope, posted, signed, apologised for. A counter with a blotter and a card face up on it. An apron in a shop. A corridor at eight o'clock on a Saturday. And on the Wednesday, in a room with one window, the woman who had entered it was going to publish it in the autumn with the working on it, and the woman who had written the letter was at number four in one of the four streets and was not going to be found, and did not want to be.

Nobody stole anything. Nobody broke a rule. Nobody forged a card or copied a book or lifted a single line out of the twenty-six, which is still twenty-six, and the undertaking on it is unaltered, and the households are still not told, and the twenty-two are still not the twenty-six.

The number said in a room goes where the number goes, and this time it went into three buildings and out through a window into an envelope and down a street to a kitchen where a woman of thirty-four turned a brass disc that had been in a pocket for nine weeks and six days, worn bright on one edge, and could not tell which of the four figures on a printed card was her street, and that was on the Monday, and it had been true since the middle of January, and it took nine weeks and six days and a printed card and five people in a kitchen and nobody's thanks to get here.
