# Chapter 17 — A Dead Pump, a Timer, and a Joint

The dead pump was in a yard on the Kestrel side of Northglass, under a corrugated roof, on the back of a cart belonging to a man who hired out the cart and had no interest in the pump except that it was in the way and the rent was on it.

It was a second-stage cistern pump of the same family as Weir Two's, eleven years old, seized solid, with the whole of its upper works broken out and the shaft turned round to an angle that no manufacturer would have sanctioned.

"It's dead," said Orrin.

"It's dead," said the cart man. "It's been dead since Michaelmas and I want it out before the rain."

"Right." Orrin put his case down on the boards. "I'll want an hour and I'll want the roof off that corner, and I'll want you to be able to say I was here."

"Cost?"

"There is no cost. There is no licence that covers a dead pump in a private yard and I am not going to pretend there is. I am doing it because I have three days and no practice."

The man looked at him for a moment and then went and got a spanner and helped him take the roof off, which took an hour and a half instead of an hour, and was the single most useful thing that happened to Orrin Vale that month.

---

He had the pump dead cold under his hands by ten and he sat down on an upturned crate and worked out the question.

The rule he had set himself on Wednesday morning, with Leda's soup going cold in front of him and the name of a form number in his head, was not a rule he had found anywhere. It was a thing he had assembled out of the only evidence he had, which was a man dead in a well and a woman on a tram.

*Ask one thing. Ask it about a named load. Know beforehand what it is you are asking, and be able to say afterwards what you asked.*

He had been frightened of the ninth beat for a fortnight and he had not been frightened of this, which was the interesting part, because the ninth beat came from underneath and cost nothing and told him nothing, and this cost something and told him something, and he understood that the whole of his trade for eleven years had been the practice of the second and that nobody had ever once written it down.

He put his right palm flat on the crank housing, which was a named load, and asked, in the plainest words he could find:

"If this pump is put back into service next week and worked a full shift, what fails first?"

The answer came up through the casing the way a word comes up through a floor. It was not a picture. It was a weight, leaning, and it said: the second-stage gland, because the gland is the top bearing and the top bearing has been dry for eleven years, and if it is asked to hold a head it will hold it for about two hours and then it will let go sideways.

It took about two seconds.

Then Orrin's ears filled with a low hum on the right side, a smell of hot metal came up the back of his throat, and there was a stretch of about ninety seconds that he could not account for: the roof was off and then he was under it, and the cart man was saying something about a hoist, and the hoist was on the far side of the yard, and the hoist was not in the room.

Ninety seconds. Not an hour. Not a night.

He wrote it in the grey book on the page for the third, standing up, in the yard, in the rain that had started:

*Northglass, private yard. Second-stage cistern pump, seized, eleven years. Asked: if returned to service and worked a full shift, what fails first. Answered: second-stage gland, top bearing dry, holds about two hours, then lets go sideways. Cost of the question: ninety seconds, edges clean, road, roof, hoist. I cannot account for the ninety seconds. A narrow question about a named load. I will pay this one and know it in advance. One reading cost this month, ninety seconds, and two tolls in the whole of my life, and the difference between the two is not size. It is that I saw this one coming, and I did not see the other two.*

Then he looked at what he had written and put a line under it.

*That is not a keeper's toll. A toll is what happens when you ask something you cannot pay for.*

He looked at the gland on the ground, which was where a motor fitter had put it eleven years ago and where it still was, split down one side like a nut that had been cut rather than broken, and got the cart man to take him to the address in his book and then took a spanner to the gland and proved the whole of it to the man with three questions and a thumbnail.

"You'd have had the head off the yard in a fortnight," he said.

"Would I."

"Second-stage gland's split. It's on the ground under a tarpaulin. The top bearing's dry and there's no grease in that housing at all. Fix the gland and grease it and it will run." He wiped his hands. "That's not a reading. That's a look at two things and a thumb."

"That's what I said, though."

"You didn't say it. You said it was dead."

"Well, it was dead," said the cart man, and gave him a shilling for the hour and a half, which Orrin took, and which was the first money he had taken in a fortnight for work that was not in a book.

---

The hospital timer was in Green Span and it was a matter of a ward sister and eleven minutes of a doctor's time.

It was a small brass instrument on the wall outside the pharmacy, an interval timer, forty-five minutes, wound and set by the sister at the start of every drug round, and on that morning it was losing four and a half minutes a round, and the sister had done the arithmetic on the back of a duty list and had it written down, and she was extremely tired and extremely certain.

"It's the spring," she said. "They always say it's the spring."

Orrin took it down and had it in pieces in ten minutes.

"New spring's in the box," he said. "That one's not tired. Look at it. That is a good spring, it has never been out of this case. What is wrong is the click is set a quarter of an inch too deep, so the second hand drops past the mark and the count comes up short by a hair every time." He put it back together and set it against the corridor clock and the ward clock and let it go four rounds.

It kept time to a second and a half.

"That is nothing," said Orrin, "and it has been nothing for about six months, and it is four and a half minutes a round, and eleven rounds a day, and you have written it down, which is the whole of the reason I was here. The spring is a good spring and I could not have told you that on Monday."

The sister looked at the timer for a while.

"You could have told me the click was deep. Without the other thing." She put the timer back on its bracket and looked at the window, where the light had gone from grey to a hard blue. "Because I have got the ward at half past four, and the ward has not got anybody spare, and I have sent for my sister's girl twice this week and she is coming on the bus tomorrow, and if you could have said *the click is deep* at half past ten instead of *the second will be* at twenty to twelve, I could have had the round finished and I would not have had to send for a girl from across the city who is going to be paid by somebody for it." She did not raise her voice and she was not accusing him of anything. "You are not wrong. You are just about an hour after I needed you, and that is the whole of what has happened, and I have written it down."

He did not touch it again for twenty minutes. He stood in the corridor outside the pharmacy with his hand on his own case, and the whole of the third floor was inside that door, and the ward clock by the ward door was on the line, and the line was eleven miles off somewhere and correct, and he could feel, as clearly as he could feel the corridor's own draught, that the ward's whole morning was sitting in that clock and that if he asked it one question he would be told when the round would break, and that the answer would be true, and that it would be paid for out of the answer, and that the bill would arrive in about four days in a form with the phrase *calibration strain* on it and no name and no duration and no date, and that he had exactly nothing to do with that.

He asked, because he was not yet the man who did not ask.

"Third floor, ward clock by the door," he said, under his breath, to nobody. "If this ward's round runs to the schedule the sister is writing tonight, what gives first?"

It came up out of the case in about a second and it was a small answer, a small honest answer: a woman called by the pharmacy, at about four, who will be the only one left on the corridor.

And then there was a second thing, sitting behind the first, exactly where the weight of a thing sits when the rope is heavier at the far end. He felt it arrive and he felt where it went, and it was going to the whole of the third floor, and if he went on he would have the floor, and the floor would be a question about a woman called at four, and the cost of the floor would be that woman's afternoon, and he did not know her name and could not find it out and would not be permitted to write it down.

He stopped.

It was the hardest thing he did that month and there was nobody watching him do it.

It took him four seconds. The room went strange and grey at the edges and he had to put the flat of his hand on the case to know it was there, and then he was on the floor of the corridor with his back against the wall and somebody was saying *are you all right, sir* and a tea trolley had gone over further down.

"How long was that?" he said.

"You went white and then you were on the floor," said the ward sister. "About eleven minutes. It is now twenty past one. You asked me at ten past and I have been standing here the whole time and I could not make you hear me for the first four of them, and I have been standing here shouting your name since."

"Sorry."

"You are not hurt. Are you hurt."

"No." He got his hand up and got it open on the fourth try, and it stayed open for about a minute and a half and then closed, badly, with the little finger late. "That's not nothing, though."

"What isn't?"

"The click was the deep one," said Orrin. "Twenty minutes. Thank you."

He walked out into the courtyard and sat on a bench in it and got the grey book out of the case, and his hand would not close cleanly, and it did not close cleanly for the rest of the Saturday and all of the Sunday, and he found he could not turn a regulator pin with it at all, which was the test he had used on himself since he was nineteen.

*Third floor, ward clock. Asked one question, got one answer, stopped at the second. Answer was: a woman called by the pharmacy at about four, no name, no column, no date, four days out.*

*Cost of the question: eleven minutes, the whole of them in a corridor I was standing in, and the right hand, which will not close until Monday. Entered as a cost and not as a toll. A toll is what happens when you ask something you cannot pay for, and a question that stops itself is a question that pays. Two reading costs this month and two tolls in my life, and the difference is not size. It is that I saw both of these coming, and I saw neither of the others.*

*A narrow question is payable. A question with a second part is not, and you do not learn that by asking.*

---

Ferrywright Bridge at twenty past seven on the Sunday morning, with the river down and the iron cold enough to hurt, and there was no water on it and nobody on it and nothing in the Weirs that was running to a schedule that mattered.

He walked the deck with the lamp off. He put his hand on the third joint from the north end, which was a cast shoe forty years old and the only part of that bridge anybody had ever looked at, and he asked it the question.

Nothing came up.

Not a small answer. Not a lean. He stood on a cold iron bridge for four or five minutes and he put his hand on the joint and asked and there was no answer at all, and he asked it again in different words, and he got nothing, and the joint was a piece of cold metal with no load on it and no water behind it and no one waiting on the answer.

He walked back across and stopped at the corner, where there was a street clock on a bracket over a chandler's, and it said eight minutes fast, and Orrin had a bar out of the case and corrected it in eleven minutes, the way he had corrected the one on Weir Lane the week before last, and wrote the slip, and put the slip in his own coat, because there is no form on which a hand correction is entered and never has been and there is not going to be.

Eight minutes. Corrected by hand. Charged to nobody. Shown on no receipt. Not on the stair, not in the Assembly, not in the hall's book, not in the pumping ward's book, not in Mrs Tallow's outpatients notebook, nowhere.

He went back to the bridge after that, deliberately, to feel the shape of it again.

He had asked three questions in two days and had paid for two of them, and the one he had not paid for was the one he had wanted most, and it had given him nothing, and the reason it had given him nothing was not a mystery and not a punishment. It was that a joint with nothing on it has nothing to lean against, and there was no load, and there was no water behind it, and nobody was waiting for an answer, and a man who wants to know how a thing will behave has to have something resting on it.

That was the whole lesson, and it had taken him eleven years, one dead man, a sister with a rule, and a form with three columns to arrive at.

---

Ivo Pell came to the bench at half past four on the Sunday afternoon with a clock under his arm and would not come in for a cup of anything.

He put it on the counter and stood with his back to the wall, and he was in a good coat, and he looked at the bench the way he had looked at it once before, from the outside, in the hall's own licensing floor, and he had not liked it then either.

"Where's this from?"

"A man in the licensing office owes a favour and no more than that. Quarter-case regulator clock, ship's pattern, about forty years. It's losing four minutes a day and the man who owns it has told three people it is losing four minutes a day, and I do not believe him, and I have not got a bench." Pell put his hand on the case. "That's not an excuse. That's a fact, and I'd take it as a kindness."

Orrin had it open in four minutes.

"The train's fine. The wheels are fine. The click's not set wrong and it's not worn; there's a piece of shellac about the thickness of a sheet of paper under the locking plate, and I can't tell you how it got there and I would guess somebody put it there in nineteen-something as a stopgap and then forgot." He lifted it out and showed him the pale smear. "That's your four minutes. Take it off, put the plate back, and it will hold for a year and somebody will do it again in twenty years."

"Four minutes a day for forty years is forty days."

"Yes."

"Then somebody's been standing about with a bar under a plate for the best part of six weeks of clock and never once took the cover off." Pell said it without heat. "You'd find that in any shop that had ever had a bench with a lamp."

Orrin looked at him.

"I've been doing this for three days," he said. "In a yard, with a dead pump, and on a timer, and on the bridge. And I've had it very much in my head to say it out loud to somebody who knows more than I do, and you are the only person in this city I could say it to, and I want to say it out loud before I say it to myself, which is the real danger."

Pell did not move.

"A bench man's ear is not an instrument," he said. "That's the whole of it and it is not an insult, and I am not going to soften it because you have been decent about a clock. You can hear a governor that's wrong. You cannot hear a governor that's *about* to be wrong, and there is a difference, and the difference is a fortnight, and the fortnight is where men die. And a man who practises on a dead pump is behind his trade, because a dead pump will tell you anything. And a man who practises on a live one is a public hazard." He picked the clock up. "There is no third thing and there is no way to make one, and I have watched men look for one for thirty years, and the ones who found it were not lucky, they were lucky for about four years."

"Which is what I'm trying to stop."

"You cannot stop it by doing it three times in a weekend in a shed." He was already at the door. "You stop it by having somebody in the room who can tell you that you have asked a question you cannot pay for, and I am not going to be that to you, and I am not available, and I do not have the time, and I have never once been interested in it." He glanced back at the hand on the counter, which was open and would not close cleanly and which Orrin had been flexing under the counter for a quarter of an hour in the hope that it was not visible. "That'll be fourpence for the quarter hour and I don't want it."

"Ivo—"

"Sunday," said Pell, from the doorway. "It's Sunday. You are working Sundays. Don't."

The door shut. He went down the lane with the clock under his arm and did not look back, and Orrin stood in the doorway of his own shop and found that he had wanted very badly to be told he was on the right road, and had been told instead that there was no road, and that this was worth more, and that he was not going to be thanked for the difference and would not have mentioned it.

Then he went in and shut the door and got the grey book, and opened it to a clean page at the top where a man keeps the rules he means to keep, and read what he had written there at his own bench ten days ago, in the cold, before the light, with the words copied out at the top of a clean page because a rule that lives at the back of a book is a note.

*Nothing of mine is used again without its blind margin and its toll named in public, in a room, to somebody who can object.*

He read it twice.

He then took a fresh page and wrote underneath it, in the same hand, because it had turned out on Saturday afternoon in a corridor on the third floor of a hospital that a person who is not told cannot refuse, and that Leda was right and he had been wrong for eighteen years, and that the correction did not stop with her:

*A toll is what happens when you ask something you cannot pay for. Ask one thing, about a named load, and know before you ask what it will cost.*
