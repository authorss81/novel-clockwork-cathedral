# Chapter 62 — The Retired Timers

A dead machine cannot tell you anything about a live one. That is the difficulty in the second of the three practices, it is not a difficulty anybody had warned him about, and it turned out to be the whole of the lesson, and the lesson was not the one he went for.

He went into the store at the Green Span outpatients' annexe on Sunday the seventeenth of the seventh month at about ten in the morning with a lamp that wanted winding, a cloth, a screwdriver, and the four clauses of a piece of paper dated the nineteenth of the sixth month in his coat, and the store is a lean-to at the back of the annexe with a skylight and about eleven feet of shelving and no heating, and it is the safest room in the Lower Weirs.

---

**There are two timers in it and both of them are dead and both of them are Green Span's own.**

The first is a **hospital master timer, 1938, withdrawn**, and it is about the size of a biscuit tin with a glass front and a brass bezel and four holes for the pipes, and its hands are stopped at four minutes to seven and they have been stopped at four minutes to seven since a day in April that is written on a label tied to the case with string.

The second is a **hospital master timer, 1924, withdrawn**, and it is a third smaller, and the top plate is off and lying beside it, and the going train is on a board, and three of the wheels are there and one of them is not, and nobody has thrown the missing one away because nobody has opened that case in nine years.

He cleaned his hands. He put his palm flat on the case of the 1924 one and asked the four words out loud, in plain words, about a named load, which is a rule he set himself in a kitchen on Weir Lane in the second month of the year and has never broken in public.

**What is about to let go.**

And he got the four things, in the order they come.

Heat, on the left-hand bearing of the third wheel. Vibration, in the going train, small, at the top. Resistance, and a place where the resistance is not the resistance it should be. And an edge at the limit of it, which is the fourth thing and which nobody who has not had it can be told what it is like.

He wrote it down, and the price, and the reason, under it, in the shop book, on the Sunday night, in a hand that was not as steady as it usually is.

> *Seventeenth of the seventh. Green Span outpatients' annexe, the store. Hospital master timer, 1924, withdrawn, top plate off, one wheel missing since about 1946.*
> *One question, plain, about a named load. Four things back. **Ninety seconds.** Entered as a reading cost with the reason written under it, which is the seventeenth of the second month's practice and not a toll and I am not going to call it anything else.*
> *And what the ninety seconds bought is this. **Of the four things that came back, three are the machine's own oxide and one is not, and I have got that the wrong way round from what I expected and I am putting it down before I lose it.** The left-hand bearing of the third wheel is a plain brass journal in a bell crank, in a case that has stood in an unheated lean-to since April, and the crank is rusted where the pin goes through, and there is no oil on anything in it, and there has not been oil on anything in it since before I was at this trade.*
> *I could have told you that. I could have told you that without the ninety seconds and I could have told you it in four seconds and I would not have needed a lamp.*
> *Ivo Pell said it in June, in a pump room, and I wrote it down, and I have now been shown it a second time by a piece of dead brass in a shed.*

**The count of keeper's tolls is three and this was not a fourth.** It is a reading cost and it is entered as one, with the reason under it, and the reason is that he asked a question of a thing that could not answer it and paid for the privilege of hearing a true answer about rust.

---

On the Monday morning of the eighteenth he read the wall clock in the store room two floors up, and it was right, and he had to be wrong on purpose for about an hour and a half, and being wrong was the work.

You cannot practise being right. A bench with a good movement on it and a watch in your hand is a man checking a thing he already knows. The point of a dead machine is that **it is free.** He moved the 1938 timer's hands a quarter of an hour out, and set the store's shelf clock four minutes fast against the reference, and beat both of them deliberately, badly, with his thumb on the balance, and read the errors off and wrote them down and got three of the four wrong in the way he expected to be wrong and one of the four wrong in a way he was not.

The one he was not expecting was the 1938. He had it fast by nine seconds a day and it lost the nine and a bit and settled at **one and a half seconds a day slow**, and he had it out again at a quarter past three and the figure had not changed, and there was no way at all to know that until he had put it out twice.

**Which is the second thing a dead machine can do: it can show you the size of your own error, for nothing, as often as you like, and there is no instrument in this city that will do that for money.**

---

**Ivo Pell came on the Tuesday the nineteenth at about half past eleven, which is the day the arrangement is dated, and he did not remark on the date, and Orrin Vale decided later that the silence was the joke and that it was the only joke the man has.**

He came into the lean-to and looked at the two timers and did not touch either, and read the shop book off the shelf with his hands behind his back.

"You are practising being wrong," said Ivo Pell, "and you are practising the price, and the price is the only thing you cannot practise."

"I know."

"You do not know it, you have written it in a book, and a man who has written a thing in a book knows it the way a man knows a hymn. **In about three months you are going to be working on a live machine and you are going to be frightened, and the fright is going to come out as being careful, and being careful on a live machine is a man adjusting somebody else's clock to make his own hands feel better, and you will do it in about nine minutes and you will not tell anybody and I will not be there.**"

"Four things came back off that one," said Orrin Vale, "and three of them were the machine's own oxide and one of them was not, and you told me in a pump room in June that two days in three that is the ratio."

"Yes."

"So it is three in four and not two in three."

"It is whatever it is," said Ivo Pell. "I did not give you a figure. I gave you the shape of it." He shut the book. "Here is the other half and it is the half you have not thought about, because you have spent a fortnight thinking of these two as failures.

"**A dead machine has one question it can answer and it is not a question about the machine's future. It is a question about the machine's past.** That is the whole trade, that is the only trade, and it is the part nobody teaches. Any fool can find out what is going to break. What is worth something is knowing what this thing was believed to be doing on a Tuesday in 1946, by a person who cared, in a book, in ink — and you cannot get that off the brass, and you have to go and get it out of the book, and the book is in the room with you because somebody put the two things in the same room in about 1924 and had an idea."

He looked at the bench, where three other books were stacked that had come out of the same store over eleven years of Fridays.

"**And that is the question a dead machine can answer, and it is worth more than anything you have ever got off a live one, and it took you four days to get to and you started this morning by asking it a question about rust.**"

Then he said the rest of it standing in the doorway with his hat in his hand, and it was not a lesson and it was not a kindness and he did not stay for it.

"Three months, at the outside, before there is anything for you to be frightened of. The winter is when the work is — the hospital branches, the bridges, the tanks, the whole of it, and in about nineteen weeks every one of those machines in this city will be at its worst and every one of them will be somebody's fault. So practise now, on this, and then be somewhere else in November." He put his hat on. "I am not going to be thanked and if I am thanked this stops. Clause three."

"It is clause three," said Orrin Vale.

"Good."

---

**And the books were there, and he had been sitting on top of them since the Sunday.**

Three of them, and the top one is the **ward clock book, Green Span outpatients' annexe, 1924 to date**, and it is not the hospital's ledger, and it is not a return, and it is not in any office in this city, and it is in a store because in about 1951 somebody had nowhere else to put it.

It is a foolscap book with a cloth spine and about two hundred leaves left and it is written in four hands over twenty-seven years. Five columns and a signature, and the columns are these.

**Date. Master compared. Line compared. Master's rate. Ward's worst gap.**

The first four columns are filled in for twenty-six years, every working day, without a gap, by four different women and then by two men, and the rates are in a small hand and they are all there, and they are *beliefs*, and a belief is a fact about the person who held it and not about the machine, and Orrin Vale wrote that on the back page and underlined it because it is the sentence the whole of the book is worth.

Then he came to the last four leaves and the fifth column stopped.

> *12 March 1951. Master compared: —. Line compared: district branch, 41 lb. Master's rate: —. Ward's worst gap: —. Signed: —.*

And there were two more entries after that one, both in a different and much quicker hand, both with dashes in four of the five columns, and the second one is dated the nineteenth of March.

**The dash is not a zero and it is not an omission.** It is the same statement as the empty column in a glass case in a pumping ward on the canal road, in a different building, in a different office's type, for a different reason, and it is a statement that the interval was not recorded, and there is no headnote on this one because nobody in that building ever wrote a headnote about a book in a store, and Orrin Vale stood in an unheated lean-to on the Tuesday morning of the nineteenth of the seventh month and understood that **he was the first person in this city to have seen both of them and the only person who was ever going to.**

The fifth column had been kept since 1924. It was the ward's own worst gap between the free clocks and the line clocks, in somebody's hand, every day, for twenty-six years. It stopped on the twelfth of March 1951, in the week the ward went onto the district branch, and it did not start again, and **the reason it did not start again is that from the twelfth of March 1951 the answer to it has been a number that grows fifty seconds a night and is written in a grey book in a shop on a hill, and a ward cannot be asked that question.**

He copied the two dashed entries into his own book with the dates, and the column headings, and nothing else, and he did not copy the twenty-six years of rates because he had no right to and they are not his and they are still in the book in the store with the timers.

Then he read the two dashed entries for about a quarter of an hour and worked out why a woman had written them at all.

She had written them because the column was there. For twenty-six years somebody had been paid, or at least employed, to fill in five columns, and when the two machines she compared changed underneath her she did not have a new book and she did not have a form, and the column was in front of her, and **she wrote four dashes, which is a woman saying out loud in the only language the page has that the page cannot be filled any more, and then she signed it, and then she went on to the next line.**

Nobody thanked her for that. There is no name on either of the two entries.

---

**The paid work came on the Wednesday morning of the twentieth, and it came because of a docket, and there is no other reason in the whole of that week for anybody to be in that building.**

Ellen Reeve, who has been the hospital's engineer for twenty-two years, caught him on the annexe steps at about nine and asked whether he would look at a clock on the third floor of the ward, and he said he would, and she said three things in the order she said them, which is a thing engineers do and which clockmakers do not and which he appreciated more than he showed.

She wrote it down first. That is the first thing. A quarter of a sheet of the hospital's own engineering pad, in her hand, dated the twentieth, saying what was wanted, what it was not, that it was not to be certified because he could not certify it and the hall had a board about that, and that the invoice was to go to the ward and not to the district works.

And the second thing she said was: "I am not asking you to certify it. I am asking you to make it right and to write down what you did in a way a person can check, and I will sign the certificate because it is my building and my machine."

And the third thing she said was: "**I have got no reference.**"

---

The ward's free master is a **bracket clock of 1949** on the third-floor landing, about eleven inches high, and it is the clock the ward set its own medicine intervals by hand from, and it is the clock Elsie Cobb has been comparing against the line clocks with a watch and a piece of card every night since the twenty-fourth of the fourth month.

He looked at it for four minutes before he touched anything, which is his habit and which Ellen Reeve watched without comment, and then he said, "I have to ask somebody something, and it is not you."

He went down to the ward and asked for the card.

**Elsie Cobb's medicine docket.** It is a folded card, and on the back of it in pencil in a small hand there are about seventy columns of figures running down the sheet, one for nearly every night since the twenty-fourth of the fourth month, and two of them are marked *days* and left blank, and the top line says: *Free v line. Worst gap. Who was waiting.*

"Give me the sheet," he said. "Not to keep. To price."

She gave it to him and she said, "You are the first man anybody has asked that for."

"I am not asking you to give it to me. I am asking to look at it for about four minutes and then I am going to give it back into your apron, and if anybody comes into this ward and asks whether the free clocks cost anything, you are the only person in this building who can say yes, and I would like that to still be true in four years."

---

He read it at the top of the stairs on a windowsill with a pencil and did the sum twice, and then he went back down and quoted the price before he started, out loud, to two people.

"**Three hours at the hall's out-of-shop rate of four shillings and sixpence. Thirteen shillings and sixpence.** One suspension spring, which I will draw and cut myself, about a shilling in brass. One rating nut, two shillings and sixpence. Four bushings, a click spring, cord and solvent, a shilling and threepence. **Eighteen shillings and threepence, and that is a ceiling and not a quote, and it will come in under it.**"

"Why," said Ellen Reeve, "when you have not opened it?"

"Because the docket is the specification," he said. "It is the only document in this building that says what the free clocks cost, and it has been kept since the twenty-fourth of the fourth by a nineteen-year-old who was paid at the ward rate and was not asked for it. It says the worst gap has gone from about four minutes to about fifty minutes in about ten weeks, which is forty seconds a day on the free master and fifty seconds a night on the line, every day, and I have never in my life seen a clock lose forty seconds a day for ten weeks and then be all right again.

"So I am not pricing a fault. I am pricing a fault that has been getting steadily worse for seventy nights, and the two of you have been writing it down, and the reason I want the price fixed before I open the case is that if I open it and it is worse than that, I am going to want four pounds, and I would rather be able to say eighteen shillings and threepence than have you find out afterwards that I decided what it was going to cost when I knew what it was."

Nobody said anything. Elsie Cobb wrote *18s 3d, agreed, 20th* on the bottom of the docket in her own pencil and put it back in her apron, and the docket is in the apron.

---

**What it was, and it took him two hours and fifty minutes, and it is the best piece of work in that week because it is ordinary and it is checkable.**

The movement came out of the bracket onto a folded cloth on the landing. He read the rate on the Quarter's public clock — a watch and a second watch and the case against his chest, eleven minutes, the way he has done since the first month of the year — and it confirmed her figure to about four seconds, and he told her so, and she wrote it down.

Then: a micrometer across the fork, twice, each swing, and the beat was wrong. **The beat error was nearly half a millimetre and it was wrong in one direction only, which means the crutch is out and not the pendulum.** He re-set the crutch, and beat the fork with the crutch setting tool against the banking pins until the beat was even to about a five-thousandth, and the beat error went from a half-millimetre to a five-thousandth, and that is the single biggest improvement in the whole of the job and it took nineteen minutes.

Then: the suspension. He took the spring off and held it up to the window, and there was a **kink in it about a third of the way up**, sharp enough to catch a fingernail, and the whole upper half of it had gone over to a set and the two halves were not the same length any more. That is what does forty seconds a day and does not stop doing it, and no amount of poising a rating nut will touch it, and a man who has never taken a suspension spring off will poise it for a week and then wonder why the pendulum has gone out again in September.

He drew a new one on a piece of the docket's cardboard — **not on the docket**, on a separate scrap, and put the scrap in the book, because the drawing is the only record of the part and the part will not be made again — cut it in brass strip, pierced the chops, fitted it, and hung the movement on the bracket for an hour before he touched the rate at all.

Then: pivots cleaned, one bushing replaced, the countwheel and click cleaned, the click spring new, and the **poise** moved down the rod by about a quarter of an inch, which at that pendulum is worth most of a second a day, and the rating nut then took the rest.

**Total: two hours and fifty minutes, at four shillings and sixpence the hour being twelve shillings and ninepence, with the same four shillings and ninepence of material, and the invoice is seventeen shillings and sixpence against a ceiling of eighteen and threepence. Orrin Vale has never in his life been glad of a ceiling and was glad of this one.**

The rate afterwards, read on the Wednesday evening against the Quarter's public clock, a watch and a second watch and the case against his chest, was **within one second a day**, and Ellen Reeve read it again on the Monday with her own watch and got the same figure and wrote both of them on the certificate, and the invoice says so in the only words the hall has for it, which are *beat set, spring renewed, rate adjusted*, and Ellen Reeve's certificate underneath it, in her hand, with the date, and **he does not sign it and could not.**

> *Twentieth of the seventh. Green Span, third floor, the ward's free master, 1949.*
> *Two hours fifty minutes, being eighteen shillings and threepence, the figure agreed before the case was opened and entered on the back of a nineteen-year-old's medicine docket in her own pencil.*
> *Forty seconds a day for seventy nights: a suspension spring kinked about a third of the way up and set over in the upper half, and a crutch half a millimetre out on beat. Neither of those is anybody's fault. Both of those are what a clock does in a building for eighteen years with a window open onto a landing in the winter.*
> *And the specification was her card, and the card was the only document in that building that said what the free clocks cost, and I have written down that I asked for it and gave it back, because the day somebody else asks her for it is the day it stops being hers.*

---

And on the Wednesday evening of the twentieth, which is the last hour of the day, he was on the third floor with a watch and a piece of the docket's cardboard, and Leda Vale came and stood beside him and asked him the question he had already decided he was going to have to say no to.

"Ward's gap, this month," she said. "You have given me back forty seconds a day of growth. I want to know what it is at the end of the month so I can put it on the card with the date on it and read it out in this room, because I have told this room a number four times over two years and I want to give them one with a date on it that is going to be true."

He looked at the branch figure in his own notebook, and at the date it was last stepped, and at the fact that it is stepped every night and that nobody in this city has said why.

"I cannot tell you," he said. "And I am not going to meander about it, and I want the reason on the card as well as the number, because the reason is the useful half.

"**There are two gaps on that card and you have only ever been asking me about one of them.** The one I have given you back is your free master against your own line, and that one is a clock, and the clock is mended, and it is inside a second a day this evening and it will be inside a second a day on the thirty-first of this month and every month after that, and that is a number I will put a date on for you and it will be true.

"**The other one is the gap between your free clocks and the line clocks, and I have not given you that one back and I am not able to, and it is still growing, and I cannot tell you what it will be on the thirty-first.** The line is the district branch, and the branch is fifty seconds further out every night than it was the night before, and there is nothing anybody can do to a number that is added to while you are asleep, and it is not a fault in a machine and it is not going to be put back in your time or mine, and if somebody re-set your ward's line clocks to the district reference this month they would all be that much wrong against the hospital's own rate, and I have not touched them and I am not going to.

"So the number at the end of the month is forty seconds a day taken away from one of the two, and the other one is still going, and **I am not going to hand you a figure with a date on it that I would have to come back here and take off the card in a fortnight.**"

Leda Vale wrote it down. It went on the card with the date on it and the four names on it, and the fifth name on that card, in a different hand, is E. Cobb, and the column she started that morning is a new one, and the top of it says: *Free master, growth stopped 20th, inside 1 s a day. Line gap: still growing, 50 s a night, reason not in this ward.*

The count of a clockmaker's own unrecorded hand corrections in this city remains **two**, and this was a customer's machine under a written engagement at an agreed price, and a work of maintenance is not a hand correction, and nothing on the third floor that week was a third. **A price agreed before the case is opened is not a toll and is not a reading cost and is not in any of the four columns. And it is the only instrument a clockmaker has that has never once cost anybody anything except him.**
