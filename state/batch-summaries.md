# Batch Summaries and Rolling Volume Summary

One compact section per completed batch, newest last. The rolling volume summary at the end is what a writer should read first when picking a volume up mid-run. Older chapter detail lives in `state/chapter-summaries.md`; the fourteen-volume index lives in `state/continuity.md`.

---

## Volume 01, Batch 0001 — Chapters 1–10 (The Ninth Tick, part one)

**What happened.** Orrin Vale certified the Cathedral's crown-case public clock on Monday the 15th, heard a ninth pulse arrive from below in a clock that was correct twice on his gauge, and told nobody. The next day Gerren Hask talked him out of a defect note, showed him the hall's labour book with a line in a copying clerk's hand reading *Weir Two, night pumping, non-certified standby, 1 crew, 3 nights*, and handed him an eighteen-year-old Long Nine governor that was stopped mid-travel on purpose with a bar, to be reduced to unrecorded scrap; Orrin refused to scrap a regulator with a cut pin and took it home. On the 17th he met Perry Vance alive at Weir Two, told him the second stage would run past midnight, watched him shut the hand valve in daylight and chalk *hand shut*, and heard the ninth beat a second and last time. He then filed one question as a private citizen, paying three district minutes from the Clockwork Quarter's reserve — the district with the most reserve and the least standing to object — and watched the Loom hold Ferrywright Bridge through the night tide. Vance went down at about ten to shut a valve the model said was shut, and the pump that should have stopped did not stop. Two more were injured; the Fenn boy is fourteen. The Directorate's notice pinned to the pump house door called the day a success and was not lying.

**What it cost.** Three minutes charged to a district that was neither affected nor consenting; Alma Festinger's witness interval on a Wednesday rota; Orrin's first Aftershock, unrecorded; and a lost minute of his own attention with clean edges that he can never account for. The Weirs paid the rest in hours nobody was paid for, as they had for twenty years.

**Where the batch ends.** The omission is not a machine failure but a **guild form number** — GH-9, standing withdrawal, maintenance-deferred class — with a price schedule (two shillings the head for a year) and a batch of forty-one entries transferred eighteen years ago to the Archive of Unlisted, countersigned for the affected district by a then-twenty-six-year-old deputy pumping warden, **Sel Kade**. A routing copy went to a Green Span public-health rota and the attendance line reads *FESTINGER, A.*, and nobody has ever connected it. The first repair done in company — a bearing block, a shim and a named sacrificial plate on the hospital trestle's conduit bracket — was made on the night of Saturday the 20th, held the clocks, was paid for out of a ward's forty minutes of slack and nine men's **Sunday** morning, and left Orrin with two days of lost fine motor control in his right hand and a permanent ring above a given pitch in his right ear. The first institutional gain is an objection on the toll receipt, accepted for filing. The batch's defeat is the committee: the demand for a durable toll record and for a name and a duration in the body-toll columns was referred to committee until the spring, by Councilor Imani Rook, on arithmetic rather than sentiment.

**State at the end of the batch.** Orrin: Stage 2 Aftershock, deliberately used once and named aloud; two keeper's tolls taken, neither filed; no licence offence committed; class-three renewal about eight weeks out and slack; private ledger open at the front with three entries that matter. Mara Venn: an objection on file, a measured blind margin, a professional fear for Orrin's hearing, no personal disclosure. Teo Arden: correct, unsoftened suspicion, calls it an audit. Leda Vale: arguing about method, not character, and on the page in the climax. Hask: his crime is legible to the reader and invisible to every character.

**One deliberate repetition, do not fix it.** Orrin's rule — *nothing of mine is used again without its blind margin and its toll named in public, in a room, to somebody who can object* — appears as a standalone line at the end of the Chapter 9 ledger entry and again in Chapter 10, where he turns back to it, reads it twice, and copies it out at the top of a clean page because a rule that lives at the back of a book is a note and not a rule. A duplicate-paragraph audit will flag it. It is motivated and it is the beat the batch's rule requirement turns on.

**Carried into batch 0002:** the class-three renewal's paperwork, not the renewal; the guild labour ledger and Della Marsh's drawer; the Weirs' counter-record and Bevin Sarr's board; Imani Rook's own filing due about Chapter 13; the Green Span hospital pump Orrin reads correctly and cannot certify; the hospital trestle; Sel Kade's reserve as a public fact; the two uncharged minutes; and the fact that the Archive of Unlisted stays shut until Volume 02.

## Volume 01, Batch 0002 — Chapters 11–20 (The Ninth Tick, part two)

**What happened.** The batch is a paper war in which the paper wins and the fact loses. Tobias Rill's honest quarterly inspection produces a complete, correct finding that says there is nothing to find, because the Office can only certify that a line agrees with its own record and a district's own chalk board is not a document any office is obliged to receive — and the fifth name, the one that proves the guild's book is wrong, is a boy with no certificate, who therefore cannot be entered on a form that can only enter certified men. Hask asks for the grey ledger and does not threaten anybody: the price is that from the day the notice lands, the only record of what Orrin has found in any office in the city is one the hall writes, on a form with three columns and no fourth for *I am not sure*, at a renewal about eight weeks away, with an optional interview on Tuesday the 28th of the second month that Orrin cannot decline without giving a reason. Imani Rook files her own demand a week later, on the pumping district's own returns — 1,412 hours worked at a civic water station against a certified return of nil — after she was shown arithmetic and not persuaded of anything, pays for the record out of her own ward's clerical allocation because *who pays for the record* is the sentence that killed the demand on the 24th, and still keeps the name-and-duration column out, because she does not know the size of the population the column would describe. Della Marsh makes a form number come out of a shut door for four shillings in coin with no receipt, and the copybook behind it shows nine districts on a page with no column for a man. Leda makes him say his rule aloud and prices it, posts the four districts on the back of a ward roster where families read, and names the lamp: she was nine, in a shelter, and the bells stopped, and she does not know what their father did. The Continuity Office file then shows that the same form, the same price schedule and the same habit put a £2 14s 6d bridge bracket on a shelf for two years and carried it forward twice, lawfully, in a lighter hand — so the omission is a maintenance policy as well as a moral instrument, and the volume's argument moves from blame to arithmetic. Orrin practises Aftershock on a dead pump, a hospital timer and a bridge joint, learns that a narrow question is payable and that stopping is not, and is right and contemptuous at him once. Eleven copies of the labour book go into nine buildings and cost thirty-one households a rent rise, because a district that can prove its own labour is a district that can be invoiced for it. And on a hospital landing he reads a failing pump correctly, out loud, in a room, having learned in Chapter 9 that a private correct answer is a rumour with his name on it — and the room objects, correctly, and nothing happens.

**What it cost.** Ninety seconds in a yard and eleven minutes in a corridor, both entered as reading costs, thirty-eight minutes on a landing entered as a toll, and permanently the ability to judge a movement by ear. Four shillings to a woman who has never been thanked. Nineteen years of a copyist's work. A rent rise on thirty-one households, then restored. A ward that lost four minutes and had a girl of nineteen do the ward clock's job by hand. A copper wedge out of a tram-line bond, hammered into a gland on a Sunday morning by a twenty-year-old, painted, and signed underneath the eighth course, and it was the correct thing to do and it is going to be a matter of record in two years. The Archive of Unlisted did not move an inch. The three minutes is still the only number the city has published.

**Where the batch ends.** Right, unprovable, useless. Orrin certifies a pump and writes the whole of his reading on the **reverse** of the certificate, which nobody reads, and knows it, and writes it anyway because the deal is the deal. He is told by his sister that his rule worked and that working is the problem. He takes two days over a third rule and finds that it is worse than the first because it can stop him: *no result of mine goes out of this room without a person named who can refuse it.* He discovers the reason in his own handwriting, on a page about Sel Kade's eighteen-year-old signature that he had in his hands in a room and did not take round to her, and discovers that his grey ledger stopped being a book about machines somewhere around the eighteenth of last month. Teo Arden walks into the shop without knocking with a page of the copybook and a boardinghouse on the Kestrel side of Northglass where nine women have worked for years that a hall's form says were never there, and asks whether there is a way of it that isn't his.

**State at the end of the batch.** Orrin: Stage 2 Aftershock, practised with a discipline and a boundary, three keeper's tolls taken and unfiled, the third and largest because he wanted to act and did not; a worse right ear and a permanently narrower trade; no licence offence committed and nothing certified that he had no standing to certify; a W-4 due on Sunday the 19th of the third month, an interview on Tuesday the 28th of the second month, and a third rule of his own, three days old, that has not yet had to stop him. Mara Venn: a copybook, a price schedule, a petition, an objection, a public method she is ashamed of, and a day's correspondence in a file that ends *no further correspondence on this matter*. Teo Arden: sent the copies, was right to, has not apologised, and has begun asking questions with other people's names in them. Leda Vale: from method to consequence, and the one who says in a corridor that his rule is what cost the ward four minutes. Imani Rook: files on arithmetic, pays out of her own ward, wins a smaller thing, and keeps the hard clause out for a reason she has already given.

**One deliberate structural rhyme, do not fix it.** The sentence that kills the demand in Chapter 10 is *who pays for the record.* In Chapter 13 Rook does not beat that sentence, she **pays** it, out of her own ward's clerical allocation, at four pounds ten, in an estimates line headed *district returns, exceptional*. Do not let a later batch treat that as a sentimental reversal: she is not softened, she has out-argued herself out of the clause she actually wanted, and the money is the reason the demand exists at all.

**Carried into batch 0003:** the Assembly's returning date and the Finance committee's verdict on four pounds ten; Rill's file and who else has been reading it; the trestle's deferral as a budget line rather than a scandal; Sel Kade's unreviewed March diversion and the tenant review owed by about Chapter 46; Orrin's W-4 and Hask's Tuesday the 28th of the second month, **both inside Volume 01 and both in batch 0003, where the licence is renewed on Sunday the 19th of the third month and is not suspended**; the W-4 as the mechanism by which the grey ledger's contents would become the hall's; the two uncharged minutes and two uncharged hand corrections; nine districts in a copybook and nobody named; Hester Lomas out of work; Hanna Stroud and nine blocks; Teo Arden's question about a Northglass boardinghouse and the first piece of work they will do together; and the fact that nothing has yet been organised.

## Volume 01, Batch 0003 — Chapters 21–30 (The Ninth Tick, part three)

**What happened.** The consequences of a counter-record become a method, and the method turns out to cost the people who do the copying rather than the people who own the thing being copied. Bevin Sarr will not let the board out of the pump house and gives the reason that is not sentimental; Wenna Pruitt's kitchen turns the thing into four questions — who holds it, who copies it, who may be asked, who may not — and Bessie Crane prices the copying out loud before anyone else can. The compromise is a ledger that goes round on Fridays with no owner, and Wenna says it is going to fail while it is happening. Then the structural problem arrives as a domestic argument: a landlord, an employer and a tenant council want three incompatible things out of the same eleven-year book and walk out within an hour of each other without a quarrel, and **Sel Kade** is in the room and is not on anybody's side, because a pump warden's business is the water. A request is put to the guild for its labour ledger and comes back in **four hours**, on a printed card with a form number, because the hall's defence is a *definition* and a definition is already written. Hask's optional interview on **Tuesday the 28th of the second month** is warm, generous, and completely harmless: it costs **nineteen days** of margin, tells him the counter is open on Sundays, gives him the second sheet of the W-4 nobody asks for, and — once, pleasantly — asks about the thimble in the regulator's flank and then tells him that the escapement on his own bench wants oiling, so that a man who cannot judge a sound learns what he has been told. **On Wednesday the 8th of the third month, on an hour and forty minutes of ward-board notice, a second and larger public Reading is run on the Weirs' own water schedule.** It is lawful, it is technically correct, the water it saves is worth more than the bridge was, the committee that approved it had the returns and did the arithmetic, and the blind margin in the footer is *unregistered district labour* — a category now, not a crew, and wider because the district learned to say *the pumping district's own returns* in a chamber. Forty-one Weirs are in the room and not on the rail, because a standing is a written claim and the district has no standing. Twenty-six district minutes, the Clockwork Quarter, the same district as in the first month. Mara Venn objects on the correct ground, the second Compact safeguard, and loses, and says her own method out loud while losing it. The bill for the eighth is then compiled by people going and asking, in seven currencies with no column, and Rill puts four true lines in a public file. The licence is **renewed** on Sunday the 19th, on the expiry date itself, in a two-hour window he learns about from a notice on a wall, after spending a day selecting forty lines out of two thousand pages — and one of those lines hands eleven years of the Weirs' night work to the hall, in the guild's own form, of his own motion, with nobody in the room able to refuse it. A fair copy of the Weir Two line comes back as an enclosure, signed by the covering note **E.L.**, made by the hall's only copyist out of its own back room as outwork, because she met the words *Weir Two floodgate and second-stage pumping station* and did not know what a station was. It is perfect, signed, stamped and true, and it has taken the evidence out of the labour book, because a fair copy cannot show a hand and cannot carry the Sunday in the ink above. **The hand is Hester Lomas's, who is the woman the district put in the Advertiser and who lost nineteen years for it, and she is not thanked and she is not a martyr and she asks for a lease and a wage a month.** The Weirs decide, in their own rooms, **not to take a position on the water schedule**, and the Advertiser stops, and two hundred women find out they were not asked, and Orrin discovers that the only place he has ever been useful is a footnote on page nine of a printer's return.

**What it cost.** A hundred and forty seconds, entered as a reading cost with the reason written under it, and no fourth keeper's toll. Twenty-six district minutes, one lost afternoon belonging to a named woman with no duration recorded, one hospital ward's eleven minutes, one night porter's night, one ferry timetable that cannot be reprinted, one school, one crew's return now wrong by twenty minutes, and the whole of the Weirs' night water. Eight shillings a year: the guild's price for the Weir Two crew's whole year of night work, against a hall page of hourly rates — a factor of about two hundred, which is too lopsided to be a mistake and is not a mistake. £1 7s 6d and six hours for forty minutes of clock work, which is the price of doing it in the required form, and the form is not available to a district. The Advertiser, eleven shillings, and a page about the front and nothing else.

**Where the batch ends.** A licensed maintainer with a wet stamp, a grade of a hearing that is not coming back, a district that has spoken for itself and will not be spoken for, a fair copy in a hall that has corrected the evidence, and clocks out on a street four miles from either the Quarter or the Weirs, and out on Weir Lane, and on the corner, and nobody has come. **The volume's failure is now repetition rather than ignorance**, and the author's next problem is not to be right twice.

**Batch shape, as written.** Ch 21 a method and a bill; Ch 22 a district is a place, delivered as a row; Ch 23 four hours and a definition; Ch 24 forty minutes and a wet cup; Ch 25 the water schedule; Ch 26 the bill, itemised; Ch 27 the licence, renewed; Ch 28 the fair copy and the hand; Ch 29 four days of work and a number somebody else counted; Ch 30 what a district decides, and a footnote.

**One deliberate structural rhyme, do not fix it.** The sentence that killed the demand in Chapter 10 is *who pays for the record*, and Chapter 13 pays it with £4 10s out of a Green Span ward's clerical allocation. In Chapter 25 **the £4 10s is the arithmetic that approves the second Reading.** The district's own printed returns are the only public document in Morrowfen saying a district works hours at a civic machine, the water users' committee read them, and the better the district got at being counted the easier it became to take the water off them. A duplicate-paragraph audit will not flag it, and a reader who notices it will not be able to put it down. Do not let a later batch treat the £4 10s as a sentimental reversal.

**Six card-versus-prose divergences, recorded here rather than fixed in the cards.** The Chapter 27 card's *four columns* is on the page as **four things printed at the head of the form and a ruled table of three columns beneath them**, with one of the three — *recommendation* — left empty, which is the shape the chapter actually needs. The Chapter 23 card's moved *lodgement* deadline, and its figure of four days, are on the page as **notice five on the licensing floor**, which does not move the expiry and does not move the fee and takes a renewal that had four weeks and a counter open nine to four and makes it **two hours on one Sunday morning**. Both keep Hask innocent and both preserve the 19th, and the prose governs. The Chapter 24 card's aside that *nobody in this batch has mentioned the water schedule* is nearly true: **Sel Kade says the word water about thirty times in Chapter 22**, which is the thesis of Chapter 25 arriving two chapters early, though no character files a question, expects a Reading, or knows one is scheduled, and the Water Users' Committee has never been heard of before Chapter 25. The Chapter 25 card's **Imani Rook appearing as the chair who approved the Reading** is contradicted by the same card's own chandler and by the prose: **she is in the third bench, in a plain dark coat, and the vote is the committee's.** Leda's Chapter 26 beat is **in Weir Lane and in the rain, not in a hospital corridor**, which is a deliberate relocation and is recorded rather than corrected. And **Hask's *twenty-second of the third month* invitation at the end of Chapter 24 is the twenty-eighth of next year** — his own standing day, a year on — so that the chapter has one date in it and not two.

**The second verification pass, in one line: eighteen repairs, all date or number arithmetic or a contradiction with an earlier chapter, and the two that mattered were that Chapter 28 denied its own evidence by inventing a second hall copyist when E.L. is Hester Lomas, and that Chapter 30 put the tenant council in a building Chapters 22 and 23 both say it does not use.** Full disposition in `state/open-threads.md`.

**The review pass of batch 0003, in one line: thirty-one repairs, eight of them blocking, and the three that mattered were that Chapter 25's midpoint contained a sentence about *the volume's argument*, that Chapter 27's lodgement day could not happen because the bench began on the expiry day at a quarter past two and the counter shuts at twelve, and that Chapter 25's notice contradicted the reading procedure Chapter 4 had already fixed.** Full disposition in `state/open-threads.md`.

**Carried into batch 0004:** clock slippage across the Quarter and the Weirs, first sighted on the Thursday of the 30th and already three days old on the first of the fourth month, which is a Saturday and is the day Chapter 31 opens; the Water Users' Committee, its schedule, and the fact that the schedule runs to the thirtieth and then has to be re-voted; Sel Kade's unreviewed diversion of **March**, which she has now put on the table in her own words and refused to be questioned about in a room; the Loose Minutes' disclosure argument, unresolved and expressly not to be resolved in batch 0003; the £4 10s and the six printed returns, which are in a binder on Cathedral Rise and are now load-bearing; a fair copy of the Weir Two line and a fair copy of the Kestrel-side boardinghouse line, both in a hall, both correct; the hall's four-line card, which goes out four times a year; the second-Wednesday rota and the name in the third column of a health file with no duration beside it; the 388 hours and the rates bill they will bring; Rill's file and his four true lines; and the fact that nobody in this city has ever asked who asked the hall for its labour ledger.

## Volume 01, Batch 0004 — Chapters 31–40 (The Ninth Tick, part four)

**What happened.** The batch opens on Saturday the first of the fourth month with a man who has already said his street is out, and the finding is that a carriage clock cannot be read to better than two minutes because its minute hand is bent — and that with the hand trued, four machines on one civic hourline read **one minute forty, five minutes, eight minutes twenty and eleven minutes forty** against the district reference, and a signalman two districts away has been writing **fifty seconds a night** in a bound day book for sixteen days and has never been asked to see it. The shape is a line: the line out of the Weir Two pumping station divides by eight, takes its beat off the odd teeth, **was surveyed for two cycles a day, has been given four since the fifteenth of the third month, and is the same line the Chapter 25 Reading drained.** He certifies four condition records, all true, all saying *within tolerance*, which between them say the city is on time; the district works' clerk refuses it on the correct ground, because a line is not a machine and there is no column for it. On Sunday evening the fourth draw does not come, and the news is that the error is now fixed and permanent and there is no fault in it to correct. The Water Users' Committee's schedule lapses at midnight; the district reverts to the standing rotation; the committee has neither power to continue a schedule nor power to suspend the rotation; and the ward-board notice carries, for the first time in eleven years, a sentence saying the committee **is not able to consult the district, the district having no standing under the water act.** Wenna Pruitt spends the council's last **£4 10s** on laying the ward's returns before the committee as an appendix, and the Advertiser page on the ninth is cancelled to pay for it. A **diversion note** from the district works accountancy then documents Sel Kade's March diversion for the first time: nine thousand gallons a day to the Carrow Street main, three hundred and twenty-four thousand gallons in thirty-six days, a review clause that is a discretion with no end to it, and a reserve alarm inhibited since March with no report made. She defends it as a defence of jobs and refuses to have the beneficiary's name struck out, because a rumour can be beaten by a document and a document with a review clause in it is a thing that can be ended. The hall files a question against Orrin which the honest answer cannot lawfully be given to, because the answer requires a certified district figure. The Weir Two pump records are sealed on a real security ground by a man who states both halves of his choice and is right about both. And the whole business is reclassified as **deferred maintenance**, in a published schedule with a triennium, a carry-forward and a **£4 202** budget line, which is the same instrument that deferred the hospital trestle's bracket twice — and Mara Venn's attack on it, which is the right attack, produces an inspection interval and **£411 more money and no more hours in anybody's ledger.**

**What it cost.** Wenna's last money and the Advertiser page. **A kept promise: an hour a night off the canal front's own draw for four days, and a hundred and forty gallons of standing water back at the Carrow Street end by the Saturday, paid for by a woman who was thanked by nobody.** A block of twenty-two households' standpipe and a woman of sixty-six's four hand-wound clocks. Four days of uncharged labour at a laundry and a fortnight of a printing shop's night. A district's arithmetic becoming a list of addresses. Hanna Stroud's insurance premium, because the returns she could produce in March are now *unavailable*. Sel Kade's diversion in a document, at her own insistence, with the ward's rates wrong behind it. And Orrin: a question in his file he cannot see, an answer he cannot give, eleven words in a hall file that say no person in the district was asked, and his own third rule knowingly broken on a Saturday afternoon in a bus on the Rise.

**The batch's arithmetic, and it is the book's arithmetic.** 388 hours of Weir Two night pumping at the guild's out-of-shop tariff of four shillings and sixpence the hour is **eighty-seven pounds and six shillings for four months.** The published maintenance programme for the whole pumping district over three years is **£4 202 10s 6d.** The programme is about **forty-eight times the price of the crew**, and nobody chose it: a price schedule is a page of hourly rates and it knows exactly what a night's work costs, and a works programme is a page of item rates and it knows exactly what a governor costs, and neither of the three numbers is a man. **Nothing in this business has ever been a scandal. Everything in it is a budget.**

**Where the batch ends.** Orrin has been filed against by his own hall and cannot see the question. The Weir Two records are sealed and the district's own arithmetic is now a copy of a file nobody may read, so a number in the Assembly's minutes can be called a fabrication. The hall's answer to his answer is four lines that will go back to him as a form number, because a definition that is true cannot be beaten by producing more evidence. He has offered the district his rule, been told the district has outgrown it, and **taken the eighteen-year-old countersignature to the woman whose name is on it after eighty-six days**, and been told what restoring the crew would cost: **a rate rise on the same people the crew stands on.** He goes to a door on the Rise on a Saturday morning and is not admitted, by a printing decision rather than a person, and **nobody offers him anything.** He decides to put **eighty-two shillings of his own money** into the same form series that took the crew out, alone, on a Monday, telling nobody, and writes down that he has broken his own rule. And at twenty past one on a Saturday afternoon he can see, from a window, two clocks four hundred yards apart disagreeing without a gauge, without a watch and without a hearing — and so can every other window on that hill by Monday. **The shape is out of the window and nobody is going to be angry about it in the morning.**

**State at the end of the batch.** Orrin: Stage 2 Aftershock, used once, the answer true and unusable, a hundred and twenty seconds entered as a reading cost; three keeper's tolls unchanged; a question on his holder card, a guild answer with a form number, and a decision on a Monday that nobody knows about; the count of hand corrections still **two**; no licence offence, nothing suspended, nothing coming. Mara Venn: twenty-two four-line cards in a drawer, a *received and noted* she is thanked for by telephone for the first time in nine years, a form she fought at the ground floor of the whole city with a section she refused to read aloud, and a note she did not send about a sealed file. Leda Vale: the hospital is a constituency, her method is the batch's other instrument, and a card with a number on it goes up in the nurses' room every Monday. Sel Kade: documented, defended, not recruited, not softened, restoration promised in her own words in a room that cannot order her, and the tenant review still owed by about Chapter 46. Teo Arden: a name in a store's issue book, a light on a stair, a wet landing, and a disclosure argument that is still open. Wenna Pruitt: no money and a method with no owner, and a book of initials with no key. Rill and Hask and Rook: each appeared once or twice, in office, right, and not the villain.

**Carried into batch 0005:** the storm, the trestle, the named outage, and the seven items of the volume resolution; **the district works' review of the diversion, on the thirtieth of the fourth month, the day after Chapter 40, and the discretion that follows it**; **Chapter 41 must be a repair, training or recovery chapter before the settlement, in the same ten-chapter batch, and no chapter of batch 0004 may take its place**; the published inspection interval on eleven miles of main, which is somebody else's obligation and the only thing Mara Venn won; the guild question and the answer with a form number, due **the fifth of the fifth month**; the sealed Weir Two file and CS/114 and a note that does not reach a pumping district; the reinstatement Orrin is going to buy on Monday, and nobody knows, and the ward's rates are the cost; the Weir One governor as line three of a published schedule at £96; the trestle bracket as a survey in year three at a two-year-old estimate; Sel Kade's tenant review; Imani Rook's own toll-ledger demand still to be moved by her; the four clocks a woman of sixty-six winds every Monday and the eleven minutes forty seconds every window on the hill can now see; and the ninth tick, still unexplained, still unheard since the seventeenth of the first month.

## Volume 01, Batch 0005 — Chapters 41–50 (The Ninth Tick, the end of the volume)

**What happened.** A licensed maintainer puts eighty-two shillings of his own money into form GH-9 at a counter on Monday morning in four minutes, with a wet stamp and a woman who says *don't* before he can say thank you, and nobody in four districts is told. Then he spends four days on four clocks on the fourth floor of a block in the Weirs, and the invoice comes to exactly five pounds sixteen shillings and sixpence because he wrote the price on the back of an order pad before he took his coat off, and **the four clocks are put on their own 1911 rate and not the district's, because a woman of sixty-six was asked what she wanted and a landlord with nine blocks wanted something else and they are both right**, and **his own third rule is satisfied for the first time in fifty chapters by a piece of paper with a price and a name on it, and the person it is for uses it and tells him to take her name off a certificate and gives him the reason and he writes the reason in anyway.** The hall's answer to his answer comes back in four hours in four lines that are a definition, and quotes his own prediction back at him as proved correct while recording that nobody asked, nobody could have asked, and he had no standing to have it asked. He then measures a canal crossing in daylight off a setting-out line cut in a coping stone in 1911 and gets **fifteen thirty-seconds of an inch against twelve thirty-seconds two years and three months ago, a twenty-fourth of an inch a year, every figure of it checkable in nine minutes by anybody with a rule and a level, and the division written out on the coping so that it can be checked with a pencil** — and then finds, in a corridor in a hospital, that the ward's clock pressure is bought by pumping harder out of **one tank** whose low-level alarm has been inhibited since the second of the third month with no report made, in a district whose canal front is drawing four times a night off the same main. A rate notice goes up with a number on it and no name. An insurer moves nine blocks from a schedule to a blanket scale and the letter does not say who to write to. **Then a storm comes up the canal and a cast bracket of a pattern not made in thirty years lets go at eleven o'clock at night with a crack and a hiss, and a man gets within about eleven minutes of the Chapter 9 act — a clamp, in the dark, where nobody can object — and stops, and telephones a pump house at ten past three and asks for a room, and in that room at four in the morning a nurse says her ward's forty minutes out loud before anybody spends it, and a works clerk says nobody is touching a bridge at night without an order, and a woman writes six men's lost hour in a night book with their names on it in front of nine people.** The district works ends a diversion on its own tables and shuts a works of ninety-one in the same week.
