# Chapter 105 — Nine Feet by Eleven

On Thursday the twenty-sixth of the first month Orrin went to the printing office on Bellweather Road because its wall regulator was losing and taking the shop's day with it, and the job was ordinary and paid and took him four minutes' walk past the house he needed to see without making a journey of it.

The regulator was a four-foot affair over the composing room, and it had been losing about four minutes a day for a year in a slow even way that nobody could name. The bracket clock he had mended on the thirteenth was on the shelf by the door where a printer's boy had put it, and it was right, and the boy said so, and did not say it in a way that expected anything.

"That's the little one," he said. "Mr Coss says the big one's the one everybody hears."

The big one was the one everybody heard, and everybody heard it wrong. Orrin took the case off, found a worn spindle and a score in the plate, cleaned both, and put a new bush in that cost him sixpence, and it took an hour and a half, and Mr Coss came in twice during it and looked at the movement over Orrin's shoulder without touching anything, which was the correct way for a printer to look at a clock.

"It's the case of a thing that's been leaned on," Coss said.

"Yes."

"Nineteen thirty-one that case."

"Then it's been leaned on since thirty-one and it will go on being leaned on," Orrin said, "but not with that spindle in it."

He beat the regulator to the Quarter's public clock on the wall outside, which was the only clock in that street anybody trusted, and wrote the rate on a card, and charged eleven shillings, and was paid in coin without argument, which was also correct for a shop that had never once written a line about the brass.

"Mr Vale," Coss said, at the door. "My boy said you sent it back in paper."

"I did."

"Was that the right thing to do?"

"It was the only thing I could do with it," Orrin said. "It isn't mine."

"Aye," said Coss, and shut the door, and did not ask another question, and Orrin walked on up the road with his tool roll over his shoulder and the twenty-sixth of the first month going grey over Bellweather.

No. 9 was a boardinghouse of fourteen households, four floors, the stairs worn in the middle where thirty years of boots had taken the same line down and up. There was a smell of boiled cabbage and paraffin and a woman at the bottom of the stairs with a cloth who looked at the roll on his shoulder and said nothing, which is the correct thing for the ground floor of a boardinghouse to do with a working man.

The fourth floor was nine feet by eleven, a gas ring, a table, a list on a nail, a woman of about thirty-four sitting at the table with a brass token in front of her and a child asleep in the next room with the door open so she could hear it breathe.

Her name was Miriam Coss, no relation to the printer, a folder in a box factory two days' walk away, two rooms, a husband on nights. She had paid four shillings and sixpence for a hundred minutes of brass that was not there. Hale's paper was folded under the token with the price on it in his hand, and she had kept the paper, which told Orrin more about her than the token did.

Two Coss in one morning, and he had a full shop's worth of reason to be careful about the second one, and he wrote her name out on the back of his hand in pencil before he put the pencil down, the way a man writes a thing down when he knows he will need it in four days.

"Mr Vale?" she said. "The landlady said you'd look at the clock and not make a speech."

"I'll look at the clock," Orrin said.

The clock was a small bracket on the mantel, eleven inches high, losing steadily. He took it down, opened it, found the pendulum fouled by a picture frame moved an inch to the left, moved the frame, set the beat, hung it true. Fourteen minutes' work. He wrote no invoice yet because the token was on the table and the token was the reason he had been asked up without being asked.

"Will it work?" she said, nodding at the brass.

He sat down at the table because she had asked him to sit and because standing over a woman in a nine-by-eleven kitchen to answer a question about money would have been a second wrong.

"A token can be spent in the clock it was cut from," he said. "Yours is Q. This clock is not the Quarter's. Put into this clock, a hundred minutes comes out of the Quarter's margin and goes into the network, and the network gives it to the next clock behind. The loss lands where it has landed for nineteen years, which is nowhere with a name on it."

"Is it lawful?"

"It is lawful to hold, cut, sell and buy. What it is for is not in any form. The line between the two is a hole in a form about a hundred and forty years old, and there is no instrument anywhere that can say which side a given minute is on."

She turned the token over. It was worn bright on one edge, which meant she had carried it in a pocket for nine days without spending it and without letting anybody else try to. "Then what is the law for?"

"The rim and the hole and the letter and the number," Orrin said. "It is a very good law about the object and it says nothing whatever about the use, and it has said nothing since about a hundred and forty years ago, and everybody in this city has got extremely good at living in that gap."

She nodded and turned the token over again. "I bought back four months. A hundred and twenty nights at fifty seconds. My husband's nights. The toll took them and nobody wrote them down. Is that true?"

"The toll took something," Orrin said, carefully. "Whether it took his is a different question and I want to answer the one you asked before I answer the one I can."

"Ask me the other one."

"The other one is the works bell." He put his hand flat on the table, flatly, the way a man does when he is being careful with a floor. "It goes off the public line. In your street it goes off about four minutes late in the winter. I have heard it from this table. I have not measured it, and I am not going to tell you a number I have not measured, but it is late, and it has probably been late for years, and a man coming in at ten and a bell at ten four has two things wrong with the morning and neither of them is the clock in the room."

"Then say the first one. Is the fifty seconds on my branch his?"

Orrin thought of the ward's forty minutes and the hospital branch at three hours and four minutes, and of a night sister's chart with *calibration strain* and no duration after it, and of Vance's column with nothing in it, and he kept the two blanks apart on purpose, because one of them was a hole somebody could have written in and one of them was a hole nobody had ever built.

"It is true that a night is fifty seconds on your branch," he said. "Whether those fifty seconds are your husband's is not in any return I can read. A token is a physical object with a punched value and is never a currency or a score. A hundred minutes is a hundred minutes of brass until it is spent, and then it is a hundred minutes of somebody's margin."

"Who's margin?"

"Whose branch is the hungriest one on the network. I cannot tell you and neither can he, and the reason I cannot tell you is that the instrument that would tell me does not exist and never has, and I have looked."

She sat with that. Behind her the child breathed and turned over and did not wake, and the gas ring ticked as it cooled, which is a sound a kitchen makes like a clock and is not one.

"He gets in at ten," she said. "Out at six. The bell at the works goes off the public line and it goes off four minutes late in the winter and nobody has ever come to look at it, and four minutes a day is two hours a week, and I have done that arithmetic in my head on the landing for two years." She pushed the token toward him. "So when I saw it on the Tuesday before last, and the man in the arcade said out loud that it was a hundred and twenty nights, I bought one, and I have carried it nine days, and I have not spent it, and I want you to tell me what spending it is."

"I can't."

"That's what I thought." She did not sound bitter and she did not sound defeated. She sounded like a woman who had brought a question to the wrong shop and had suspected as much on the stairs and had come up anyway, because the alternative was not to ask anybody.

"Will you put it in?" she said.

"No," he said. "I have no licence to certify a window and no standing to spend a district's minutes. I can clean this clock and I have. I cannot spend brass into it."

"Could I take it to a Quarter clock? Borrow one for an evening."

"Have you ever put one in?" she said. "Not this one. Any one."

"Once, on a bench, with the officer standing next to me, for a man who had never handled one, and I wrote the die number on the back of it and the officer wrote his own under mine." He said it without pride, because there had been none at the time either. "It is a good thing done properly. That is the difficulty. It can be done properly by about four people in this city and every one of them is behind a counter, and one of them is going in front of a panel on the Sunday after next, and I do not know which."

"Any clock of the Quarter's that isn't that clock is the same as this one," Orrin said. "The only clock it can go into is the one it came out of, and that belongs to a hall, and the hall is shut on Sunday and closed on Saturday and the man who could put it in for you does not know it exists and would not do it if he did."

She nodded as if she had expected that answer and had needed to hear it said by a man who mended clocks rather than sold them.

"Will you write down that I asked?" she said. "Not a receipt. Just that I asked and you said no, with the date."

He wrote it in the grey ledger on the stairs, with the date, in front of her: *Asked 26th, 9 Bellweather, 4th floor, to spend Q100 into mantel clock. Refused. No standing. No fee.*

A refusal binds the person who asked and nobody else. He did not say that sentence out loud because it belonged to a council's minutes and not to a kitchen, but he wrote it under the entry because a refusal that is not written down is a conversation.

He tore the page out and gave it to her, and she folded it into two and put it in the tin with the coins, and did not look at it again, and Orrin went down four floors past a man playing with a knife and a girl of nine who came in out of the cold and did not stop.

On the second landing a woman with a can of hot water asked him whether he was the one for the clock on the third and he said he was not and did not stop, and thought about that for the whole of the walk down, because a man in a trade who says no to a stair is doing the only thing in this city that can be done exactly right, and it does not pay and nobody finds out it was done.

On Friday the twenty-seventh he beat the mantel clock again, found it holding, and wrote the bill and read it back before he said it: four shillings and sixpence for the call and a shilling for the beat, five shillings and sixpence, exact, no materials. She paid in coin out of the same tin she had paid four and six out of, and counted it twice herself, and put the token back in its paper, and did not ask him to take it back, and he did not offer.

"How long can I keep it?" she said.

"As long as you like. It is lawful to hold. It will not go off. The only thing about it that changes is the edge where you have worn it bright."

On Saturday the twenty-eighth he wrote the kitchen in the book at length, because a kitchen nine feet by eleven with a gas ring and a list on a nail was where the city did its true business, and no Assembly man, no Directorate man and no guild man had been in it.

*Nine feet by eleven, gas ring, table, list on nail, fourteen households. Woman 34, paid 4s6d for Q100. Clock fouled by frame, moved, beat true. Asked to spend, refused for want of standing. Nobody thanked, nobody looked for it.*

Sunday the twenty-ninth he did no work that needed a counter, and the shop was shut, and the three places in this city where a person could walk in and ask a question and be answered by a stranger were all shut as well, and that was not an accident and not a religion. It was the only thing a public counter has that a counter in a back room does not: a day on which the person behind it is not there, and therefore a day on which nobody can be told anything by anybody by accident.
