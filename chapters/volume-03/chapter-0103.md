# Chapter 103 — The Die Drawer

On Thursday the nineteenth of the first month Orrin went to the guild hall on a job he could have sent a boy with, because the job was a pretext and the drawer was the reason, and he admitted both to himself on the walk up Verge Lane, which was further than he usually got with himself before ten.

The hospital branch that morning was three hours and four minutes and twenty seconds out of the district reference and gaining about fifty seconds a night. He knew the figure because he had done the working on the back of the rate notice Mara had left: two hours and one minute on Friday the fourth of the eleventh, seventy-six nights at fifty seconds, one hour three minutes and twenty seconds added, three hours four minutes twenty seconds. He did not say it on the stairs. He carried it the way Leda carried a ward's slack, as a number to be said in a room and not on a stair.

The licensing floor was quiet. The monthly list was on the wall, the counter polished, the die drawer behind it open because a first-class holder was cutting a token for a Quarter clock that had come back with surplus at the return.

Orrin waited his turn and watched. The drawer had nine district punches in it in a row, each in its loop, each oiled, each with its letter facing out, and blank space at the end where a tenth could go and had never gone. The officer cut the token, weighed the surplus, entered the weight in the plate book and put the token in a tray.

"Morning, Vale," the officer said. He was a man of about fifty, licensing officer for eleven years, necessary, correct, and the only person in the city whose job was to say what a token was made of. "Your licence still suspended?"

"Suspended on the first of the sixth, term unaltered, expires Sunday the nineteenth of the third month," Orrin said. "I am not here to ask about it."

"What are you here to ask about?"

"The drawer. How many punches, how many cut, how it is lawful."

The officer wiped his hands. "Nine punches. Q, W, G and six others. Blank space is blank space. The last return cut two hundred and sixty-one, took back two hundred and six, sold nine hundred and forty. The law says a token may be held, cut, sold and bought, and the law says what it is made of and nothing else. Twenty-two millimetres, three thick, rim, hole, letter, figure, year and die. That is the whole of it. The form is about 1883 and it has no column for what a token is for, because in 1883 nobody needed one."

"And the harm?" Orrin said.

"The harm is not in my drawer," the officer said, not unkindly. "A token spent in the clock it was cut from holds a wheel off a dead escapement for a stated time. That is the rule. What happens after — a Quarter clock sharing its rotation with the rest of a line, the network giving it to the next clock behind — that is not cutting, that is physics, and physics is not licensed. I am right about the law and I know I am wrong about the harm, and there is no form on which to be right about both."

Orrin put the printer's job on the counter, paid the counter fee, and asked the question he had come to ask. "If a man puts a Quarter token into a Weirs clock, whose minutes are they?"

The officer did not hesitate, because he had been asked it before and had answered it the same way every time. "The clock's. The reserve meter does not know where the brass was cut. The law does not know either, because the law never asked."

"And if the token is false?"

"Then it is a phantom minute, and a phantom minute in the network is slippage, and slippage is a machine repeating or skipping a movement. The assay office on this lane will weigh one free. Nobody brings them."

Orrin nodded and went out with the receipt in his pocket, and nobody thanked anybody, and the hall's clock struck eleven.

He walked to Green Span in the afternoon because Leda had left word that the ward's medicine times were going wrong and wanted a clockmaker's eye and not a clockmaker's answer.

The ward was warm and loud, eleven patients, three staff, the free master on the landing ticking clean and the line clocks three hours out and still stepping. In the nurses' room a chart on a board carried the phrase *calibration strain* against a night sister's name with no duration beside it. Orrin looked at it once and looked away, because the empty duration column beside Vance's name in the pumping ward's glass case was a statement and this blank was a different building's different blank, and the two were not comparable and no chapter of his life would treat them as comparable.

Leda was at the bracket clock with two watches round her neck. She was twenty-seven and tired in the way nurses are tired in January, which is a different tired from December.

"Say it," she said, without preamble.

"Three hours and four minutes and twenty seconds, gaining fifty seconds a night," Orrin said, in the room, in front of the people whose hours they were. "A hundred minutes is a hundred and twenty nights. The ward's whole slack on the best night of the year is forty minutes. Two hundred minutes is five times the whole of the ward's slack, and two tokens cost nine shillings, and the ward has no fund, and the branch has been stepping for about two hundred and seventy-three nights and two tokens last two hundred and forty."

A student nurse wrote it down. Nobody thanked him, and Leda did not look for it.

"And the medicine?" he said.

"We give it on the free master and write down the line clock beside it," Leda said. "We cannot guarantee every dose on time. We can publish the missing intervals and tell the wards which patients are most urgent. The hospital's reduced efficiency is a real cost, not a hidden line item. That is what we do with a number we cannot spend."

He checked the bracket clock's beat, set it as true as it would go, and refused to certify anything, because he had no licence and the offence of Volume 01 was spent and was not to be repeated as a joke.

On the walk home he thought about the drawer with its nine punches and its blank space, and about a licensing officer who was right about the law and wrong about the harm and knew it, and about a ward with forty minutes of slack being asked to measure itself against a hundred.

At the bench that night he wrote the drawer in the grey ledger, and the hospital figure, and the chart with its blank he had not filled and would not.

*Drawer: nine punches, blank space. Law is what it is made of. Purpose is a hole in a form about 1883. Keeper at assay weighs free, four minutes, half a grain. Officer knows the harm and has no form for it.*
