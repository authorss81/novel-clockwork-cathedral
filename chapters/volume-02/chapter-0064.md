# Chapter 64 — The Third Practice, and a Bending Frame

The third practice is on a bridge joint, and the joint is out of a bridge, and the frame is a bending frame, and Orrin Vale loaded a **tension** splice four tons in a machine built for bending a beam, and Ivo Pell told him so on the Wednesday afternoon and did not stop him, because the number the frame gave was worth more than the machine it was in.

That is the finding of the chapter, and it is not supernatural, and it has been available to any clockmaker in the trade for about a hundred and forty years.

---

The stores at the district works' yard on the canal road keep a **bending frame** that has not been used since the yard laid a floor slab in about 1948, and it cost the works four hundred and ten pounds and it is two knife edges set eleven feet apart in a cast bed, a screw jack with a three-quarter-inch thread, and a bracket for a dial indicator that is missing.

The dial indicator was in the same cupboard, in its case, in a piece of oiled paper, and it is a hundred-thousandth-reading gauge with a thousandth of an inch on the dial and a lever, and it was the works' own and it was bought in about 1931 and there is no name on it.

**The lead standard was also in the cupboard**, in a wooden box, and it is a bar of lead about eleven inches long and two inches by one and a half, cast, with a hole bored in one end and a figure stamped in the end face: **1/2, 20.** It is a standard for the frame. It was made to be loaded, and it is the only object in that store with a figure on it, and it is made of the one metal in this trade that will not come back to where it started.

He asked Ambrose for the frame on the Friday before, at the counter, and Ambrose said yes in about four seconds and then said a condition.

"You leave them as you found them, and I enter the four items in the issue book with a line against each, and the lines are mine and not yours, because you have no number in this yard and I am not going to invent one for you in a book that goes to an inspectorate."

"That's fair," said Orrin Vale.

"It is not fair, it is a book," said Ambrose. "I have had a set of five lines in this book in the fourth month that I did not take out of a drawer, and I have written them down unasked twice, and I am not going to have a sixth one be a thing a man with no licence did to my frame."

---

**Sunday the twenty-fourth. Two hours on the frame, and he got the zero wrong, and the zero was wrong by two thousandths of an inch, and he did not find out for two days.**

The procedure is the same in every trade and the order of it is the whole of the skill. You zero the machine. You load it in steps and read at each step. You unload it and read again at zero load. If it comes back, the thing is elastic and you have learned something about it. **If it does not come back, it has moved, and a thing that has moved is a thing that is going to fail, and no further measurement is required to know that, only a decision about when.**

The lead standard is how you zero a frame, because it is a bar of known section that every foundry's testing room had and every works' store had and nobody has had since about 1940. He set the dial to zero against the lead, loaded the lead to about two tons, unloaded it, and read at zero.

**It came back two thousandths low.** Lead creeps. Every time you load it and take it off, a bar of lead is very slightly shorter than it was, and in a testing frame that creep is a permanent change of length in the standard itself, and if you have zeroed against it you have not zeroed the frame, you have measured the lead.

He found out on the Tuesday, when he loaded the frame's own calibration bar — a mild-steel bar of known section that lives in the same cupboard under a piece of newspaper with a date on it — and got two thousandths out against a lead standard that was supposed to be the datum.

**The lead standard is not a datum. It is a thing that moves, slowly, in the direction of being shorter, and every number taken against it is two thousandths optimistic after about forty years, and a man who does not know that will sign his name under a figure that is wrong in the direction that lets the iron look better than it is.**

He wrote both of those down and he wrote the date, and the paper with the date on the steel bar is in the book in a sleeve, and the bar was bought by a man in about 1931 and there is no name on it.

---

**Monday the twenty-fifth. The joints.**

The Bellweather Road footbridge was renewed in June, in the ordinary course, and the old cast-iron came out whole and went into the yard's store, and there is a heap of it against the north wall about nine feet long, and **four tension splices out of it** — two flanges, four bolts, cast in 1911, about eleven feet six long, of a section that was rolled for the Bellweather bridge and has not been rolled since about 1908.

None of them has a mark on it. They are the same casting, from the same pour, out of the same four hundred yards of structure, and there is a number stamped in one corner of each which is the pattern number and not a number that distinguishes one from another, and the yard's store is a store.

That is the practical problem and it took him the whole of Monday morning and it is a real problem and not a story: **you cannot test four identical castings and act on the answer unless you can say which one is which, and nobody in that yard ever said which one is which, because in 1911 nobody imagined a day when a person would want to know.**

He solved it the way a man solves it with a paint pot and a piece of chalk, which is that he marked them A, B, C and D in chalk on the flanges, in his own hand, and then found that chalk comes off iron in a yard in about a fortnight and did not solve it, and then marked them in **oil paint**, and then wrote the letter and the date in his own book, and then stood there for about a minute looking at four identical cast-iron ties in a yard with a piece of oil paint, and understood that in about eleven years this will be the only thing in this city that knows which is which.

---

**The procedure, and the numbers, and the wrong instrument.**

A bending frame with knife edges eleven feet apart is a simply supported beam. You put a tie across it, jack the middle, and read extension or deflection on a dial indicator on a stand. That is a perfectly good way of comparing one casting with another and it is **not a testing machine, and a foundry would not accept a single figure out of it**, and Ivo Pell said so on the Wednesday and did not stop him.

Load in steps to **four tons**. Two runs, up and down. Read at each step. Compare each casting against the 1951 comparison bar, same section, same length, out of the yard's own stock, which has never been loaded and which is the only thing in that store with no history in it.

> **Steel bar, 1951, unloaded, same frame, four tons: 0.312 in.**
> **A. 1911, four tons: 0.318 in. Off load, returns to within 0.002 in of where it started.**
> **B. 1911, four tons: 0.346 in. Off load, returns to 0.011 in below where it started.**
> **C. 1911, four tons: 0.319 in. Off load, returns to within 0.002 in.**
> **D. 1911, four tons: 0.320 in. Off load, returns to within 0.002 in.**

B is the only one of the four that has moved, and it has moved eleven thousandths of an inch, and eleven thousandths is not a large number. It is eleven thousandths.

**It is a fortieth of an inch that you can see from a towpath, on a bracket, in daylight, from a boat.** He wrote that down in the same breath, in the same hand, in the same book, because the man who made the tell-tale is the man who counted the barrel's ninety-eight teeth, and **the brass pointer at eleven shillings and fourpence exists because somebody worked out how to make a fortieth of an inch visible to somebody who is not a clockmaker, and this is the same arithmetic done twice in one book by one man, and nobody in this city has ever put the two of them side by side.**

---

**And on the Sunday, on the Friday before, on the Saturday, and again on the Monday, he put his hand on B and asked the four words, and got the four things, four times, and B is the one that gave him the fourth one.**

Heat at the bolt line, on the lower flange, at the third bolt from the end. Vibration, small, in the splice, at the top of the load. Resistance, and a place where the resistance is not the resistance it should be. And the edge at the limit of it, the fourth thing, which is not a number and has no shape and cannot be described to a man who has not had it.

**He got all four off A as well, and A is sound.** A has oxide on the same bolt line and A has a spot of rust in the same place where the resistance is wrong, and A returns to within two thousandths.

That is the finding and it took him two days and a frame to be able to write it down.

> *Twenty-fourth to the twenty-fifth of the seventh. Four cast-iron tension splices, 1911, out of the Bellweather Road footbridge, in the works' store, in a bending frame that has not been used since about 1948.*
> *One of the four has moved eleven thousandths of an inch and three have not. **I found the one that has moved with my hand, and the frame told me how much.***
> *And here is the thing I have not got round in six months of writing things down: **half of what I have been calling a gift is instruments.***
> *The ear found it. That is all the ear does. It finds the joint that is going to fail out of four identical castings in nine minutes. **And then you measure it**, and the measuring is a dial indicator in a stand and a comparison bar and an oil pot, and it cost eleven pounds and eleven minutes, and the eleven pounds bought a number that will be in a book in this yard in about nine years, long after I am not able to climb into that frame.*
> *And the other half cannot be bought and I have been trying to sell it to nobody and buy it from nobody and it is the half that says which of the four to put your hand on, and I cannot teach it and I cannot write it down and I cannot certify it.*

And then, because he is the sort of man who cannot leave a thing alone:

> *And the other half of that sentence is the one I have to be careful about. If the ear is the instrument that finds it and the dial indicator is the instrument that measures it, then **the two together are a machine, and a machine can be bought, and a man who owns two machines is a man who can be checked, and a man who can be checked is a man whose word is worth something in a room.** That is not a small thing and I have had it in a book for two days and I have not been able to put it anywhere better than here.*

---

**Ivo Pell came on the Wednesday the twenty-seventh at about four in the afternoon, which is eleven days after the last time, which is about a fortnight, and Orrin Vale wrote in the book that he was not going to be the man who counted it.**

Pell came into the store, looked at the frame for about half a minute from the doorway, and did not come in, and said the whole of what he had to say about the machine from where he was standing.

"That is a bending frame and you have pulled a tie in it."

"Yes."

"Do you know what a foundry does with a number out of that?"

"No."

"They do not accept it," said Ivo Pell. "They ask what it was measured against, and if you say *a bar in a cupboard*, they will not put your name on a drawing. **A number is nothing. A number and a thing it is compared with is a measurement, and if the thing is the same shape and the same year and has never been loaded, then it is worth having, and that is all it is worth, and you have written that down already and I have not read it.**"

"I did not want to touch it," said Pell. "You will want to know why, so I will tell you, and it is not humility and it is not a lesson. **I am an operator. If I put a finger on a maintainer's instrument, then in any room anybody ever puts us both in, I am a second man who can be wrong, and I have spent twelve years being a man who is not wrong in public, and that is the entire asset, and I am not going to spend it on a dial indicator in a shed in a yard.**"

Then he read the four lines of figures off the page without touching it, standing up, at about nine feet, which he did not say he could do and which was not the reason he was there.

"A is sound, B has moved eleven thousandths, C and D are sound, and the bar is three hundred and twelve thousandths at four tons." He turned the page over and looked at the underside, which is blank. "Now I am going to say the thing that costs me, and I have said at the top of that sheet on the nineteenth of the sixth that I would say my reason once, and this is a different thing from my reason, and I am saying it because if I do not say it now I shall not say it and you will be a man in a room in about two years with a number and no way of standing in it."

"Somebody in a good coat is going to ask you whether your readings are instruments."

"Yes," said Orrin Vale.

"And you are going to say no, and you will be lying, and you will not know that you are lying, because in about a year the ear and the frame will have been working together so long that the answer will feel like a fact about a machine instead of a fact about a man. **And I am going to be asked the same question, separately, and I am going to have to say yes, and I am going to have to say it in a room, and I am not going to have a copy of anything you have written, and the fact that I have read it from nine feet away and remember it is not going to help me at all.**"

"That is what the arrangement is for," said Orrin Vale. "You do not have to."

"I have never signed it," said Ivo Pell, "and I am not going to, and you have known that since June and so have I, and the reason I am telling you this on this afternoon is that a man who has not signed a piece of paper is a man who can be asked to produce it, and there is no version of this in which I produce it."

He looked at the frame again.

"Now the price of the season, and it is one sentence, and then I am going to Northglass. **The practice is the winter and the winter is when the work is.** In about nineteen weeks every hospital branch, every tank, every bridge joint and every stopcock in this city will be at its worst, and every one of them will be somebody's fault, and every one of them will be a decision somebody took in a room. So you have nineteen weeks to be able to stand in a yard with a machine and be told a number and not need anybody to tell you what it means. After that you are the man who has a pencil book and no autumn."

"November," said Orrin Vale.

"November," said Ivo Pell, from the doorway. "And I will come on a Tuesday because I said I would and not because you asked, and if you write that down I shall stop."

He did not write it down. He wrote down the date, and he wrote down *Tuesday*, and he has never once in eleven years been able to tell anybody the difference between those two entries and he has looked at them a great many times.

---

**And the last thing that happened that week was not in the yard and it is the only reason to record the week at all.**

The works' file for the Green Span hospital trestle is a card, and the card is in the works' maintenance schedule, and the card is not the conduit bracket. Item seven of the schedule is the bracket, and the bracket is a bracket, and the bracket had a survey in the fifth month of this year and a two-year-old estimate and a new casting out of a pattern in box 41, and every one of those is in Chapter 42 and every one of them is on the page.

**The card he wanted was a different card.** The trestle is not a bridge; it is a timber and iron structure built in 1911 to carry a hospital branch across a cut, and it has a **movement joint at each end**, and a movement joint is not a bracket and does not fail by shearing a fixing and does not take a load until it has moved and keeps moving, and there is a survey card for it with an interval on it.

**Interval: four years. Last survey: two years and one month ago.**

He read the card in the file at about half past four on the Wednesday, standing up, and then he went and read the works' own published maintenance schedule on the board in the yard, because Ambrose publishes everything, and the trestle's movement joints are on it under item nine, at **two pounds four shillings and sixpence**, and the line reads *survey, four-year interval, condition of movement, both ends*, and there is no condition column and no last-survey column in the published schedule, only the cost.

**Which means the interval is on the card in the file, and the date is on the card in the file, and the two are not in the published schedule, and the published schedule is the thing the works puts in front of a committee.** So the interval is a thing a man in a yard knows and the fact that it has not been done is a thing a man in a yard knows, and there is no version of it that reaches a room.

It is not overdue. It is not due for eleven months. That is the point and it took him an hour to see it and it is the whole of Chapter 64.

> *Twenty-seventh of the seventh. A cast-iron movement joint at each end of a hospital trestle built in 1911, with a four-year interval, last surveyed two years and one month ago.*
> *It is not due. That is the finding and I have turned it over nine times tonight and it is the finding.*
> *A joint on a hospital trestle has an interval and a date and neither of them is in the published schedule, and the published schedule has a cost against it, and the cost is two pounds four shillings and sixpence, and **the cost is the only part of the line anybody can see from a chair.**
> *There is nothing to do about it for eleven months. That is not a comfort. A thing with an interval and a date and nobody reading the date is a thing that has been due before and was not done, and the card does not say whether it was, because the card has three columns and the fourth is *I am not sure*, and the hall found out in about 1908 that men will say anything at all if the box is big enough, and the works' cards are smaller than the hall's.*

And then the ledger line that is the count, and it is the count because it was the one thing in the week that could have moved it.

> *My own hand corrections on this city's machines: **two.** Eleven minutes on a lane in the Weirs and eight on a bracket at the Ferrywright corner, both in the second month, both now inside an institutional form. Two. And there were four cast-iron splices in a yard this week that are not machines in this city's service and are not on any schedule and are going back into a heap against a wall on Monday, and one of them has moved eleven thousandths, and I could have put my hand on the middle of it and heard a note and walked away and let a bridge carry it, **and I did not, and the not-doing was not difficult, and that is worse.***
