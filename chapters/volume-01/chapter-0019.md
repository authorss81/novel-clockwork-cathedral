# Chapter 19 — The Pump He Could Not Certify

The pump was in the wet wall on the third-floor landing of the Green Span hospital's east wing, behind a flush door with a plate on it that said *rising main, auxiliary, no admittance*, and it had been putting up with the building for sixty years.

It was a vertical spindle pump, brass, with a belt off a small motor, and the belt had been re-laced twice and the gland had been packed twice, and Ellen Reeve had been the hospital's engineer for twenty-two years and had done both of those things herself and had told Orrin so before she let him put his hand on the case.

"That's the last thing anybody's touched," she said. "I did the packing last summer and I did it wrong, because I did it the way you do it when you can get at it from the landing, which is not the way, and I've known that since August. So before you start: if it needs pulling the spindle and reseating, I want that on the record as mine and not as a fault."

"Noted," said Orrin. "Now go and stand at the far end of the landing with Mara, because I'm going to say what I'm going to ask out loud before I ask it, and I'd like the two of you to hear the words and not just the shape of them."

There were four people on that landing. Ellen Reeve. Mara Venn, who had come up from the city in a plain coat and had a notebook out. Leda Vale, who was twenty minutes into a shift. And Orrin.

He put his right hand flat on the pump's case and left it there, and made himself stand in the middle of the landing and say it in the middle of the noise of a hospital, because that was the only way he knew to be sure afterwards of the exact words.

"Four things. Take the fourth away and throw it away if you want, because I am not able to stop anybody taking it, and I have been in a room where nine men took their coats off after I said less than this, and I would like it on the record that I know what this is worth and I know what it costs.

"One. I can feel the gland going. Not that it is damaged — I know the gland is damaged, Ellen told me she packed it last summer, and it is packed wrong and it will go. I mean I can feel when the second will be. If this pump is asked for the head it is asking for now, on a night shift, with the corridor clock eleven minutes out from the ward clock, it will hold for about two hours and then it will let go sideways, and the water will come out of the gland and go down the landing, because there is nothing under that landing but a linen store and a stair."

"How long is *about two hours*?" said Ellen Reeve.

"That's the honest weakness in it and I am not going to dress it. Two hours is a shape, not a number. It could be ninety minutes on a cold night and it could be three hours on a warm one with a light head, and I cannot put a figure on it, and if anybody in this room asks me for a figure I will make one up and it will be worth nothing."

"Then how is that any use?" said Leda.

"It is use if somebody can do something about ninety minutes." He did not take his hand off. "Two. What I do not know. I do not know whether the evening list has it in it. I do not know what the pharmacy has been drawing off it this week, and I do not know who is going to be on that landing at two o'clock in the morning, and I have not asked, because if I have to ask, then the answer arrives too late to be a number and arrives exactly on time to be a warning, and a warning at two in the morning in a stairwell is not a warning, it is a story to tell afterwards.

"Three. Which of it is a guess. The gland is a guess, and it is a guess Ellen made last summer and I have checked. Two hours is the reading and it is mine and there is no instrument in this city that will confirm it, and I cannot put my name under it in any way that anybody is obliged to believe. There is a tape measure in my case and a tape measure will tell you about a gland the same way it will tell you about a bridge: not at all.

"Four. What it costs." He looked round at the three of them. "This is the part I have never once got right in my life and I am going to get it right in a landing. Fixing this properly means isolating the main, and the main is not ours, the main comes off the Lower Weirs pumping line and it runs the whole length of the canal frontage, and isolating it and drawing from it is a district toll, and a district toll takes minutes off a district's clocks to hold a window open so the work can be done. That is what a local toll is. That is what the words mean. And I have to certify it before anybody can draw it, and I cannot."

The landing was extremely quiet. Down the corridor a lift arrived and went away again.

---

"No," said Mara Venn.

She said it the way she said things, without weight, and she had her notebook shut.

"No, you cannot. And I want to be careful, because there are four people on this landing and three of us have watched you do this before and one of us has watched you do it in a pump house with nine men in it, and I am not going to be the one who is cruel. So: no, and here is the whole of the no, and it takes about ninety seconds, and then we will have tried everything."

She opened the notebook.

"A maintainer's licence, class three, may service and certify one civic machine. That is your licence. This pump is a civic machine. You may put your hand on this pump and you may certify this pump and the certificate would be true and worth the paper it is on. And that certificate does not get anybody anything, because the certificate is a statement about a pump, and what is broken is not the pump, it is the money.

"The repair needs the main isolated for a window. The window is bought with minutes. The minutes come off a district's reserve, and if a district's reserve is spent doing a thing for another district, that is not a repair, that is a Reading, and a Reading is charged to a district reserve and drawn in proportion to what the district holds. Which reserve? The pumping ward's, because the main is the pumping ward's. And the pumping ward has no reserve, and has not had one since March, and everyone in this city knows it because the warden said it out loud in a pump house seventeen days ago in front of a man with a notebook."

"Sel Kade would consent to it," said Leda.

"Sel Kade would consent to it and there is no Sel Kade in this building and no way to reach her that does not take until Monday, and if she consented on Monday it would be for the Tuesday, and the list for that night has forty minutes of ward slack in it and two hours of it unbudgeted, and I have already spent them." Leda said it without turning round. "So the answer is: no reserve, no window, and no way to get consent from a district that does not exist by Friday."

"Right," said Mara. "Second route. The Office. I am going to write to my office this afternoon and I am going to ask it to concur in a district toll for a hospital on the strength of a public interest, and I am going to tell you now, before I do it, exactly what will come back, because I have had nine years of it and I have never once been wrong. It will come back inside a week. It will be courteous. And it will say that the Office of Public Reckoning audits the Toll Stair and has no authority over a district reserve and no power to concur in a toll and no standing in the Lower Weirs, and that application should be made to the district works committee. Which is the note I got eleven days ago, about the certified shift returns, word for word, in the same hand."

"Nothing happens," said Ellen Reeve.

"Nothing happens," said Mara. "Third route. The Cathedral. A Reading can carry a window. That is precisely what a Reading is: a machine that holds several districts in agreement for four hours so that somebody can do a difficult thing. It has a rail. Three witnesses. Operator, affected district, record keeper, and the affected-district station is filled by a Green Span resident on a rota, and we both watched that happen three weeks ago, and it happened to a man who filed it himself. And the rail is booked a day ahead. It is Tuesday afternoon. Tomorrow's rail has eleven readings on it and two of them are hospital schedules. Sunday's rail — " she stopped for a beat. "The Cathedral does not sit a rail on a Sunday for a pump. I have looked. There is no Sunday."

"Then that's the end of it," said Ellen Reeve.

"No," said Mara Venn. "That is the end of every route that is lawful. There is one more and I am not going to pretend it is not there, because you all know it and one of you has known it since August."

She looked at Orrin.

"Your licence may certify a single civic machine. It may not certify a toll. There is no licence above yours in this city except a guild master or a Continuity Office inspector, and both of those men are men who have said no to this hall's maintainers in writing, and recently." She put a hand flat on the pump case, next to his, and did not touch it. "You could certify the window. You could put your name under a local toll for the Lower Weirs pumping main on a class-three licence, and the paper would be perfectly well made, and every hour of the rest of your life you could look at it and it would still be a piece of paper with your name on it that you had no standing whatsoever to write. And I want to say the rest of it, because the rest of it is the part that matters and I have watched you get it wrong twice. That act is not a grey area. That act is the one thing in Morrowfen that a man cannot do and survive doing. It is not a risk. It is the end of your trade. And it would work, Orrin. It is the correct instrument. It is the only instrument in the country that would hold that main open for three hours, and it would work, and in about eleven months a man in a good office would take a piece of paper out of a drawer and put your name next to a number."

---

Orrin took his hand off the pump.

He had been holding it for four minutes and he had been listening, and the answer had come up about two minutes in, and it was the same answer it had given him at the yard on the Friday: the gland, going sideways, in about two hours, and then the rest of it, and behind that the landing, and the linen store, and a ward clock.

The room went grey at the edges and the hum came up on the right and there was a smell of hot metal at the back of his throat, and this time it did not stop.

It took the best part of four minutes by the corridor clock, or by the state of things. He was aware of the landing, and of the flush door, and of a voice saying his name once and then not saying it for a long time.

When it was finished he was on the floor with his back against the wall and his legs out, and Ellen Reeve was kneeling at the gland with a torch and swearing quietly, and Mara Venn was holding his wrist, and Leda was standing over all of them not touching him.

"What happened," said Leda.

"You asked it a bigger question than the one I said out loud," said Orrin.

He got up in stages with the wall, and then sat on the bottom step of the stair with his hands on his knees.

"It is twenty past one," said Leda. "Off my own watch. I have not looked at the ward clock or the corridor clock since you went down and I am not going to start now."

"Five items," he said. "I'll say it now so that nobody has to ask me afterwards what I knew and when.

"One. The gland goes sideways, at about two hours' head, and the water goes down the landing. Two. I have no instrument for the two hours. Three. The thing that would hold the main open is a district toll, and a class-three licence cannot certify a district toll, and Green Span cannot get a standing to certify a toll on the Lower Weirs' main, and the pumping ward's reserve was spent in March on Carrow Street. Four. A Reading could carry the window and the rail is booked a day and the Cathedral does not sit a rail on a Sunday, and the Office will write me a note inside a week telling me I have no power. Five. The only instrument that would do it in time is the one thing I am not allowed to do, and I am not going to do it, and that is a decision and not a tactic, and I would like it written down that I made it before I felt like making it and not after."

Mara Venn wrote all of that down, and read it back, and he made her read the fifth one twice.

Then she tore the page out and gave it to him.

"Take that," she said. "You will want it in about four months, and there is going to be a day when you decide that a rule is a thing that was true in a pump house and is not true now, and on that day you are going to want a piece of paper with a witness on it."

---

They did the sensible things, which were the only things there were.

Ellen Reeve packed the gland properly with the spindle drawn and reseated, which is what she had asked to have on the record and which buys a fortnight, and they all knew it, and set out a written watch for the night staff, and put a bucket and a mop and a torch in the linen store and wrote the three of them on the inside of the cupboard door where a person opening the door in a hurry would see them.

Orrin did the certificate. It took nine minutes and it was the whole of what his licence allowed him, and it was true: *auxiliary rising main, third-floor landing, gland packing renewed, spindle within tolerance, no defect in the certified sense of the term, no recommendation.*

Then he turned it over and wrote on the back of it, in full, in his own hand, because that was the deal and he was going to hold to it while it was costing him:

*Packing renewed by E. Reeve, hospital engineer, at my direction, 2nd month, day 7. My reading of this pump is that the gland will fail sideways at load within approximately two hours' head, and I have said so in a room with four people in it and one of them can be got on the telephone. I have not certified a toll and I have certified no district reserve. I can certify nothing else about this machine. M. Vale, class three, no standing on this main.*

He read that back twice before he stamped it, and it took him nine minutes longer than a class-three certificate has ever taken in the history of the guild, and the reverse of a guild certificate is not read by anybody, and he knew that, and it was not why he had written it.

The telephone call to the ward sister was Leda's and it took four minutes and the sister was not pleased and did not pretend to be. The pharmacy was told to go back to the ward clock and the ward clock was to be compared against the corridor clock and the corridor clock against the public clock in the porter's hall, three clocks, by hand, every four hours, and every one of those three clocks is now going to disagree with the other two, and everybody in that room knows it, and the pharmacy is doing it anyway because the alternative is worse.

And then the hospital wrote to the Office of Public Reckoning, and Mara filed the request herself rather than let the hospital's clerk do it, because she wanted it on the right paper.

It came back on the Wednesday, a day after it went in, which was five days quicker than the same office had managed the pumping ward's returns eleven days before, and nobody in the building could account for the difference and neither could he. It is four lines long, and it is the single most useful document the Office of Public Reckoning produced that month.

> *Received and noted. This office audits the Toll Stair. It has no authority over a district reserve, no power to concur in a local toll, and no standing in the Lower Weirs. Application should be made to the district works committee.*
> *No further correspondence on this matter.*

*No further correspondence on this matter.* It was printed in the corner in the same small type as the blind margin on a Reading notice, and Orrin thought about that for a long time, and it turned out to be the most useful thing anybody had said to him all month, because it told him in four lines exactly what a form was for, which is to make the boundary of an office legible in a sentence that a person can carry.

---

Leda Vale caught him in the corridor on the Thursday morning at seven, outside the nurses' office, on her way off, at the end of a run of three nights.

He had come in on the Thursday to ask whether the pharmacy had been told anything he had not already said, and she had read the ward chart that morning and neither of them said anything about that either.

"This morning," she said. "The pharmacy asked me this morning why the pulse on the third floor had been taken off the corridor clock and put back on the ward clock in the middle of a night shift by a man from a clock shop. And I told her, and she said — she actually said — 'well, that's what the paperwork says, isn't it.' And she was right. That is what the paperwork says. The paperwork says that the ward clock is the correct clock, because the ward clock is the one the medicine is drawn off, and I have been saying that for four years in three different meetings."

"Leda—"

"So I have to tell you what I have got to say, and I have been not saying it since Tuesday and I am saying it now, and I am not saying it about you, and I want that clear before I start, because if I say it about you I get to be angry and I have not got the time to be angry today." She put her back against the wall, in the way people do in a corridor they are going to have to leave in a minute. "On Tuesday I said out loud, in a room, with four people in it, exactly what a thing was worth and exactly what it would cost to be wrong, and I named the cost out loud, and the naming was correct. Every word of it was correct. You were right and the room could not act and the thing you wanted to happen has not happened. There is a boy of twenty in this district who will hammer a piece of tram line into that gland on Sunday morning and do it better than the hospital's own engineer could have done it last summer, and there is not going to be a form for it, and it is going to be the right thing to do, and it is going to be a matter of record in about two years, and I am not going to stop him, and I am not going to be able to say afterwards that I did not know it was coming.

"And whatever that boy does on Sunday, in about four days a man who was almost out of round the time will be back on that pump, and it will go again, and it will go again in the winter, and every single time somebody will be in a room deciding whether to write to the Cathedral, and every single time the answer will be what it was on Tuesday, and every single time the answer will be correct."

"Mara filed the request. It came back yesterday. It's in the file."

"I know it's in the file, I have read it, it is in the file because I asked her to send it to me." Leda's eyes were very bright and she was not crying. "So here is the hard thing. I set you a rule in a kitchen at five in the morning and I priced it and I thought I had understood it. I understood it well. And I have been wrong about one part of it, and I want to correct it, because I am not going to be one of those people who is right once.

"The rule says the toll gets named in a room to somebody who can object. And I told you in that kitchen that the rule was worth nothing when the person who needs it is a patient and not a witness, and I was right, and I have thought about it every day since Tuesday, and I have got it the wrong way round."

She stopped and started again.

"The rule did not fail because I could not object. I could object. I stood in that landing and I said the pharmacy was drawing off the wrong clock, and you listened to me, and you wrote it down. The rule did not fail there. The rule failed afterwards, in a file, on a piece of paper, when a thing I said in a room with four people in it went into an envelope and came back the next day with *no further correspondence on this matter* in the corner in small type, and there was nobody in that landing to shout at, and nobody to object to, and no room anywhere in the whole of this business."

She took her coat off the hook and shook it out.

"You did everything you said you would do. You said it out loud, you named what you did not know, you named the cost, you put your name under a thing you can certify and not under a thing you cannot, and you did it in company, and you handed it to a competent woman who could not help you either, and you did not do the one thing that would have worked. And the ward will still have a morning, and the morning will be four minutes wide, and somebody will write down in about a year that a hospital on the Green Span line lost four minutes in the one week of a month in a year nobody can remember.

"That is the whole of it. Your rule was kept. The rule worked. And it is a rule that takes eleven weeks and four letters and an inquiry to fail, and in those eleven weeks a boy is going to be holding that pump up with a wedge of tram line he cut off a rail, and a girl of nineteen on nights is doing the work of the ward clock by hand and getting paid for it, and she does not know that any of it is connected."

She put her coat on.

"Do not you dare do it on your own," she said. "That is the last thing and then I am going. If you go up that landing alone on a Tuesday night with a bar and a licence in your pocket, you will fix the pump, and it will be the best piece of work you have ever done, and in eleven months a man in a good office will put your name next to a number, and I will be the one holding the lamp and I will have no idea what I am holding it for. Don't. Whatever you do next, do it in a room with somebody in it who can refuse."

She went off down the corridor with her hands in her pockets and got as far as the stair and turned round.

"And bring the soup back on Sunday, if you are going past the kitchen. The soup was not good either. I have eaten worse on nights and I have eaten better from a bin."

---

Teo Arden shimmed the gland on the Sunday.

He did it at about six in the morning with two of his own people and a length of copper from a tram-line bond, driven in eight courses with a drift and a hammer, and the top of it was painted with three coats of hospital white so that anybody who came on that landing in the dark would know that a man had been here and had done something on purpose, and had left his name on the bottom of the eighth course where only a man kneeling down would find it.

There is no form for that. There is no licence for it and no tariff and no way of invoicing it and no office that could receive a report of it without opening an inquiry, and it is the most competent piece of repair work done on that pump in sixty years, and the man who did it is twenty years old and unlicensed, and he told Orrin, on the Sunday evening, in a corridor, in nine words, that he would do it again on Thursday and did not think anybody was going to stop him.

It held.

The following Wednesday night the ward had a morning. Two medicines arrived four minutes apart, the insulin from the pharmacy and the anticoagulant from the ward's own drawer, and the four minutes was not anybody's fault, and the two doses were within the window the sister had written on the back of a duty list, and the sister said the word *within* about eleven times that morning and cried twice in the linen store for no reason anybody could identify, and did not apologise for it.

The pump held for five weeks. On the sixth, a district works committee sat in Green Span and did nothing for two hours and then referred the rising main to the regional programme for the following year, which is a real document with a real number on it, and which arrives, if it arrives, in fourteen months.

The seventh of the month. She was gone by then and back on nights and the certificate was in his coat pocket that was true, and Orrin Vale stood in a corridor on the third floor of the Green Span hospital and found that being right about a pump had cost him a set of stairs he would not get back.

Because that was what it had taken. Not his hand. Not the ring, which was worse and would go on being worse. Thirty-eight minutes. He put it together afterwards out of a woman and a rubber stamp, and neither of them was a clock, which in that building was the only kind of evidence left. Leda had said twenty past one when he came round, off her own wrist and not the ward's. The certificate lying on the counter in front of him was stamped at two minutes to two, because he had read the back of it over twice before he pressed it, and there was no account of him in that gap at all, clean edges, no residue, and four people on a landing who had all been looking at somebody else.

And a permanent change in the thing that had made him who he was, which was not that he could hear less. He could hear. He could still hear a watch at thirty feet in a still room and he could still hear one lying on the bench a foot away, and he had tested both since the Tuesday, because it was the first thing he had checked and the thing he most expected to be gone. What was gone was the judgement. He put a movement on the bench on the Sunday and it sounded correct, and it was out by a quarter of a second a day, and he could not tell. He listened to it eleven times. He listened to it with a sounding board under the case and he listened to it with the case lifted off, and the sound was the same sound, and there was no longer any part of him that knew which of the two the sound was making.

He had done that since he was fourteen. He had walked into a room and put two fingers on a plate and known, and the knowing had never once been a thing he had had to work for, and there was now no version of the doing that got the knowing back, because nothing had been strained and nothing had broken and there was nothing to mend. It had simply been taken.

It was the largest thing the machine had ever taken off him, and it was the one he could least afford, because a clockmaker who cannot judge a sound is a man who has to check everything, and a man who checks everything is a man who has to be in the room, and the room is the thing he cannot be in twice a week. He understood, standing in that corridor with his hand flat on the wall, that this was what it cost to want to do something so much that his body put the whole of it on his account before he had decided.

Four minutes wide. A copper wedge with his name under the eighth course. A form with a price on it that had been right two years ago. Nine districts in a book. Eleven copies in nine buildings. Thirty-one households without a rise. A sister in a corridor. A woman in an office with four lines and no further correspondence.

Nothing, anywhere, in any place anybody could read.
