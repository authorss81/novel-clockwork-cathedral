# Chapter 116 — Forty Pounds

On Friday the tenth of the third month a woman of thirty-one came to the back room behind a tobacconist's in Weir Lane with a printed price list, and on the Saturday morning after that six people met there and there was a company that did not exist, and the minute said so, and the money went into an envelope, and nobody thanked anybody.

The room was over the shop and behind the shop and had been a storeroom for shirts until about 1949, and it had a table that did not match the chairs, and a gas fire that had been out since the coal came back, and there were six of them in it and a seventh person standing up, which was the clerk.

She gave her name as Verity Slee and her age as thirty-one there and then, on the Friday, and she said both because she had been told by a man the previous week that people in this city write things down afterwards, and she had come to a room where things are written down, and she had come a day early because the room belonged to the district and not to her.

She put the price list on the table face up and squared it and said, "Before anybody decides anything. A limited company is formed by a memorandum. The memorandum has one clause in it that the law insists on and everything else in it is whatever you want it to be, and the whole of the rest is a list of directors, a list of shares, and a place. The list of directors has to have a person in it, because a company is a body and a body has to be answerable at an address, and there is a fee for the register entry, and the register is a public register, and it is public for ever."

"How much is the whole thing," said Hanna Stroud.

"Forty pounds," said Miss Slee, "all in, including engrossing, the stamp and the register entry, and I have done four in the last year and three of them cost the client more than that because the clients argued with me about the objects clause. Here is the breakdown."

*Advising and drafting the objects clause, £6 4s. Memorandum and articles, engrossed, £12. Stamp and registration, £18. Register entry and certificate, £3 16s. Total, £40.*

"Forty pounds," said Ivy Pike, from the wall, where she had been standing the whole time because there were six chairs and seven people and Ivy Pike is the chair of the Weirs tenant council and did not want to be the chair and got the chair anyway.

"It is also eight hundred shillings," said Miss Slee. "I mention that only because you will all have seen the figure before in a different sort of document, and if I say it I would rather say it than have somebody say it after I have gone."

She had the memorandum in a folder and she took it out and read the objects clause aloud, twice, in the flat voice of a person who has read a thing out loud to rooms that were not listening and has decided to go on doing it at the same speed.

*"To represent the pumping district of the Lower Weirs in all questions concerning the water supply of that district."*

Then she put it face down on the table and said, "I am going to say four things about that sentence and I am going to say all four, because a woman of thirty-one who stops at two is a woman who has been paid forty pounds to make a mistake, and I would not take the money."

"Wenna," said Hanna Stroud. "Go on."

"**First.** *All questions concerning the water supply* is wider than this room wants. If the clause stands as written then the company can be asked about a tariff, about a main, about a fire plug, about a stoppage, about anything at all that water touches, and the company must answer, and if it cannot answer then it must write back and say it cannot answer, and there are about nine hundred households in this district and there are not nine hundred people in it, and somebody will be answered by a letter from a company that was formed in a back room over a tobacconist's in March of this year by six people who were not all of the district."

"**Second.** A company is a body. A body answers. It is obliged to have a registered address and a director, and the director is a person, and the person's name goes on a public register for ever, and there is no way of taking a name off a register. I have never seen it done. I have seen people not look."

"**Third,** and this is the one I would like the room to argue with." She put her finger on the paper. "In about four years a room will be asking this company about the rate. Not about the water. About the rate — the three shillings in the pound on six thousand nine hundred and sixty pounds that has been in every printed minute since January. And when it does, the company will have to answer, and whatever it answers will be true, and it will be about the company, and nobody in that room will be able to get back to the six of us. A minute can be argued with. A minute is four lines and a date and anybody may stand up in a room and say it is wrong. A company is a door with a brass plate on it."

"And there is a fourth," said Ivy Pike, "and it is mine and it is not about the law."

"**Fourth.**" Miss Slee looked up. "A company cannot be asked about the water. The water is a pumping district's, and a pumping district is not a body, and there is no form in this city on which a pumping district can be asked anything by anybody, and that is the whole reason anybody in this room is sitting in it. So the one question the clause names is the one question the company would have no standing to be asked. Everything else it could be asked, and it can answer all of it, and none of it is the water." She put the pencil down flat on the list. "And there is a fifth objection which is not mine and is the only one in this room that cannot be answered with money, and it is Mrs Pruitt's, and she has not said it yet, and I am going to let her say it in her own time and not in mine."

The room was quiet for a while, and outside the tobacconist's went on selling tobacco to about nine people an hour, and the gas fire was still out.

"So it is a body that can be asked about the water," said Sel Kade, "and the reason it is a bad idea is that it would make this room the answer."

She had not said anything until then and she had not sat down.

"Go on," said Wenna Pruitt.

"I am the only person in this room who can be asked about the water," said Sel Kade, "and I have not been able to be asked about the water since the seventh of the first month, because I gave up the post and said so in nine lines, and I have no water and no hours and no standing under the water act and I am a person on a tenant council. If this company were formed, it could be asked about the water, and it would have to answer, and the answer would come from this table, and the table would be six people who are not entitled to answer it. I have spent nineteen years being the one person anybody could ask. I am not going to spend the next nineteen years being the reason nobody could ask anything."

"You are the only one of us who could sign it," said Hanna Stroud.

"I am," said Sel Kade. "A company needs a director and I am the only one at this table who lives in the district and is on a body. And that is precisely the reason I am not going to, and I would like it written down that the reason is not modesty, because it is not, and it is that the only instrument this district has ever built for itself is a right to refuse, and the instrument I am being asked to put my name to is a right to answer."

"A body that cannot be refused is not a body," said Ivy Pike. "It is a room. I have been in this district nineteen years and I would like there to be one thing in it that can be, and we built it in January with five clauses and nobody thanked anybody, and it is the only instrument in this building I have ever been proud of, and you are asking me to help make another one that cannot."

Hanna Stroud had been listening to all of it with her hands flat on the table, which is a thing she did and which is not the same thing as what Orrin's hands do and never has been.

"Forty pounds," she said at last. "That is the whole of what is wrong with this and I want it said plainly before anybody starts being clever. Forty pounds is not money. I have a key ring on this table's size in my pocket with about two hundred and eleven keys on it and I have never once used it to ask anybody anything. Forty pounds is nothing. My husband died in March and I could put it in a tin."

"Then it is a good price for a mistake," said Wenna Pruitt.

"It is the worst price there is," said Hanna Stroud, "and I have watched two bodies get formed in this district for good reasons and both of them are still here and neither of them is the reason any more. The first is a burial society and it is a burial society. The second is a water committee from 1931 and it is four men in a room in Northglass who do not come here. Both were forty pounds and a Saturday morning. A thing anybody can afford is a thing that gets done on a Tuesday by somebody who has not read the question, and there is nothing in the history of this district or of any district to suggest that the people who form things are the people who end up being represented by them."

"And the answer to that is a form," said Ivy Pike. "A form that says who may not. And I have been in this district nineteen years and there is not one, and the reason there is not one is that the last time anybody tried to make one there was a woman of forty-four who had to write a number on a piece of paper and give it to her mother, and we are not doing that again inside my lifetime if I can help it."

"So: no undertaking, then," said Verity Slee, taking her pencil out, and putting it down again without writing. "I will say this once and then I will stop, because it is not my room. A minute book is a list. Every entry in it is a date and a person and a street if the street is written down, and this district has a piece of paper four lines long with four signatures on it that says a list of addresses is not to be printed and is not to be copied and is not to be added to any other list and is not to be used to plan any journey, and the twenty-six households are not to be told. I do not know what is on that list and neither do three-quarters of the people in this room. If we form a company we will have a minute book, and in four years somebody will be asked to produce it, and there will be a list of addresses in it, and there is no form on which anybody can say those are not to be produced, because the only instrument anybody has ever written about that in this city is four lines and four people and a date, and none of the four is in this room."

"Get them," said Hanna Stroud.

"You cannot get them," said Wenna Pruitt. "You can go and ask, and asking is a knock, and we do not knock, and if we knock then somebody's answer is in a company minute book for ever, and the price of that is a thing we will not know for four years. So: no undertaking, and a minute book that nobody has ever promised to keep out of anybody's hands, and every single one of us in this room knowing that."

Miss Slee wrote the minute. It took her a while and she read it back twice before she read it aloud, which is a thing she said she had learned in 1949 from a man who took minutes for a friendly society and had watched one go into a court.

*"Present: H. Stroud, landlord, nine blocks. I. Pike, chair, tenant council. S. Kade, tenant council representative. W. Pruitt, organiser, not a member. Two further members of the district, not named on the ground that the minute is published. V. Slee, formation clerk, attending. O. Vale, not a member, attending, and having no standing in the room.*

*It was moved and carried that no company be formed. Grounds, as minuted: a company could be asked about the rate and not about the water; a company is a body that answers and the district has one instrument, which is a right to refuse, and it cannot be used here; no standing exists to constitute it; and no undertaking exists or can be taken about a minute book containing a list of addresses. The fee of £40 0s 0d was not incurred and is to be held against the day. No person thanked."*

"Against the day," said Miss Slee.

"Against the day," said Hanna Stroud, "when somebody comes with a form that says who may not. Because there will be one, and it will be about something else, and it will be in about four years, and I am not going to be dead before it and neither is Ivy."

"Read the last line back," said Wenna Pruitt.

"There is no last line."

"There is," said Wenna Pruitt. "It is the fourth ground. *No undertaking exists or can be taken about a minute book containing a list of addresses.* Read it back and say it slower, because that is the sentence somebody will read in four years and I want the room to have heard it said properly once."

Miss Slee read it back. And the six of them, and the man who was not a member and had no standing, and the seventh person with a pencil, sat in a room over a tobacconist's in Weir Lane on the Saturday the eleventh of the third month, at about eleven in the morning, and did nothing else at all, and were not thanked for it by anybody then or afterwards, and it is not the first time people in this district have got together with a document and it is the first time the document has cost them nothing and stopped everything at once, and neither of those is a thing anybody has thanked them for, and neither of those is going to be in the notice run.

On the Sunday the twelfth of the third month the forty pounds went into an envelope with the memorandum in draft in it, and the envelope went into the inside pocket of a coat on a hook behind a door in Bellweather Road, and it was there on the Monday and on the Tuesday and for a long time afterwards, and there is no form in this city on which anybody may be asked for it, and nobody asked.
