# Chapter 42 — A Number Anyone Can Check

The answer came on the Friday morning of the fifth of the fifth month, and it came in four lines on a printed card with a form number in the corner, and it took four hours off the question he had answered on the twenty-second of the fourth.

> *Answer 9/4. Question 6/4, put by the hall. The answer of the holder of record, received 24th ult.*
> *The hall notes the answer at length and does not find fault with it.*
> *The hall does not keep a record of hours for work that is not part of a certified shift, there being no such hours, there being no such shifts.*
> *The hall is unable to do anything further and the answer is entered on the holder's card.*

The eleven words in his sent answer were *that no person in the district was asked*, and the hall's reply to eleven words was the same four lines it has been sending out of that drawer since before he was apprenticed, about four times a year and six in a bad year, to any man who asks it anything.

A definition that is true cannot be beaten by producing more evidence. He had written that sentence in the grey book in the second month and had been complaining of it ever since, and the hall had now demonstrated it on him, on a Monday-to-Friday cycle, for nothing.

Della Marsh put the card on the counter face down and turned it over with one finger.

"It came in the second post," she said. "It is not a letter. There is no letter. It goes on the card at the front of your file and you may look at the card whenever you like, because the card is yours and the file is not."

"And the last paragraph," said Orrin Vale.

"The last paragraph is the same paragraph it has been since the year you were apprenticed, and it is the same paragraph it was in the nineteenth of the third month, and I did not write it this week." She was not looking at him. "Mr Vale, I have four hundred and something of those in that drawer over there, and about four a year go out, and I could tell you the sentence in a dark room. I do not want to be thanked for a sentence I did not write this morning."

The bottom third of the card, in the same type, which he had not read at the counter because he was reading for the sentence he had been waiting four months for:

> *The hall further notes that the recommendation at line 31 of the holder's work book, entered 16th ult., in which the holder states that the main will step when the night draw is restored and that the error will be about twice its present figure within a month, has proved correct. The hall records that it was not asked to test it, and that it could not have tested it, and that the holder was of no standing to have it tested.*

He read that three times.

He had put it in the recommendation column of a hall form in a shop on the sixteenth of the fourth month, of his own motion, with nobody in four districts able to refuse it, because there was nowhere else in the city to put a thing a man had worked out. The hall had now confirmed it, in print, with a number, and had observed that nobody had asked and nobody could have asked and he had no standing to have it asked, and all three of those are true, and he could not put any of them anywhere, and it is the only document in this city that has ever said he was right about anything in advance.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't," said Della Marsh, and this time he had not said it at all.

---

Ambrose had a permit-to-inspect in the works' own book, and it took him about nine minutes to produce it and about four seconds to say what it was for.

"I am not writing you a certificate and you are not getting one off me, and if you stand on that crossing and tell anybody in a good coat that the district works inspected the crossing, I will deny having had a conversation with you." He put the book down on the counter with a finger on the line. "This says a licensed maintainer is admitted to the Green Span hospital crossing on the eighth and ninth to take such measurements as he may name in advance, and that nothing he finds binds the works. There is a column for the measurements. You are to write them in it yourself, in your own hand, and not on a separate paper, and I am going to have a copy of that page in a month, and if the figures in your book and the figures on that page differ then we are going to have a morning about it and I will not enjoy it."

"What may I name in advance," said Orrin Vale.

"Anything you like," said Ambrose. "That is the entire content of the permission and it is why I would rather you asked. If I named the measurements then a man could come back with a different measurement."

He came. He brought the works' ladder himself and a lamp and a pair of gloves, and he stood on the parapet for the whole of both afternoons and read every figure aloud as it was called, and he wrote two of them and would not write the third, which was that the works' inspectorate was still looking for a man who had signed five lines out of a store in the fourth month and had not stopped, and that Ambrose had said at the time, and had asked for it to be minuted, that he had not taken the book out of the drawer and was not going to.

He came at ten on the Monday and again at ten on the Tuesday, and on the Tuesday he stayed.

---

The kit was a steel rule, a four-inch machinist's level, a straight edge, a plumb line, a tape, a lump of chalk, and a leather pad, and it went across the canal road in a basket with a lamp in it, and it is worth saying that this is the whole of the equipment and that it is what a man in this city would have had in about 1911, and that not one item of it is a guild instrument and none of it can be produced in a room by anybody who was not there.

You do not measure a drop by sighting. That is the first thing and it is the thing that a man who can see a thing is always wrong about. You take a datum that cannot move, you take the thing that has moved relative to it, and you get the angle between the two with a level, and you turn the level end for end and average the two readings, because a level that reads a twenty-second out when you turn it round is a level that is a twenty-second out and not the bridge.

The datum was already in the ironwork. The tramway's own masons cut a chisel line and two punch marks into the coping stone on the north side when the span was put up in 1911, one foot east of the third bracket, because you have to have somewhere to come back to in forty years and the masons of this city have always been more practical than anybody who has come after them. It is cut in stone. It is three inches long and a sixteenth of an inch deep and it has not moved, and a man with a straight edge and a level can come back to it in nine minutes, and the whole of what happened to that span in the last eleven years is written in the difference between the two ends of that line.

What he read, on the Monday, off the third bracket, was this.

> *Datum: setting-out line, north coping, two punch marks, cut 1911.*
> *At the third bracket, span against datum, steel rule and four-inch level, level reversed and averaged. **Fifteen thirty-seconds of an inch.** Read twice, at ten and at eleven, and both men read the same.*

And what the works' file said, from two years and three months ago, on the annual line survey, with the engineer's name on it, was this.

> *At the third bracket, span against parapet, three-eighths of an inch.*

Three-eighths is twelve thirty-seconds. He wrote the subtraction out in longhand on the coping in chalk, in a hand that a person on a towpath could read from a boat, and Bevin Sarr read it back to him, because a number that only one man can read is not a record and she had known that for eleven years and had been proving it with a painted board in a pump house since long before there was anything to prove.

> *Fifteen thirty-seconds now. Twelve thirty-seconds then. Difference, three thirty-seconds of an inch, in two years and three months.*
> *Which is a twenty-fourth of an inch a year.*
> *And every figure on this stone can be checked by a person with a rule and a level, in about nine minutes, and does not require anybody to believe anybody.*

And the load, which was the fourth line and was not arithmetic.

Two of the four fixings had been out of their castings for the better part of two years, which the survey already said, and he had the drift in and a lamp on them and he could see it. The third fixing was carrying the conduit alone and had been carrying it for about a year and a half, and the cast iron of the bracket at the fixing holes was grey and pitted and, where the pitting had gone deepest, it was a colour he knew from a different job four days before, because on the Wednesday of the fourth of the fifth month he had looked through a glass at ninety-eight teeth in a going barrel and counted a bright spot the width of a hair where the grease had gone and the tooth had touched steel.

Grey iron does not warn. It has no ductility left in it and it does not creep and it does not cry out; it goes from sound to a line of graphite flakes in the length of a breath and then it is in pieces, and the length of the breath is shorter every year the iron is loaded and the shorter it gets the more of the load goes onto the third fixing. That is the whole of the eleven years. A clock tells you a month before and a bridge tells you on the day, and there is no instrument in this city that makes a bridge say it early, and that is not a sentence anybody would put on a printed sheet.

---

The other half of it was a gauge, and the gauge was on the third floor of the Green Span hospital on the Wednesday morning, and Leda Vale took him up herself and would not leave him with it.

The hospital line carries its pressure along a conduit and the conduit crosses the canal on that trestle, and the district's working figure for the branch is forty to forty-two pounds, and a clock that has not got pressure is a clock that slips, and the third bracket had a weeping joint at the far end of the run because a conduit that has been carried on two fixings and then one for a year and a half has been sitting in a bed it does not fit, and a bed it does not fit leaks.

He read the hospital gauge at twenty past nine on the Wednesday and read it again at half past ten, with the machine in charge of a routine ward round, which is the condition the figure means, because a gauge read with the machine idle is a gauge read in a lie.

> *Green Span hospital, engineering, third floor. Line gauge, hospital end. **Thirty-four pounds.** Two readings, twenty minutes apart, machine running.*
> *District works gauge on the hospital branch, canal road, same morning, same instrument type, read by me at ten: **forty-one pounds.***

Seven pounds short at the far end of about a mile and a quarter of conduit, on a branch that is nominally a forty-to-forty-two-pound branch, and it has been short for eleven years, and the district works do not carry a defect against it, because a conduit that leaks at a joint is a joint, and a joint is a maintenance item, and the joint is two thousand yards away at the other end of a trestle and there is a line item against it at two pounds fourteen shillings and sixpence in programme year three.

The make-up is the part he could not measure and had to be given to him.

"They do not make that up out of thin air," said Leda. "Ellen Reeve can tell you what the district does when a branch is short, and I have asked her and she says it is pumped. She says the station runs the second stage up a bit harder and the ward's tanks fill a bit higher and everybody calls that a normal night."

"And whose water is it."

"Mr Vale." Leda had the gauge in front of her and she was not reading it; she had stopped reading it when he said the word *tank*. "It is the pumping ward's. It is all the pumping ward's. There is no other tank on this line."

That is the whole of it, and it took him about ninety seconds in a corridor and it is the fourth thing this book has been about since the fifteenth of the first month.

The hospital's clock pressure, the eleven minutes and forty seconds the ward loses, the medicine drawn off a clock that has not got its grip, and a hundred and forty thousand people at the bottom of a tidal basin: all of it is bought by pumping a little harder, out of one tank, in a ward that has been at under two days' reserve since the second of the third month, in a district whose canal front has been drawing four times a night since the seventeenth of the fourth, on a schedule made by nine men with a property qualification who were not able to consult the district and did not consult it.

And the tank has a gauge on it. And the tank has a low-level alarm on it. And the alarm was inhibited on the second of the third month, entered against a diversion note in the district works' own accountancy file, and under the works' own tables an inhibited reserve alarm requires a report, and no report has been made, and the inhibition is the only reason there is no number.

So there are two documents in the city that would show this and neither of them mentions the other: a diversion note that says the ward gave nine thousand gallons a day away from the second of the third month, and a permit-to-inspect book with a page in it in a licensed man's handwriting that says seven pounds has come off a branch that should have forty-two. Put them side by side and the tank is doing two jobs it was never sized for. They are in two buildings a mile apart and both are correct, and the man who wrote the second one has never seen the first, and the woman who signed the first has never been asked about the second, and neither of them is anybody's villain.

"I am going to write this down," he said.

"You are going to write down the seven pounds, which is yours, and you are going to write down the tank, which is not yours and is not mine and is the warden's, and if you put the warden's name under the tank on a piece of paper in a hospital then you have done to her what was done to her with a form in the storm month, and you have written your own rule about it and then broken it four months later." She had her hand flat on the gauge glass, which is not a thing to do to a gauge and which she did anyway. "Put the tank down as a tank. Say that it is the pumping ward's reserve and that it has been below two days since the second of the third month and that the low-level alarm on it has been inhibited since the same date and that no report has been made. Any person can read that and work out the rest in about four seconds, and none of it is a name, and Sel Kade can be asked about it in a room in front of four people, and that is the only way anybody gets to hear about it."

"That is not what I wanted," said Orrin Vale.

"No," said Leda Vale. "It never is. Say the number out loud in front of the people whose hours it is and let them be the ones who refuse you."

---

On the Thursday morning of the eleventh the glass began to fall, and the town got on with the forms.

There is a form for the weather. There is a form for the weather at the works, with a column for the state of the river and a column for the height of the tide and a column for the district, and it is a good form and it has been used every day for thirty years. There is a form for weather at the hospital, and one at the guild, and one at the Assembly, and the Assembly's has a heading for *districts affected*, which is a heading of two inches and a half that can take the name of a place and not the name of a person, and which is the only place in the civic record where a district is allowed to appear as though it were a party.

The forecast signal went up on Thursday morning for rain from the south-west, heavy, with a spring tide, on the fourteenth. Three days. Every office in the city has a form for that. Not one of them has a column for a district, and the Lower Weirs pumping district has no standing under the water act, and a column is not a thing a place can be given.

He did not go up the Hill. He went down to the canal instead, at half past two on the Thursday, and stood on the towpath under the trestle with the water four inches above a sleeper and coming up, and read the chalk on the coping stone again from underneath, which you cannot do, and then went on to the ward board outside the pump house and stood in front of the schedule notice with the clerk's sentence on it and thought, in the ordinary way a man thinks at half past two on a wet Thursday:

*There is one tank and it is in a ward four miles from here, and it is doing two jobs. One of the jobs is a hospital. The other one is the four draws a night that nine men with a property qualification gave us on the seventeenth of the fourth, and there is a gauge on that tank with its low-level alarm switched off, and there is one person in this city who can put a reserve where she wants it, and she is not on the rail, has never been on the rail, and cannot be on the rail, because a station on that rail takes a person of the district, and there is nobody in the district to put there, and there has not been for eighteen years, and the form that would change it costs two shillings a head and I have paid it.*

*So the machine cannot be asked about this. Which leaves the thing a man with a bar does at night, and I know exactly how that goes, because I have read about it four times in four months and I have got the whole of it in a grey book in a shop with a lease, and the only difference between the fourth time and the fifth is that on the fifth I would be the one doing it.*

*And there is one reserve and there are two districts, and there is a storm three days out, and not one form in this city has a column for a district.*

---

There are four places the number is now.

It is in the works' permit book on the canal road, in a licensed man's handwriting, in the column headed for measurements, signed and dated, and Ambrose will have a copy of that page in a month and will send it to the hospital's engineering department because that is what the works does with a copy of a page.

It is in the grey book, in longhand, with the two readings and the date and the arithmetic and the statement that the level was reversed and averaged, which is the only sentence in two thousand pages that a person cannot check for him.

It is in chalk on the coping stone of a canal crossing, where rain will take it in about nine days and where it will be legible to anybody standing on a towpath with a boat.

And it went onto the card in the nurses' room at Green Span on the Wednesday evening, under the eleven minutes and forty seconds and above the line that already says *no fault; line error; reported, no report form exists*, because Elsie Cobb asked for her name to be on it in front of fourteen people in the fourth month and nobody took it off, and Leda Vale wrote the new line and then read the card out in the nurses' room, all of it, including the name, before she put it up.

The seventh pound, at the hospital end of a mile and a quarter of conduit, on a branch that should read forty to forty-two.
The tank that makes it up, four miles away, on the pumping ward's reserve, under two days since the second of the third month.
The alarm on that tank, inhibited since the second of the third month, and no report.
The canal front, four draws a night since the seventeenth of the fourth, by order of a body of nine men who were not able to ask the district and did not ask it.

Four lines. Any person can read them. There is a number at the end of none of them.

Nobody was angry about it on the Friday. That was not a thing he had any right to expect and it is the thing that had been arranged for him: two hundred yards of coping stone, a hospital card, a column in a works' book, and a line in a book that nobody will ever open, and a district four miles away with eleven hundred people on it who would find out about it on the seventeenth of the fourth, from a notice nailed to a board, because there was nobody they could write to.
