## Volume 01, Batch 0001 — Chapters 1–10

**Fixed calendar established on the page** (batch 0001 must not contradict it): Chapter 1 is **Monday the 15th**; Chapter 2 the 16th; Chapters 3, 4 and 5 all the **17th** (the Reading is filed at ten past two and Perry Vance dies that night); Chapter 6 the 18th; Chapter 7 the 19th; Chapter 8 the 20th; Chapter 9 the night of the 20th into the 21st; Chapter 10 the 24th, one week after the death. The Cathedral certificate Orrin signs is dated **the 15th**, two days before the Reading. Orrin's class-three renewal is **about eight weeks** after the 15th and does not fall inside Volume 01.

## Chapter 1 — The Extra Beat

Orrin signs a Continuity Office hourline inspection authority left at his bench (Tobias Rill inspects, Halden Rusk's office appoints him), certifies the Cathedral's crown-case public clock for a clerk who will not take his name, and is refused nothing and offered no welcome. At the ropeworks a foreman takes a worker's minute and hands her an hour token, and Orrin says nothing because he is not her employer. He works a hospital ward clock, a school bell and a market scale, and in the Cathedral's clock — correct twice on his gauge — hears eight beats and then a ninth pulse arriving from below, like water returning to a pipe. He signs a clean certificate dated the 15th, tells nobody, and writes the ninth beat in his private grey ledger under a date.

## Chapter 2 — Non-Certified Standby

Orrin takes the anomaly to the guild hall hoping to file an ordinary defect note, and Gerren Hask never says no: he describes the fee, the letter, and the Quarter's reputation, and tells him the clock is right and that a building keeping its own accounts dislikes being told what it is carrying. Under Hask's elbow, open, the hall's labour book shows one line in a copying clerk's hand — *Weir Two, night pumping, non-certified standby, 1 crew, 3 nights* — and Orrin does not ask about it. Ivo Pell, a twelve-year operator at the licensing counter, calls a bench man's ear a trade habit, not an instrument, and leaves. On the wall is the Long Nine crew photograph, uncaptioned beyond the night and a shift number, with Edwin Vale somewhere in twenty-two faces and no one to say which. Hask produces a governor stopped mid-travel on purpose, with a bar's jaw marks on it, and asks Orrin to reduce it to unrecorded scrap; Orrin refuses to scrap a regulator with a cut pin and takes it home instead.

## Chapter 3 — The Hand Valve

Called out to Weir Two, Orrin finds the certified clock four minutes fast and a pump that is not failing: its stop logic assumes a hand valve is shut, and the valve is open because a man opened it in June and wrote nothing down. Bevin Sarr keeps the crew's own hours on a chalk board; the guild's line for that work is *non-certified standby*. Perry Vance, forty-one, twenty years on the nights, unlicensed and cheerfully unresentful, arrives, and Orrin tells him the stage will run past midnight if the head keeps rising. Vance goes down, shuts the valve in daylight, chalks *hand shut* on the board, and then points out that the board and the clock both say the valve was already shut, and the machine has been wrong about it for twenty years. The ninth beat arrives a second and last time through the pump house's own line, carrying a consequence rather than a fault. Vance mentions his daughter's six o'clock tram and warns him not to write the night shift in.

## Chapter 4 — No Unseen Cost

At the Question Hall, Orrin files as a private citizen, names the paying district — the Clockwork Quarter, the district with the most reserve and the least right to be charged — and watches the spindle reject a moral question with *QUESTION NOT ACCEPTED. NO NAMED ACT.* Ivo Pell takes the operator station, Ostrand the record station, and the affected-district station is filled by **Alma Festinger**, 56, of Green Span, on a public-health rota, because the Weirs have no standing; she signs because she was asked, wearing a witness bond she does not understand. The single Reading notice of the batch appears: the trace holds Ferrywright Bridge, the pressure says the second stage will not stop before midnight and the hospital's night clock slips with it, the blind margin reads *unregistered night work is not represented* in a footer half the size of the signature, and the toll is three district minutes and a witness interval. The countersignature carries **Anja Voss's** name and nothing else. On the Toll Stair the chalk reads *3 — Clockwork Quarter*, and nobody can see from the notice who paid.

## Chapter 5 — The Pump That Should Have Stopped

The afternoon after the Reading is empty and Orrin tries to find what the Loom left out by listening at his own bench, and can hear everything within reach and nothing at all about the omitted shift, because his sense is blind to whatever the network does not carry. At dusk at Weir Two the hand valve is open again, driven open by the opened gate and the running second stage, and there is no water record of when. He puts a hand on the stage, asks the question, and gets Aftershock for the first time: a weight leaning, a whole night, a man going down a ladder. He tells the watchman and the watchman is right to refuse him — no licence for that machine, no proposal, no shift time, no name, no number — and he sends nine coppers to the Hall with a boy and the Hall is shut. The crew comes on at seven, four of them, and nobody is told anything. Vance goes down on his own initiative to shut the valve the model said was shut, and the pump that should have stopped does not stop. At dawn Bevin Sarr tells him the valve was open and the stop never came, and the fifth name on her board is a boy who was two streets over, which is what let four look like a miscount instead of a removal. A clerk pins the Directorate's notice on the door: every word of it is true. Orrin's account of his own guilt is that he had the information and no instrument that turns information into an action somebody else can take; he is not the cause and is not let off.

## Chapter 6 — The One Minute He Cannot Account For

Teo Arden, twenty, Vance's apprentice, up since Tuesday, has the better account of the night and the correct suspicion, and tells Orrin flatly that three minutes is the price of the bridge. Orrin admits he filed the question and keeps back the ninth tick and the regulator. At the bench, writing the evening down, Orrin finds a minute with clean edges and nothing in the middle, produces two complete and incompatible accounts of it, and writes that he cannot say whether he read the stage or imagined the reading. In a Green Span corridor Leda Vale shows him a health slip recording a tram driver's lost interval as *calibration strain* with no name, no duration and no date, and names the guild's separate phrase, *self-inflicted occupational strain*, for a clockmaker who listens without a witness. They have the book's first real fight, both quoting their mother, and he still does not tell her about the tick. Perry Vance goes into the private ledger as the first person in it. On the six o'clock tram, Ada Vance, thirteen, asks him one question through a closed window and he answers it uselessly, and lets the tram take her round the corner. A clerk from the Office of Public Reckoning arrives at ten past six with a toll receipt and a question about a street clock in his own district.

## Chapter 7 — Three Minutes

Mara Venn of the Office of Public Reckoning audits the Toll Stair and nothing else, and meets Orrin at a street clock on Halloway Street that is five minutes slow where the file says three, which she attributes to a hand correction, the way everybody does. In her office she has his clean certificate dated the 15th, his filed question, and the receipt. The deepest resistance is that nothing he felt at Weir Two is a document: his feeling has no paper because the paper requires him to certify a district toll he has no licence to certify. She names the rule that lets a Reading be charged to any district in the city, files an objection on the receipt on the ground that the paying district was neither affected nor consenting, and it is accepted for filing, which changes nothing. Orrin gives her the one checkable fact — the blind margin's exact wording and its size against the signature — and she has measured it at eleven to the point against twenty-two. She quotes the rule about *calibration strain* in a tone with a whole room behind it and refuses to explain. Then she notices he cannot account for a full minute of a conversation he clearly had, and her reaction is professional fear, not scepticism. The Weir Two night roster goes upstairs and comes back in one courteous line: *referred to the sealed archive.* The Archive of Unlisted is named.

## Chapter 8 — The Form Number

Sindri Lake, keeper of the public Archive room, is helpful, precise and immovable, and shows Orrin that Weir Two's public record holds a maintenance line, a period, a quantity of hours and no names, because the record's purpose is the hours. The public index also records every transfer *out* of the open record, because a hole must be distinguishable from a lie. The Weir Two batch: transferred eighteen years ago, forty-one entries, on guild request form GH-9, standing withdrawal, maintenance-deferred class, countersigned for the district by the pumping warden of the Lower Weirs, deputy, **S. Kade** — a name Orrin has never heard. A guild price schedule prices the removal at two shillings the head for a year, which is what Perry Vance's night work cost the hall, and the routing card copies went to the hall, the ward, the Continuity Office inspection file, and a Green Span public-health rota, where the attendance line reads *FESTINGER, A.*, and nobody in the room connects it. Lake will not hold testimony. In Wenna Pruitt's kitchen over a chandler's shop, the tenant-council organiser who has kept the pumping district's shift book for eleven years states the case plainly: Orrin has the paper and not the person, and a district that produces the hours and cannot produce a signature is not going to be believed this century. She asks the question he cannot answer — whose name goes on a form that says there was no crew. On the way out the Weirs' clocks slip out of phase, the slip crosses the Green Span hospital line, and the only reserve that could absorb it is not there.

## Chapter 9 — What the Ward Can Absorb

The Weirs' clocks repeat and skip and the hospital line goes with them, and the cause is not a retroactive toll out of the Reading — three minutes and a witness interval is all that Reading charged — but the ordinary late arrival of a district that has been running twenty years on hours it does not have. Orrin states the three reasons there is no Reading tonight, out loud, in order, with Sel Kade and Teo and Mara in the room: a model of conditions that change by the minute, a witness rail booked a day ahead with no district resident to fill the third station, and a fee that would come out of the reserve that has failed. Sel Kade defends the pump reserve she moved to Carrow Street in the spring: seventy-one people, and it is true, and it is exactly the moral shape the book is about. Leda Vale brings the ward's arithmetic — forty minutes of slack, six hours of free-running ward clocks, two hours of it unbudgeted — and makes it a method condition: if her ward is going to be the number he spends, say so in a room before you spend it, in front of the people whose hours they are. Orrin names Aftershock aloud, a tape measure confirms him, Teo's crew and Marta Dey and Bevin Sarr wind and hold the rate all night, and a sacrificial plate goes on the bracket with a name on it because a ward that names its plate can find out later who owns it. The clocks hold; the trestle's failing load is exposed in daylight on the back of a shutter; a second, larger keeper's toll takes his right hand for two days and leaves the ringing in his right ear as a condition. Teo does not thank him and calls it an audit.

## Chapter 10 — The First Demand

Mara drafts a two-clause demand in the Office: first, that every Reading's toll be entered in the Assembly's record with the paying district named, because the Toll Stair is chalk scrubbed every morning and is not a record; second, that body tolls be recorded with a name and a duration, which is the Compact's fourth safeguard read literally and has never been done. She warns him that a published ledger will panic four Green Span wards, that some of the people in it did not ask, and she gives him the sentence that will kill it: *who pays for the record.* In the Assembly's public-health committee, Councilor Imani Rook blocks both clauses with real arithmetic — sixty-one calibration-strain entries in one committee's files in a year, scaled to the low thousands, and no estimate for the transcription — and Orrin has no answer and is not equal in the room: he has no seat, the affected district sent nobody, and the paying district sent nobody because why would it. The demand is referred to committee with a date in the spring, read twice, entered in the minutes, discharged. Alma Festinger is asked in the gallery by a clerk collecting names whether she understood what she signed at the rail, says no, means it, and has it written down; nobody follows it up. Sel Kade states on a public street that she moved the reserve in March, for seventy-one people, and would move it again. At the bench Orrin stops writing Vance as a shift and writes him as a man, copies the three minutes charged beside the five minutes the street clock was out, and writes that the two numbers do not agree and that he cannot say why. He writes the rule at the top of his own ledger: nothing of mine is used again without its blind margin and its toll named in public, in a room, to somebody who can object. The hall's reply to the Office's request for its labour ledger is courteous and entirely true, and ends the chapter with the form number written in a book nobody can read, and eight weeks.
