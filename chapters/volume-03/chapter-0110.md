# Chapter 110 — A List of Doors

On Tuesday the fourteenth of the second month Hale opened at ten with the rate card fresh and the trays full, and Orrin was there to see what the batch had been walking toward since the thirteenth of the first: a broker's book, and a list of streets that is a list of doors.

Hale's day book was on the counter, bound, ruled, a buyer's street in it beside every sale over two tokens, in his own hand, with the date. He did not hide it. A broker stands between a thing and a buyer and takes a cut, and in this city the cut is a percentage of minutes, and the arithmetic of the whole trade is checkable by anybody with a beam and the price list.

Orrin asked to see it and Hale showed it, because the book was not the undertaking: the twenty-six addresses in a kitchen had a four-line undertaking signed by four people on the fourteenth of the tenth, not to be printed, copied, added to any list, used to plan any journey, ever, no person to go to any door, the households not to be told — and the market's day book was also a list of addresses, and nobody had taken an undertaking about it, and that was the thread's return and not a new one.

The book ran eleven days since the refusal. On the tenth of the second month, eleven days after the council refused him in its own words, a token had gone into a clock on Sluice Row. Not by Hale's hand — by a householder's, bought on a Tuesday, carried home, spent into a mantel clock that was not the Quarter's, the minutes coming out of the Quarter's margin into the network. The entry gave the street and the door and the district letter, Q, and the price, four shillings and sixpence.

"A refusal binds the person who asked and nobody else," Hale said, reading the entry over Orrin's shoulder without apology. "Your council refused me. I have not asked the council anything since. The row may refuse the council and the council may not ask the row. I am neither. Eleven days later brass is in a clock on that row. Nothing in your right anticipates that and nothing in it is contradicted by it."

Orrin closed the book. "Whose minutes?"

"The clock's," Hale said. "The reserve meter does not know where the brass was cut. That is physics and physics is not licensed. I told you that on the seventeenth of last month."

"And the doors?"

"Are doors," Hale said. "A man of twenty says a list of addresses is a list of routes, and he is right, and a day book with a buyer's street in it is a list of doors, and I keep it because a broker who does not know where his brass goes is a man who will be robbed by Friday. Nobody has taken an undertaking about it. If anybody wants one, let them bring four lines and four people and a date, and I will read it."

Orrin walked to Sluice Row in the afternoon and stood at the top of the street without knocking, because the twenty-six households were not to be told and the twenty-two were not the twenty-six and that had been said in December rather than assumed, and knocking now would have been a second asking without seven days.

The row was quiet, eleven clocks, two standpipes, the brick cistern under it drawn down and refilled in December, five hours and three hundred minutes, hand-wound throughout, the row book on the ledge in the pumping ward with the timetable in it. A curtain moved in one house. A boy came round the side of another in a coat. Nobody asked him anything.

He went back to the bench and wrote the book in the grey ledger, doors not copied, streets named only as streets, because a number said in a room goes where the number goes and in December it had gone to the people the number was about, which was the one time anybody had wanted it to.

*Hale's day book seen 14th second, No. 14. Q100 into Sluice Row clock 11 days after refusal of 3rd answered 10th. Refusal binds asker only. Doors not copied. No undertaking taken. Thread return, not new.*

On Wednesday the fifteenth through Friday the seventeenth he worked ordinary bench work, beat two watches to the Quarter's public clock, the most accurate in the city, and wrote the rates on cards. The tank that week was about fourteen hours, exactly balanced, alarm inhibited since the second of the third month, no report made and no form requiring one, Corvin Tole writing the level on paper in his coat every afternoon since the second of the ninth, Mara knowing the figure on a printed four-line card, received and noted, not admissible. He did not go to the tank and did not ask for the sheet, because there was no form on which to ask and the volume would keep it so.

On Saturday the eighteenth he swept the step, locked up at noon, and sat at the bench with the grey ledger open at the week. The shop smelled of oil and cold iron. Outside, Verge Lane was quiet in the way a Quarter street is quiet on a Saturday afternoon, with the shutters half down and the public clock showing the hour to nobody in particular.

He turned the pages back to the thirteenth of the first month and read forward: a printer's clock and brass sent back loose, an arcade on a Tuesday, a drawer with nine punches, a beam that weighed free, a kitchen nine feet by eleven, a nurses' kitchen with four women in it, a letter with a date on it answered in the council's own words, a ward's forty minutes, a weight of one thousand eight hundred and sixty grains, and a day book with a buyer's street in it.

The letter from the Cathedral was still face down on the shelf. He turned it over, looked at the direction in her hand, and put it back face down. Tuesday would come round again, and the north door would be open or not, and he had not decided, and not deciding was also a thing a man did with his hands.

He wrote the last line of the week under the date, in ink.

*Hale's book seen. Refusal holds to the man. Brass in row clock eleven days after. Doors not copied.*

He put the pencil down, left the letter where it was, and went up to the room above the bathhouse to sleep.
