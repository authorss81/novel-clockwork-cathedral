# Chapter 63 — A Schedule of Houses, and the Man Who Made It

A pressure event on the Carrow Street branch on the night of Friday the twenty-second of the seventh month put the front's pressure at about thirty-four pounds instead of forty-one for about two hours, and the front's twenty-seven street clocks lost their reference for about two of them, and by about half past five in the morning on Saturday the twenty-third every one of them had been wound and set by hand, and **no two of them agreed with any other one.**

Before the event they were spread over about forty seconds among themselves. After it they were spread over six minutes and forty seconds.

---

It was a hot night and that is the whole of the meteorology. The twenty-second of the seventh was the fourth hot night running and the canal road had a dew on it at two in the morning that would not come off the iron.

At about half past nine that evening Marta Dey at the Weir Two pump house said one sentence to about four people and it went into the night book, and the sentence was that the tank was at its highest since March and the branch was warm and everything in the district was running at once and somebody was going to get up in the night.

**She was wrong about the night.** She was wrong by two hours and she was wrong about the reason, and being wrong about it is the most useful thing that woman has done for that book in four months, because it is on the page in her own hand at half past nine, and it is wrong, and **a district that has a wrong prediction in a book is a district that can be shown to be right one**, and there had not been one of those in this city before the first of the sixth.

The cause, found on the Saturday morning at about eleven by a man of thirty-one at the works' counter out of a defect return and a booking form, is this. A firm in Northglass booked a stopcock shut on the Carrow Street branch **from half past ten at night until half past eleven**, for a new service to four houses, on a form, in the ordinary way, by two men.

The stopcock is a clock-driven stopcock and it is on the branch, and every clock-driven thing on that branch is late by that end of the branch's own figure, and the Carrow Street branch's figure on the Friday morning was **one hour and forty-two minutes**, and the figure was in a grey book in a shop on a hill and in six lines of chalk on a board in a pump house, and the twenty-seven clocks on the front were two hours and nine minutes out at the best-placed of them that same morning, and the booking form does not have a line for it, and **nobody from that firm ever found out why their stopcock shut at twelve minutes past midnight when they had asked for half past ten, which is one hour and forty-two minutes, to the minute.**

So: the stopcock shut at about **twelve minutes past midnight** and released itself at about **twelve minutes past one**, and two men from Northglass arrived at twelve minutes past midnight, which is the time the clock gave them, and they shut the main themselves and put in a service, and it took them one hour and fifty minutes, and at about ten past two they came off the main and the branch was back to about forty by about half past two.

The branch lost pressure in about four minutes. The front went from about forty-one to about thirty-four. The pumping ward's tank dropped far enough, in about half an hour, that the branch lost its reference to the twenty-seven street clocks, and **a street clock that loses its reference free-runs**, and there are twenty-seven different clocks on that street made by four different makers between 1897 and 1941, and free-running for an hour does not mean the same thing in any two of them.

Marta Dey read the front gauge at about half past three, rang the pumping ward, and Bevin Sarr was at the pump house in nineteen minutes. Nobody was hurt. Nobody died. There is no fault in the district and there is a fault in nobody, and the new service to four houses is sound and is in use and cost nineteen pounds three shillings and fourpence.

**And the works' return for the twenty-second of the seventh says: *Carrow Street branch, stopcock shut for new service, four houses, 1 hr 50 min, no defect found.* And that is a true sentence, and it is the most expensive true sentence in the district's file this year, because it is true, and the reason a firm booked the stopcock for half past ten is in a book in a different building belonging to a different firm, and the reason the stopcock shut at twelve minutes past midnight is in a grey ledger in a shop on a hill, and the reason the front's twenty-seven clocks no longer agree with one another is on a board in a pump house.** **And no two of those three documents has ever been in the same building.**

---

**The front had already done it, and the front did it entirely without him.**

The front is the canal front from Bellweather Road to the Weir One gate, and it has **twenty-seven street clocks and four standpipes** on it, and on the night of the twenty-second every one of the twenty-seven lost its reference for about an hour because the branch that drives them lost pressure, and a street clock that loses its reference free-runs, and there are twenty-seven different clocks on that street made by four different makers between 1897 and 1941, and **free-running for an hour does not mean the same thing in any two of them.**

He got the figures at about eleven o'clock on the Saturday morning by going down the front with a watch and a notebook, and he wrote them down, and the spread is the fact.

> **Spread before: about 40 seconds, best to worst.**
> **Spread after: 6 min 40 s, best to worst.**
> **Best-placed clock: 2 h 09 min out. Worst-placed clock: 2 h 15 min 40 s out.**
> **Before the event the front agreed with itself to within forty seconds. It has not agreed with itself since. It agrees with itself now because six people wind it by hand at four different hours, and not otherwise.**

Bevin Sarr's board has a second set of figures on it by noon and the board is a door and a half high and he had to write small.

---

**And then the thing that made the night, which is that nobody needed him, and that the man who did not need him is the man who taught the front to do it.**

The schedule exists. It is on a folded sheet in a drawer in the pumping ward, and it was made in the fifth month of this year by going along the canal front and knocking on doors, and it is signed, and it is the first piece of paper in this district's history with an unlicensed man's name at the bottom of it, and the name at the bottom of it is **T. Arden, from the thirtieth ult., licensed: no**, and that is still what it says, and nobody has taken it off, and there is a line in the council's book that says anybody who wants the name off can come and take it off and it will be written down who and when, and nobody has.

**It is the same schedule.** Fourteen houses, twenty-seven clocks, four standpipes, and a time against each one, and the times are not a clerk's times; they are times somebody got by standing in a street at half past three in the morning in April and asking people when they are up, and the schedule has a column for the winder's own name and a column for the time taken, and there are twenty-seven entries in it and a person against every one.

At about one o'clock on the Saturday afternoon the sheet came out of the drawer and went into the works' book in **four-inch capitals**, and the yard was doing nothing, and about forty people watched a man of thirty at a counter write twenty-seven lines down in a column that is four inches high, and then nobody went back to work for about ten minutes.

Ambrose did it on his own. He is about thirty and he has been at that counter eleven years and he reads the ward's night book, which is not a record of the guild and not a record of the works, and which now runs to **nine pages for the fifth month and five pages for the sixth and this one for the seventh**, and he said one sentence about it at the counter on the Friday morning and then went back to work.

"There is a column in the works' maintenance schedule four inches high that says *maintained by*," he said, "and it has had a column that size since before I came, and in eleven years the only things in it have been pumps and bearings and a stopcock, because that is what the works maintains. Last night six men who are not the works maintained twenty-seven clocks for four hours, and if a man from the works does not maintain a thing it is not in the book, and last night the thing that was maintained was done by men who are not the works, and that is either the best argument I have ever seen for putting people in a maintenance schedule or it is the end of the meaning of the word, and I have not got time to work out which and I am going to put them in the book in the morning."

> *B. Sarr. A. Dench, known as Amos, full name and not an initial. R. Morrow, night porter, Green Row, hours as returned. T. Arden, from the thirtieth ult., licensed: no. T. Arden, from the twenty-second inst., twenty-seven clocks, four hours, licensed: no.*

The name is on the works' own line twice now and both times with the same three words after it, and the second one is not a reprimand, it is a **precedent**, and a young man who signs his own unlicensedness against a works' asset in a works' book has done the one thing in this district that no office in this city can take off him, because there is no form on which a district may ask a man to stop having been honest in a book.

**Nothing was put up in four places.** Orrin Vale suggested a sheet on four boards, because that is what he did in the fifth month and it worked, and Teo Arden said no.

"No," he said. "You put four sheets up in May and every one of them had a number on it that a man from the Quarter had measured, and it was the best thing anybody has done in this district this year, and it cost the district a night of a man's back and a rate in October. **This one is ours. Four walls, a bell, a list, and no office has to answer for it. If you put this in four places it stops being the front's and starts being a document, and a document is a thing you can send to a man in a good coat with a badge and a form, and the form does not need a name on it.**"

---

**Saturday the twenty-third, from about one in the afternoon to about half past five. Six people, four hours, and the cost has no column anywhere in this city, and it did not have one in the fourth month and it does not have one now.**

Bevin Sarr and Teo Arden and Alder and Amos Dench and two of the men off the Weir Two night side. Two went one way down the front and four went the other. The list was out of the drawer at about one o'clock and the last clock was done at about half past five, and they worked to a schedule that had been made house by house in April and that gave a time for each clock to within a few minutes, and Bevin Sarr's own figures on the sheet held to within about a minute of the times.

**Nobody thanked them.** There is a rule in this district, written by somebody, in a clause, that a finding shall carry no thanks, and the rule has got itself a habit, and by the seventh month the habit holds without the rule.

**And four hours of six people's night is four hours of six people who were going to be awake anyway, and that is the sentence the district tells itself, and it is the sentence that a rate is made on.**

The water: about two thousand gallons off the front's storage, which is half a day, and the works' own tables say the front can be down to a day before anybody has to do anything on a hot night, and **nobody wrote the two thousand gallons down anywhere, and there is no column in the warden's return for gallons a district has decided to spend so that its clocks are not four different times.**

And the eighty-one.

**Alden Fenwick, 81, top floor, 14 Ferrywright Row, four flights, and the light in the fanlight at the top of that stair has not worked since about 1948.**

---

The house is on a private spur cut off from the district supply in 1923. The spur has a rising main up the back of it and there is a stopcock at the foot of that main in the lane, and the spur is off the district main above the Carrow Street stopcock, and **when the branch went down to about thirty-four at about ten past midnight, the two ground-floor taps at number fourteen went dead with it.**

He is the man whose cellar Orrin Vale went down into on the eighteenth of the sixth month with a lamp and a bucket and Ivo Pell standing on the top step for forty minutes, and it is item one on a folded piece of paper in a young man's pocket, and the cellar under that house has been under about four feet of standing water since the twelfth of the first month.

He waited until twenty to three in the morning, because the last time he reported a thing like this, in 1951, he was told it was a matter for the landlord, and because he is eighty-one and it is the fourth hot night running and a man does not walk down four flights at one in the morning for a tap.

At about twenty to three he came down. He came down with a lamp. The water came back on at about ten past four, and the water was on when he got to the bottom of that stair, and he went back up, and it took him about half an hour each way, and he was eighty-one, and it was dark, and there is no light in the fanlight.

The schedule had the ground floor of number fourteen at **02.40**, and the man who lets the top room went up at about ten to three and knocked at the top-room door and got no answer and wrote *no answer* on the sheet in his own hand, which is a thing that is in no column of anything.

The works' return for the twenty-second of the seventh says *no defect found*, and **the eighty-one-year-old man on the top floor of number fourteen is not in it, and the four flights are not in it, and the lamp is not in it**, and the lamp is the same lamp that a man of sixty-eight carried up and down about two hundred times in a cellar in the sixth month.

Nothing was done about the fanlight. It is not in the programme and it is not in the works' schedule and it is a landlord's light in a house of four flats, and there is no form on which a woman of fifty-four may be asked about a light.
---

**And Ada Vance came out to look at it, and nobody asked her one question, and that was arranged before she came down.**

She is thirteen and she lives over the second court and the second court is on the front, and a woman from the council's rooms went up at about eight in the morning and told her aunt what was happening and said that nobody was going to ask her anything.

"She can come down and stand at the end of the row and she can go home whenever she likes, and nobody is going to ask her a single question, and if anybody does she is to come and tell me and I will deal with it and nobody will be told she told me."

She came down at about ten past eight and stood at the end of the row by the second court's wall for about three hours with her hands in her sleeves, and she watched six people go up and down a street with a bell and a list, and she watched a clock on a bracket taken off its bracket and carried indoors and brought out again, and she watched four men go up to the top floor of number fourteen and knock, and come down, and write something on a piece of paper, and go up again.

Nobody asked her anything. Nobody has asked her anything since the twenty-first of the sixth, when a keeper of a public archive asked her three questions slowly and then stopped, and there are rules about that in three buildings and **all three of those rules were written by men who were frightened of a child, and the child has never once been told that they were written for her.**

At about eleven she asked one question. It was not to anybody; it was to the man who had come back down the stairs, and it was whether the top floor of number fourteen had a light anywhere on the stair, and he said there was not one since about 1948, and she said *oh*, and went and stood at the end of the row again.

---

**Orrin Vale was there for about two hours and he was not needed at all, and there is no way to dress that up.**

He had the notebook. He had two watches. He had walked the front at about eleven in the morning and got the spread and written it down, and on the Saturday afternoon he was one of about forty people standing in the middle of Bellweather Road watching a list of times work, and at about half past two he went to find a thing to do, and there was nothing to do.

He went to the works' yard at about three and got a very old inspection lamp out of a cupboard and carried it up four flights to the top floor of number fourteen and set it on the landing, and then he carried four gallons of water up as well, and then he went down again, because there was nothing else he was needed for and the four flights took about nine minutes each way and his right hand closed mostly on the way up.

And then at about half past four he went and stood on the canal road with his notebook open and could not think of one thing on that page that anybody wanted.

**A professional's work being taken away from him by being done properly is the best thing that has ever happened to him and he could not stand it for about two hours.** He is not a man who is unused to being unnecessary. He has been on a bench in Verge Lane for eleven years and he knows precisely what it is to be the only person in a room who can do a thing, and the thing he has wanted for five months is to be in a room where somebody needed the thing he could do, and what he has got is a street where six people did it, out of a list, made by a man of twenty, in April, by knocking on doors.

He was not proud of Teo Arden. He was not jealous of him either, or not only. What he felt, standing on the canal road at half past four in the afternoon on the twenty-third of the seventh month with a grey book and no licence, was that **a method he had watched a room invent had been written down by a man who is not him, made better by people he had argued with, and put into a printed schedule by the works, and it would go on working after every one of them had stopped.**

And that is a thing a man wants and does not know he wants, and it is the whole of what this year has been for him, and it is a restraint and not a power, and it cost him about two hours and he wrote it down on the Sunday night in four lines and did not expand it.

> *Twenty-second to the twenty-third of the seventh. The front, the night draw, four hours, six people, twenty-seven clocks, four standpipes, fourteen houses, one list, one bell, and about two thousand gallons off the front's storage that is in nobody's column.*
> *I measured the spread before and after and the spread went from forty seconds to six minutes and forty seconds and the whole district now agrees with itself because it is wound by hand at four different hours, and the reason it is wound by hand is a document made in April by a man of twenty who went along the front and knocked on doors.*
> *And a man of eighty-one went down four flights in the dark at twenty to three with a lamp and found the water on and went back up, and he is not in the return, and the four flights are not in the return, and the lamp is not in the return, and the return says no defect found, and it is a true sentence.*
> *I was there for two hours and I was not needed, and I carried a lamp and four gallons of water up four flights and then came back down, and that is the correct outcome and it is the best possible outcome, and I have written it down in four lines and I am not going to write a fifth one, because a man who explains a good feeling to himself in a ledger has already decided the feeling was about him.*

---

**And on the Saturday afternoon Teo Arden, who had no reason to and who has never in his life claimed any of this, did the one thing in the week that is his.**

He went into the works' yard at about two o'clock and asked Ambrose, at the counter, in front of nobody, for the winder's own schedule — not a copy, the sheet — and then asked for it to be entered in the works' book in four-inch capitals with the times and the houses and the twenty-seven and the four hours and **his own name twice and *licensed: no* against both**, and Ambrose entered it, and did it in about four minutes, and did not ask him why, and did not thank him.

"You have not asked me for anything," said Ambrose.

"No," said Teo Arden.

"Then I will tell you the price and you will not be able to pay it," said Ambrose. "**Nobody in this yard has maintained anything that was not a pump in eleven years, and there has not been a line in that column with a person in it since before I came. You have just put twenty-seven of them in in four-inch capitals with the times and the houses and your own name twice, and I did it in four minutes and nobody in the yard has been paid for it and nobody in the yard is going to be. I am not going to be thanked and if I am I shall rub them out, and I want it minuted that I said that, and there is nobody to minute it, and I have said it anyway.**"
