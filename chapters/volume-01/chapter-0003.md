# Chapter 3 — The Hand Valve

The Weirs smelled like the tide had come up and gone down twice in one day and left the smell behind in the brick.

Orrin came off the early tram with his case and his coat into a morning with no light in it worth the name, and the first thing he saw, from the top of the slipway, was that Weir Two was already running. The second stage, which should have been shut for the ebb at three the day before and had been kept running ever since, was turning over with the flat unhurried sound of a machine doing a proper job, and the water in the forebay was up to the fourth of the white marks on the wall.

*Head rising hard,* the tide clerk had said on the speaking tube. *Crest late tomorrow afternoon. Wet all day tomorrow. Keep it shut and it will want opening; open it and it will want shutting. Your problem, not mine.*

The pump house sat at the top of the slipway like a boot left in the mud, one storey, brick, tarred up to the eaves, with a stove pipe and a lamp bracket and a bell that had been taken down at some point in living memory. The lamp bracket had a hood on it and no lamp. The door was propped with a bootlace.

Inside it smelled of hot oil, wet wool, tea that had been on the boil since morning, and the particular sourness of a handrail that four men hold every shift for twenty years.

A woman of about thirty with a board-hook in her hand and a lamp in the other looked at him and said, "You're the clock man," in a voice that made it a category.

"Orrin Vale. Guild hall sent me. Certified clock's four minutes fast."

"It's four minutes fast," she agreed, "and you're the third man to say so."

She put the lamp down. "Bevin. Sarr. Second on the night crew. If you want the truth about that clock, nobody here is going to stop you, so you may as well have it first and be spared the afternoon." She jerked her chin at the far wall. "That's the board."

The board was a slab of painted board about four feet by three, hung on the wall where the old plan used to hang, and it was covered in chalk. Rows and columns of figures in two hands, most of them a careful block capitals, some of them a scrawl. Hours. Dates. Names in the left-hand column, abbreviated, with hours against them, and along the top a line in different chalk that said something like: *board is ours. board is not the works'.*

"That's your shift record," Orrin said.

"That's our hours." Bevin Sarr set the hook against the wall. "The works has a book. The book says what was certified. The book does not say we were here, because the book does not have a column for us, and there is no form number in this city you could give anybody for a form that says *these five men kept the district's water out of their own kitchens for twenty years.*"

He looked at the board for a while. Then he said, "The guild line for that is non-certified standby."

"Yes." She said it flatly, without weight, the way a woman says a word she has said a great many times. "Work that keeps a machine running and isn't part of a certified shift. So it isn't hours, it's a courtesy. So if a machine breaks, no man was on it, and if a man is missing, he wasn't working." She picked the lamp up again. "Your clock's four minutes fast because somebody pinned its stop lever so it would let go before the crew were out of the water in a bad night. Eleven years ago. Pinned by a man who is dead and who was right to do it, and it has been four minutes fast ever since, and if you take that pin out the next high water will come into this room while three men are in it."

Orrin looked at the clock. It was a wall clock, a good one, an hour case, of the kind a ropeworks or a market would have bought, and the guild mark on it was a plate rather than a stamp, which meant somebody had renewed it by hand for years without ever looking at the movement.

"How many is your crew?"

"Five on nights. Four is the works' number. Four is what the book has." She said it the way you say *it's twelve o'clock.* "You want to know which five, and I could tell you, and it wouldn't be a record, would it."

"No," he said. "It would be a man telling me."

---

The pump was not broken. That was the first thing he had to unlearn, and it took him until about two in the afternoon to admit it.

He had a theory inside two hours and it was a good theory. The clock was four minutes fast, the stop logic ran off a movement in a grey box on the wall, and the whole case went round on the same line, so a fast clock would call the stage's stop four minutes early or the start four minutes late, and somewhere in that arithmetic lay a fault that would show as a stutter at the changeover. He went over the logic box with a lamp and a pencil and found the movement inside it eleven years older than the clock and never certified by anybody, and he took it out and cleaned it and adjusted it to the clock's error and then to nothing at all, and he set the whole case by the water gauge in the forebay instead of by the clock, which is the correct way to set a stop in a rising head and which takes four hours and makes a man feel useful.

The stutter stayed.

At ten past eleven he was down on his knees with his cheek to the frame of the second stage, and he understood that the pump was doing exactly what it had been told, and that what it had been told was correct, and that the correct instruction contained a fact that nobody in the building had entered anywhere.

The instruction was: shut the hand valve before you start the stage.

And the hand valve was open, because a man had opened it in the dark in June to keep a cellar from filling, and had written nothing down, because the form for that is the same form as everything else here and the form is a courtesy.

He heard it in the pipe. He heard it in the frame. And then, before eleven, with his hand flat on the second stage's case and the water climbing the white marks, the ninth beat came up through the floor.

It was the same shove he had felt in the crown case at the Cathedral, the low round knock of water finding a pipe again, and it belonged to no part of this room any more than it had belonged to that one. Here it was not a noise under a machine. It came in and it did not stop, and it went on past the tick and leaned, the way a weight leans on a scale that has already decided, and what it leaned on was not the stage under his hand and not the water climbing the wall but somewhere else, and heavier there than it was here, and there is no instrument in Morrowfen that will tell a man where.

It gave him nothing else. No night in it, no picture of anything, and not one word, and it lasted about four seconds, and after it there was nothing in him that felt used.

Then it went, and the pump turned over, and the room was a room again.

He sat down on the step outside with his back to the wall and his ears full of the room's noise, and understood that he had it twice in two days, once in a cathedral and once in a pump house, under two machines that were each doing their job perfectly, and that both times it had been about a place and not about a machine, and that the first time he could have written a fault and did not, and that the second time there was nothing at all to write.

---

Perry Vance came in at half past eleven with a lamp he did not need, because the lamp was part of how he moved through a building.

He was forty-one and looked fifty on the eyes, and he had been on Weir Two nights for twenty years, and he was unlicensed, which he mentioned in the first four minutes without being asked, in the tone of a man who has found that getting it in early saves trouble.

"You're the crown case man," Vance said. "Certify a bell for the Cathedral. Heard you did that Monday."

"I did."

"D'you hear anything in it?"

Orrin looked up. The man was not smiling. He had a broad flat face, wet to the eyebrow, and hands that were red to the second joint, and he was standing in his own pump house at half past eleven in the morning and asking a stranger a direct question.

"No," Orrin said.

"Good." Vance hung the lamp on a nail. "Course you don't. Nobody hears it and they say they do, mostly, so as somebody'll put it in a book." He crouched and put his hand flat on the second stage's bedplate the way Orrin had, and easy, and without ceremony, the way you touch a horse. "This is going to run past midnight, and it isn't going to want to stop, and there'll be nobody in the building after four because the book says four, and four is me and Bevin and the Fenn boy and Amos, and then a boy called Teo comes along because he is twenty and thinks a bar is a kind of spanner, and he's my fault, he's learning. Five in the room. Four on the book. That is the whole arrangement and it has been the whole arrangement for twenty years."

"Learning where?"

"Here. He doesn't have a ticket. There's four of us without tickets on this crew and there's nine of us without tickets in the district, and none of us are getting one, because there is no form for a man to be a tuner." He said this without any bitterness at all, the way you describe the weather on a good day. "The Arden boy'll be at the market tomorrow and half the machines in the lower lanes are going to be somebody's problem by Sunday. Fine. He can learn off me and I'll teach him the hand work, because the hand work is the only thing any of us is actually certified at, in the sense that anybody means it."

"The clock," Orrin said. "The fast one."

"Leave it." Vance said it without emphasis, the way you would tell a man not to lean on a rail. "It's four fast and it's the safest thing in this building. D'you know what your machine up the hill thinks happened at three in the morning?"

"What did it think?"

"Nothing happened." Vance straightened up, and for the first time there was something in his face that was not mild. "That's what I wanted. The stop comes at the right minute and the stage runs to the right count and there's no record anywhere of me and Bevin and two others standing in eight inches of water at three in the morning shutting a valve by hand. That's the product. The clock is the product." He put his thumb on the plate of the stage. "You've come down here to find a fault. There isn't one. There's a valve that's open and no instrument on it, and there are going to be four men on shift tonight and a fifth name on the board, and the book pays for the four, and the head is going to crest tomorrow afternoon, and if this thing runs past midnight with that valve open it'll drive the return up through the bypass and put water in Ferrywright under the bridge deck, and then in the morning the works will have a hole and the book will have no man in it."

Ferrywright. Orrin knew the name, the way everyone did, from the posters and the trams and the school stories: the low iron road bridge with the tram wires over it that everybody in the Weirs used and nobody looked at.

"You've had this before," he said.

"Every autumn for twenty years," said Vance. "And if you want a name for it, it's a bridge and a gate and a piece of river, and the machines get the arithmetic and I get the wet feet." He went to the door and put his head out into the rain to look at the forebay. "Ada's tram's at six. I want to be up the stairs by half four to be standing on the platform with her, and if you want a man to shut a thing by hand tonight, I'm the man. I'm on at seven. It's not in the book, and I'd thank you not to write it in."

Orrin had the water-gauge in his hand. He looked at the marks, and the marks were the water, and the water had a shape.

"It'll run past midnight if it keeps rising," he said. "And you should shut that hand valve now, in daylight, and put a mark on the spindle so anybody can see it shut."

Vance did not argue with him about the machine. He went and got a bar, and he went down, and Orrin stood at the top of the ladder and listened to a big man in bad boots going down iron rungs into moving water, and the noise of it was enormous and unhurried, and then the noise of a valve being closed properly, which is not a bang but a long complaint and then a silence.

Vance came up. Water to the knee, hair flat to the skull, breathing like a man who had carried something.

"That'll hold till the crest passes," he said. "Now you've got your valve."

He went to the board and took the chalk and wrote on it, in his own line, at the bottom of the day's column, the two words that were the whole of his working life: `hand shut`.

Orrin said, "The board says it was already shut."

Vance looked at the board. Then he looked at the valve spindle, and at the white paint he had put on it, and then at Orrin, and the mildness went out of his face and something colder and much older came in, and he said, in an entirely ordinary voice:

"And the book says the crew's four, and the board says five, and the clock says the valve was shut, and the machine up the hill has spent two hundred years' worth of somebody's arithmetic working out which of us is the least expensive place to be wrong. Ain't it."

He put the chalk down and wiped his hands on his coat.

"Twenty years I've been closing that valve in the dark before it needs closing," he said. "Two years I've been not closing it before it needs closing, because there was no need, because the head was low and the year was kind. And you have come down here this afternoon and told me to shut it, and I have shut it, and the clock up the hill still says shut, and the clock up the hill has said shut every single night for twenty years while I was down there in it with my hands."

He looked at the painted spindle.

"So when it opens again — and it will open again, that head's coming — nothing in this city is going to know it but you and me and a piece of chalk."

Then he picked up his lamp, and said that if Orrin wanted to know anything else he could ask him tonight, four to eight, and after eight he would be on the platform at half four in the morning waiting for the six o'clock tram with his daughter, and by God he would be there whatever the water did.

---

Orrin walked back up the slipway in the rain with the gauge in his case and the shove still under his ribs, and the thing he carried out of the Weirs was not a fault and not a fear.

It was this: that there was one machine in Morrowfen which could sit a bridge and a gate and a piece of rising river side by side and say out loud what would happen if a man pulled a handle now, and that it was eleven miles away and it was open to anybody, and that he had never in his life walked into it, and that there was a form somewhere for walking in.

He got as far as the top of the slipway before he understood that the form was the problem and not the walking in.

Nobody in the Weirs had one either. Vance had said, four hours earlier, without any particular weight, that there was no form in Morrowfen for a man to be a tuner.
