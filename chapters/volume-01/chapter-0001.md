# Chapter 1 — The Extra Beat

The form arrived at the bench at ten past five, before the light, laid under the brass weight Orrin used for keeping flat work from curling. It was a good weight — a rolled bearing in a felt socket, and he had never once been able to explain to a customer why the thing that held paper down was also the thing he trusted with gears.

He read it standing up, in the cold, with his coat still buttoned.

*Continuity Office, regional water and power scheduling — inspection of civic hourlines, Clockwork Quarter.* The right-hand column gave a name and a title: **Tobias Rill, Inspector of Hourlines**. Beneath that, in the line that stated the authority, a second name in the same hand: **Halden Rusk, Continuity Minister**. Orrin read it twice, because the important thing about the form was not who was going to inspect him but who had appointed the man who was, and the form put both in the same ink and asked him to initial the box at the bottom without asking him anything.

Four years. That was how long the Office had had the right to look at an hourline, and in four years Morrowfen's clockmakers had learned a new reflex: that a stranger on the step with a form and a question about hourlines was weather, like rain off the canal.

He signed the box. He put the form in the out-tray with the certificates, because a maintainer's licence, class three, might service and certify a single civic machine and nothing else, and might not file a district proposal and might not witness a Reading, and the fine print of that licence said that a licensed man who failed to give an inspector his co-operation at a reasonable hour would be answering to the guild before the year was out. Hask would enjoy that conversation. Hask enjoyed all of them.

The bench was two benches, a vice, a wall of lathes that had belonged to a man called Petrossian, a lease, a sign with ORRIN VALE, LINE HOROLOGIST on it, and the smell of oil and hot brass that Orrin had stopped noticing around the age of nineteen. His private ledger was on the shelf behind him, the grey one, the one that was not the shop's. He looked at it for a moment, decided it could wait, and put the kettle on.

Rain on the skylight, flat and grey, the first real rain of the season. The Cathedral bells went over the roofs at five, six, seven, unhurried, the best time in the city, and if a man set his watch by them he would be right for years.

---

The clerk on Cathedral Rise gave him a counter and a stool.

That was the whole of it. He said his name and she wrote nothing, and he said it again with the shop's name attached, *Vale's Bench, Verge Lane, class three*, and she gave him a second look that went over him like a broom and found nothing worth sweeping, and set a numbered tag in front of him instead. A numbered tag. His name would have been four seconds of work.

Above the counter, cut into the lintel in letters a foot high, the Directive's motto:

*NO UNSEEN COST.*

The public clock of the Cathedral of Consequence stood above her in a glazed case at head height on a stone bracket, because a man filing a paper ought to be able to see the machine that was being asked to speak. It was a good clock. It was a very good clock, and Orrin had known it for eleven years.

He set out his case on the counter: the timing gauge in its felt cradle, the drive weight he always brought and never needed, the small brass spanner he used to seat a regulator, the chamois, the certificate forms, the stamp pad. He could have done it blind with his knees.

The clerk asked what he was here for. He said certification, public clock, crown case, and she said the crown case was in March and could not be re-opened, and he said it was a daily certification, which was true, and she said the fee was three crowns and a quarter, and he said the fee was two crowns and six, which was also true, and she said the fee was three and a quarter, and Orrin looked at the clock above her head while he counted out the money, because looking at the clock was the polite version of not looking at the clerk, and because the clock was what he was here for.

He put the gauge on the case bracket and clamped it to the movement's input wheel, and he listened.

The Cathedral's public clock ran on a weight through a going train into a det escapement that Petrossian's grandfather had overhauled before Orrin was born. He knew its voice. It had a small dry click where the third wheel tooth entered, and a faint breath on the down-beat of the escapement, and under both of those, the sound of the frame itself, which is not silence but a kind of held-up noise that any wood or brass will make if you ask it the same question every day for eleven years.

He counted. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight.

Then, arriving through the bracket and up through the bench he was sitting on and into his jaw, a ninth pulse.

It was not a tick. A tick has edges. This was lower and rounder and it came from underneath the floor of the case, and under his stool, and up the leg of the counter, and it had the wet sound that water makes when it comes back into a pipe it has been out of: a shove, a knock, a settling.

Orrin did not move. He kept his ear where it was and let it come again.

Eight, and then the shove from below, and then nothing at all, and then the next second.

He took his hand off the bracket and put his ear against the case itself, the way he would put his cheek to a cold wall in a house to feel where the draught was. The case was cold. The frame was stone and the movement was brass and the wall behind it was two hundred years old and full of other people's masonry, and there was nothing in it that had any business being nine.

He ran the gauge. He ran it twice. The clock was gaining a tenth of a second in the hour, which was a tenth of a second better than the tolerance allowed, and the going train was clean, and the escapement was clean, and the weight was exactly as far down as it should be for this hour of the day.

The clock was correct. He could have certified it before he had opened the case. He took the case apart anyway, because he had opened it anyway, and looked at the pallet fork, the banking pins, the crutch, the little brass plate he had replaced himself four winters ago. Everything that could make a ninth sound in a clock of this make was present and accounted for, and none of it was making one.

He sat with it for the better part of twenty minutes and the clock struck eight, and the shove came with eight, and the shove was not in the clock.

The clerk said, without looking up, that if he was finished with the consultation, the next person was waiting.

He filled in the certificate. *Public clock, crown case, Cathedral Rise.* Condition: within tolerance, no defect observed, no recommendation. Fee two crowns and six. Date. His name, spelled out, and the number of his class-three licence, and the date of that licence's issue, because the form wanted it and because he had never once known a form to be stupid on purpose.

It was a clean certificate. He was proud of the handwriting, which was his father's handwriting, learned at a bench that had not been this one. He stamped it. He did not add a line about the ninth sound, because there was no defect to report and a note that says *I heard something and cannot explain it* is a note that makes a man look like his grandfather.

He walked out past the woman at the counter with the numbered tag still in front of it, and the fee receipt warm in his hand, and the taste of the ninth beat still somewhere behind his teeth, and the clerk already calling the next name.

---

The ropeworks on Verge Lane kept a work clock by the gate, and the clock kept the shift, and the shift kept the money, and the three of them were a closed system that Orrin got eleven crowns a month to keep honest.

The clock was right. He had been certifying it for four years and it had never needed anything.

The foreman was a heavy man called Ombersley who believed in a first cup of tea as a moral instrument, and who had a way of saying *rounding* in a voice that closed the subject. He was saying it now to a woman called Miss Trask at the gate bench, and her shift card was on the counter, and her face was doing the thing faces do when a minute has been taken and cannot be given back.

"Nine minutes late in, one minute out of tolerance on the gate clock, so the quarter hour's a quarter hour," Ombersley said. "Rounding."

"A minute is a minute."

"A minute is a fraction of a quarter, Miss Trask, and I'm not going to have the yard running on arguments about fractions." He turned to the drawer and took out a small brass disc and set it on the counter, and Orrin, who had seen a hundred of them, felt the small hot flush of shame on somebody else's behalf arrive under his own breastbone. A quarter hour of a pound, cut into the edge of the token. The number stamped in the middle, and the district, and the workshop's little mark. Miss Trask picked it up. A token is not money. A token is a record, punched, of minutes owed by an employer to a person, and it is worth exactly nothing until somebody with authority countersigns it, and the only people who countersign tokens are the people who take the minutes.

"Thank you, Mr Ombersley," she said. She put the token in her coat and left. She was not in a hurry. That was the part that stayed with him all day: she was not angry, she had been angry earlier and had got over it somewhere between the gate and the yard.

Orrin serviced the work clock for fifty minutes, cleaned the gate lever, adjusted the stop, wrote his slip, and said nothing about the minute, because he was not the man's employer, and because saying it would have cost Miss Trask her hours and given her nothing to eat except being right.

He thought about it for the rest of the afternoon, at the hospital, at the school, at the market, and he thought about it the way a man thinks about a tooth.

Green Span's hospital kept three clocks and would not be argued with about any of them. The public clock in the porter's hall, the staff clock in the corridor outside the nurses' office, and a small ward clock in each ward, on the wall by the door, which the ward was responsible for and which nobody outside the hospital was ever asked about. Orrin had the ward clocks in a rota that took him to Green Span twice a month, and he had long ago made his peace with the fact that the last hour of any ward clock's day was attended by somebody who would rather be anywhere else.

He did the corridor staff clock. The ward clock was already out when he got there, four minutes fast, and the sister on duty told him the whole history of it, which was that it was fast, that it had been fast since the summer, and that the pharmacy drew medicine off it.

"Four minutes fast means your medicine comes early, not late," he said.

"It means my medicine comes when it comes," she said. "Change it."

He set it to the corridor and put his ear to the case out of a habit that had nothing to do with pay. Nothing came up at him from under this floor either. The hospital floor was three storeys above the water line and the water line was four hundred yards north, and he was a fool, and he put his tools away.

At the school on Verge Lane a boy of nine had to be held up to the hammer to stop the hour, because the striker's rope had stretched over the summer, and when Orrin had shortened it the boy asked whether it would break the bell.

"It will break the rope," Orrin said. "Not the bell. Bells are harder than rope. That's most things."

The market scales were the last of it. Halloway's stall scale ran light by a twelfth and had been running light by a twelfth for long enough that a good deal of the market had quietly stopped weighing apples. He adjusted it and put the check weight on it twice and wrote the slip, and Mr Halloway watched him do it and said, without much in the way of complaint, that a quarter of a pound in eleven weeks was eleven weeks of nobody cheating anybody, so he ought to be grateful, and did not appear to be.

---

The last bell went at nine, and the bench was cold, and Orrin made tea he did not want and stood in the doorway with it.

The Quarter was keeping perfect time. That was the thing about the Clockwork Quarter, and he had known it since he was eleven years old and standing in this doorway the first morning he ever slept here: every clock in the district agreed, and it agreed with the Cathedral, and if you asked anybody on this street what time it was they would tell you and be right, and the whole of Morrowfen's self-regard stood on that fact. The city that never lost a minute. He had grown up inside the boast and had never once asked what the boast was standing on.

He went in and took down the grey ledger from the shelf.

It was not the shop's book. The shop's book had columns for a customer, a machine, a fault, a part, and a fee, and it was kept for the tax man and the guild. The grey one was a school exercise book bought for nine coppers, and it had been going for six years, and it recorded what machines actually did, in the order they did it, with dates, and no conclusions.

He turned to a blank page and wrote the date at the top, and then, in the margin where he had started keeping the oddities, he wrote:

*Cathedral public clock, crown case. 8 + 1. Ninth sound not in the clock. Low, from below, through bracket and floor. Water returning in a pipe. Gauge says correct twice. No defect.*

He read it back. It was the sort of line he had written perhaps two hundred times in six years — a sound he could not put a name on and could not put a fault to, in a machine he had no reason to distrust and could not stop thinking about.

He put the pencil down and thought about writing it anywhere else. There was a defect note, which was a normal thing, and a normal thing would send a guild engineer up the Rise with a ladder and a bill, and would cost the hall a fee and the Quarter some standing with a customer that could afford to be choosy about its clockwork.

He did not write the note. He closed the grey book and put it back on the shelf behind him, in the dark, where it had lived for six years, and washed his cup, and went up the outside stairs to his room over the bathhouse with the Cathedral bells still going in his head, counting, out of long habit, and not finding anything wrong.

Nobody knew his name at the Cathedral. He had told himself that was an injustice.

He had also told himself, on the walk down the hill, that it was a relief.
