# Chapter 41 — An Invoice, and a Bracket in Year Three

The licensing floor at half past nine on the Monday morning of the first of the fifth month had four men on it and a rack of forms and a clerk he did not know, and Orrin Vale went up the stairs with eighty-two shillings in coin in his coat pocket and told nobody, because he had told nobody on the Saturday either, and a decision that has been made in a shop book on a Saturday night is a decision that goes to a counter on a Monday morning and nowhere else.

He had been in this hall two hundred times in eleven years and had never once used it as a counter. He had used it as a place to bring a work book to, and a fee to, and a complaint to, and a book to be copied, and on the nineteenth of the third month he had sat on a bench outside it for nineteen hours. He had never asked it for anything.

The form was in the rack at the end nearest the window. It is a single side, and it is the same side he read in the public index on the Rise four months ago with somebody else's name in the countersignature box.

> *Form GH-9. Request by an owner, an occupier, or the maintaining hall, in respect of a standing entered under this series. Class: state which. Heads: state the number. Period: one year from the date of this request. The price schedule is at the foot of this form and is the hall's own property.*

And at the foot, in the same type as the rest of it:

> *Withdrawal and reinstatement. Two shillings the head for a year.*

He filled it in on the counter, standing, because there is a pen on a string at the counter and a man who sits down to fill one in in this hall looks like a man who has a difficulty. It took him four minutes and it took him four minutes because there is nothing on the form to think about. It wants a number and the number is forty-one and he has had it in his head for four months.

*Class: maintenance-deferred, transferred out eighteen years ago. Class required: as at the date of the original entry. Heads: 41. Period: one year from the date of this request. District: Lower Weirs pumping. Site: Weir Two floodgate and second-stage pumping station. Reference: batch 21/4, public index of transfers, Cathedral Rise.*

He put the coin on the counter in a heap, because the hall's counter does not like coins to be counted twice, and Della Marsh came out of the back of the licensing floor with a day book under her arm and did not say good morning.

She counted it, and wrote the figure in the margin, and initialled it, and did not give him a receipt, because she has a rule of her own about receipts and has had it since before he was apprenticed.

"Eighty-two shillings," she said. "Two shillings the head, forty-one heads. And you are one pound and a penny over and there is a heap in front of me and I am not going to argue with a man about a penny at ten to ten in the morning."

She put a wet stamp in the box. The hall uses that stamp on a request and not on a record, and it came off on the second try, which it does about one time in six, and she did not apologise for it.

Then she wrote a number in the top right corner in pencil. "That is a request number. It is not a record and it is not a certificate and it will not go on your card, and if you come back in a year there will be nothing on the card about this either, because requests are not held against holders. They go in the file by site. The file by site is on the second floor and the second floor is open to anybody who asks."

"Thank you," said Orrin Vale.

"Don't," said Della Marsh.

On the stairs he was aware that he had thanked her, and that she had got in first, and that this was the correct outcome, and that he had lost about half a second.

---

The fourth floor of 12 to 20 Ferrywright Row was lit on the Monday afternoon, and it had not been lit since the Friday of the fourteenth of the fourth month.

The works had put a current-pattern bracket on the riser on the twenty-third and three feet of new pipe with it, and had charged the landlord nine pounds and four shillings, which sits on a works' invoice in the same type as four hundred and eleven pounds of plaster and means the same kind of thing, which is that a week of somebody's dark is a thing with a price on it, and the price is not apportioned to the four people who live in the dark.

He went up at two with a case, a tray, and a lamp he did not need. Hanna Stroud was on the landing with a lamp she also did not need, and neither of them said anything about that.

The clocks are in a recess on the fourth-floor landing: three long-case, about forty inches, of 1911; and a bracket clock on the half-landing wall, facing the window.

Dilly Amnersley was sixty-six and she was in 4B with her door open and her cardigan on, and she told him the whole of it in about ninety seconds without once asking him to do anything.

"They went on the Saturday," she said. "The big one first and then the other two inside a week, and the little one on the wall stopped on the Sunday and it has not moved since. I have been up here on the Monday and the Monday and the Monday with a lamp in my hand and I have wound the two that would take a wind and I have let them down again the way my husband showed me, and I want you to tell me if I have done it wrong."

"You have not done it wrong," said Orrin Vale.

"You have not looked."

"I have looked at them for a quarter of an hour from the step and I have looked at the case of the big one through the glass and the big one has come off its pulley, which tells me the rest. Come and hold the light."

They had been standing since the Saturday and had run for eleven years before that, which is a thing that sounds like neglect and is not. They had run on a private spur off the town's own water since the block was built in 1911, and the spur was cut off from the district supply in 1923, and they had gone on running on their own weights and their own going barrels at the rate the town kept in 1911, and nobody had ever corrected them, and that last fact is the whole of what they are. Eleven years is two full turns of a going barrel a day and a half, and barrel teeth do not like being asked to do that on heavy oil and no attention.

He wrote the price on the back of an order pad before he took his coat off, which is a thing he has done four times in eleven years and does not enjoy, and which Hanna Stroud has paid for without arguing once, and which Ombersley at the ropeworks said four months ago was the only reason he had ever let this man into his building.

> *12–20 Ferrywright Row, fourth floor. Four clocks, private spur, 1911. Clean, let down, bush, re-beat, hang and certify. Twenty-two hours at the hall's out-of-shop rate of four shillings and sixpence, being four pounds nineteen shillings. Materials, cord, one click spring, four bushings, a pint of solvent, one pound seventeen shillings and sixpence. **Total, five pounds sixteen shillings and sixpence.***
>
> *One thing would make it dearer and I am telling you now instead of afterwards: if the big one's going barrel is cracked, a new barrel is about three pounds more and a fortnight's notice. I do not think it is cracked. If it turns out to be cracked, you will get a telegram from me and not an invoice.*

Hanna Stroud read it and gave it back and said, "That is the second time this year a man has told me what a thing would cost before he started, and the first time was you, so I am becoming a woman who reads these."

She said the other thing then, and she said it on the landing, and Dilly Amnersley was in the doorway and could hear all of it, and neither woman pretended afterwards that the other had not been there.

"I want them on the district line," said Hanna Stroud. "I want a firm, and a certificate a firm has signed, and an entry in a schedule somewhere, and I want to be able to hand an insurer a sheet with a firm's name on it and say: this is what the clocks in my building are and this is who looks after them. I have nine blocks. I have a premium under review because a records office says my returns are unavailable. If those four clocks are on the district line in programme year three with six hundred other brackets on the same sheet, then I have a piece of paper. I do not have a piece of paper. I have a lamp and a woman of sixty-six and a card with a key on it that says the key is not a service."

"And if they are on the district line," said Dilly Amnersley, from the doorway, "then they will be eleven minutes out by midsummer, and so will the clock in the hall, and so will the one at the corner, and the four of mine will be the only four in this building that agree with one another, and in about two years there will be a woman up here with a bar correcting them and the two of them who can see the light out of this window will not be the ones in the right."

He did Dilly Amnersley's. He said so in one sentence, on the landing, before he took his coat off, in front of the landlord, because a result of his that goes out of a room with nobody in it who can refuse it is a result he is not going to let go of quietly, whatever the third rule was invented for.

"I am taking them to their own rate. The rate of 1911. Not the district's, and not the time. The four of them will be set to each other and I will certify them against each other and I will not certify them against the district reference and I will not be able to tell you what time it is from any of them. I will write on the certificate that they are four minutes ten out and that the district keeps the time and that this is a property of the building. That is the truth and it is the whole of the truth and it is useless to an insurer, Mrs Stroud, and I am going to do it because I asked Mrs Amnersley what she wanted and she told me, and nobody in this building has asked her in eleven years."

"Monday mornings," said Dilly Amnersley. "I have never asked for anything. I want it in the certificate that it is done on a Monday morning by whoever is in 4B, and I do not want my name on it."

---

He did the three long-case on the Tuesday and the bracket clock on the Wednesday, twenty-two hours in all, and the fourth floor was lit, and he could see his own hands.

Letting a weight clock down is the first thing you get wrong. A going weight clock is not a weight on a line; it is a weight on a line attached to a going barrel, and the barrel is the thing that keeps the train in motion, and if you unclick the ratchet and let the weight go then the weight will run down, the barrel will turn backwards, and the train will be driven backwards against its own train drive, and you will find out what you have done in about four seconds and you will find out from the sound. So you put one hand flat on the ratchet, hold the weight off the line with the other, ease the click a fraction at a time until the weight's inertia is on your palm and not on the barrel, and then you take the weight down in your hand like a bucket of water while your other hand keeps the ratchet off the click and the barrel off the train. He had learned it at nineteen from a man who had learned it from a man, and he had never once been able to teach it in words, and it is the only thing in his trade that has to be felt.

The big one on the shelf had thrown its weight off the pulley, and it lay on the boards against the skirting with its cord standing up out of the loop like a dropped wire, and he put it back on the pulley and made a new loop and tied the old cord off short and left the tail hanging with a scrap of paper under it, because there is nowhere on a clock for a note and hanging a paper in the case is the whole of the trade's idea of a record.

Then the case, the dust sheet, the movement on a tray, and the barrel.

He cleaned the barrel teeth with a badger brush and a pegwood and he did not use a sharp tool on them, because a sharp tool on a barrel tooth is a chisel, and a chisel is how a tooth gets sheared off, and a sheared tooth on an eleven-year barrel is not a repair, it is a different clock. He counted them anyway, twice, with the glass, because counting is free: forty-one in the click wheel, thirty-six on the great wheel, and ninety-eight on the barrel, and the ninety-eighth tooth on the third wheel had a bright spot about the width of a hair where the grease had gone and it had touched steel. That is what eleven years does. That is not a fault. There is no form anywhere in this city for a bright spot the width of a hair.

He took the train apart in the order the man taught him: great wheel, centre wheel, third wheel, escape wheel, and the escapement last, because the escapement is the only part of a clock that will tell you a lie, and you want to know that you have cleaned everything else first. The bushings he drilled and the pivots he burnished, and he left the escape teeth alone, and the pallets he cleaned with a scraper of soft brass and a piece of window glass, and he held them up to the light and looked for a bright line along the impulse face, and there was one on the left pallet, and he took two thousandths off it with the glass and no more, because a pallet face with no mark on it is a pallet face that will not lock.

And then the beat.

A long-case of 1911 is beaten to a rate, and a rate is a thing the town kept in 1911 and the town has not kept since, and to set this clock to the district's rate would be to take a machine built to one standard and put it on another and lose the only thing it has got, which is that it is the same as the other three. So he put a chronometer on the sill and read all four against it for six hours on the Tuesday and again for six on the Wednesday, and he read them in the order long, long, bracket, long, so that no clock got the first hour and the coldest bench, and he worked out the mean of the four and set each one to that mean. The mean of the four that evening was two and a quarter seconds a day slow. After the Wednesday it was one and a half seconds a day spread across the whole of them, and one and a half seconds a day on a clock with a going barrel is a clock that will still be in agreement with the other three at Christmas.

A beat set is not a curved glass and it is not a piece of paper. It is a mark on a piece of card, and the card goes on the wall of the room for three months, and the man who set it looks at it four times a day and does not touch it, because a beat that is adjusted once a day is a beat that will be hunting for the rest of its life. He marked the four cards and left them and wrote the date and the hour on each of them, and they were still on the walls on the Thursday, and Dilly Amnersley came up at eight on the Thursday morning while he was packing the tray, looked at all four of them for a while, and did not touch them either.

The bracket clock on the half-landing wall was the one he had been dreading and the one he had the least trouble with, and he could not have said beforehand why. It hung on a bracket screwed to a brick that is a facing and not a wall, which is a thing that is true of about half the bracket clocks in this city, and the bracket had gone out of true in a way that leaned the case about a degree and a half into the window, and for fifty years that has meant that the case has been gaining and losing in a cycle with the month, and it is why the little one on the wall is the one that has needed the least attention in a life of not needing any. He took the bracket out, packed it with a copper washer cut to his own hand, and put the case back a half-degree plumb so that the window is on its side of the case instead of its shoulder.

Then he hung all four, and wound all four, and started all four within about four minutes of each other, and stood on the landing and let them run and did not look at any of them for eleven minutes, because the first eleven minutes after a hanging are the eleven minutes in which a clock will tell you it is pleased with itself whatever has been done to it.

They were still running on the Thursday. He read them one more time against the chronometer, and wrote his ledger entry, and the entry is the only reason this chapter is in the book rather than being a nice morning somebody had.

> *Monday the 1st, the fifth month. Four clocks, 12–20 Ferrywright Row, fourth floor, private spur, 1911, let down, cleaned, rebushed, re-beat to their own rate. Two long-case and one long-case and one bracket. Twenty-two hours. Materials one pound seventeen shillings and sixpence. **Total five pounds sixteen shillings and sixpence, which is the figure I gave Mrs Stroud on the Monday and the figure on the invoice.**
>
> *Mean rate of the four, two and a quarter seconds a day slow, spread one and a half seconds a day. Four minutes ten out of the district reference, as they have been for eleven years, and the district reference is the one the town keeps now and it is not going to stop for four clocks.*
>
> **This is maintenance on a customer's machine. It is not a correction. The count of my own hand corrections is two and it was two on Monday morning and it is two now.** A correction is a bar against a civic clock and a receipt and a column. These four are on a private spur that has not spoken to a district since 1923, and I have not altered a minute of anybody's time anywhere in this city this week, and Mrs Amnersley in 4B has four clocks that agree with one another and cannot tell anybody what time it is, and she asked for exactly that and I have given her exactly that, and it is her decision and not mine, and she is sixty-six and it is the only thing in this book that has gone right this year.*

Then the certificate, which is four of them, one to each clock, on a single sheet with four heads, because a certificate is a certificate of one machine and four clocks is four machines and there is no provision on the form for saying so, and the hall's practice is that a man writes four.

Each head carries the site, the pattern, the date of the last overhaul, the number of hours, the mean rate, and the standard to which the machine is certified. And on the standard line, for all four, in the same words: **certified against the mean of three other clocks of the same date, pattern and maker. Not certified against the district reference. The machine is not on the district line and has not been since 1923.**

And then the fifth line, which is not on the form, on all four, in the same hand, because Hanna Stroud asked him for a piece of paper and he is not going to give her a piece of paper that stops where the form stops.

> *Wound and maintained on Mondays by the occupier of 4B, to whom a key was given in 1911 and whose tenancy record states that the key is not a service. Eleven years. No charge. The person named has asked that her name be not written, and has said the reason, and the reason is on file: she has been paid nothing for eleven years and does not intend to be paid now and would rather not be found out.*

Dilly Amnersley read that on the Thursday morning with her glasses down her nose and did not say a word for about a minute, and then she said, "You were asked," which is a sentence about a rule and not about a clock, and he said, "You were," and she said, "Then it stays," and went down, and he wrote down that she had read it and that she had said it stays, and dated it, because the whole of this batch is a fight about whether a thing that happens is written down.

The invoice went out on the Thursday afternoon in an envelope with a copy of the certificate in it, and it is the only piece of work this year that has gone out of that room with a name on it that can be refused.

He noticed that. It sat on the end of the counter with four pins in it and a date on it and a figure on it that came to exactly what he had said it would come to, and the person it was for could refuse to pay it and did not, and he looked at it for a moment longer than a man looks at an invoice, and then he wrote the day's second entry on the back of the order pad and did not say anything out loud about it at all, which is the entire difference between the third rule as it was broken on Saturday the twenty-ninth of the fourth month and as it was satisfied on Thursday the fourth of the fifth, and the difference is a piece of paper with a price on it and a woman who could send it back.

---

Two documents came on the bench that week and neither of them was a scene.

The first is a sheet of foolscap on a nail on the ward board outside the Weir Two pump house, and it went up on the morning after a sitting, and it is in the hand of Nadia Erridge, who is thirty-five and is the clerk of the Water Users' Committee and posts its notices because the ordinance requires an affected district to be told.

> *WATER USERS' COMMITTEE, LOWER WEIRS. Notice of a schedule of the night draw upon the district main, from Bellweather Road to the Weir One gate, made at the sitting of the seventeenth of the fourth month and effective from that date until the thirtieth of the fifth, the committee not having power to fix a longer term. The night draw is restored to four. The daytime draw is held to the present schedule.*
>
> *The committee is not able to consult the district, the district having no standing under the water act.*

That last line is not in the ordinance. It is the only reason the sheet is on that board and not in a file, and a clerk does her best inside a form with one line for it, and nobody has ever asked her whether she put it there.

Orrin read it standing up on the Thursday, with his back to about forty feet of canal, and it was correct. It was the correct instrument, made by nine men with a property qualification at a lawful sitting, and it protects the canal front for the spring tides, and the front does not flood in the spring because of it.

The line is stepping again at fifty seconds a night in one step at the fourth draw, and the two clocks on that hill that were six minutes and forty seconds apart on the Saturday the twenty-ninth of the fourth were eight minutes and twenty seconds apart on the Monday the first of the fifth, and every window on the hill can see it, and there is still no form and no column and no office that has to answer for it.

And his own prediction, made at a bench on the sixteenth of the fourth in the recommendation column of a work book and entered there in a hall form of his own motion with nobody in four districts able to refuse it, came true on the seventeenth, in about nine words: *that the line will step when the draw is restored, and that the error will be about twice what it is inside a month.*

The committee were right to do it. That is the part he has not got over and is not going to get over this month: the committee did the correct, lawful, careful thing, in a room, with nine men who own property, to protect about eleven hundred people from water in their kitchens in the spring, and they did it on a district's own figures, and the district could not be asked, and the line stepped again, and every clock in this city is now further out than it was, and the front does not flood.

The second document is a line in an accountancy file on the canal road, and the file is the district works' and the line is dated the thirtieth of the fourth month, which is the day after he read a countersignature out loud in a room over a boat-builder's shop, and the line is this, entire:

> *Diversion, ward reserve to Carrow Street main. Reviewed. The ward's flood performance is a matter for the ward. Reviewed at the discretion of the district works.*

A warden gave a date in a room on the tenth of the fourth month, and the room had no power to order it, and she kept it anyway, and the review on the thirtieth is four lines long and gives the district what it was given in the third month, which is a discretion, and a discretion has no date on it and does not have to be given twice.

---

He shut the shop on the Thursday at six because he had a job on the Friday, and a job means a man is somewhere else, and the run on that street between the fifth of the fourth month and the fourth of the fifth had been the best the shop had done in four years, because every window on the hill could see the difference between two clocks four hundred yards apart and a man who can see a thing is a man who will telephone somebody about it, and a telephone is not a form and a form is what he has been trying to find for four months.

He had a job on the Friday. It was a regulator on a church clock at Ferrywright corner that Ombersley had recommended and the vicar had owned, and it was eight hours at the out-of-shop rate and about three pounds ten, and it wanted doing properly and not at night.

What he did not have, on the Thursday night, in a shop with a lease, was a person in four districts who had been told that he was going to pay eighty-two shillings of his own money on Monday to put a crew's standing back into the civic model for a year, and who could have stopped him. He had looked for one on the Monday and had found a counter and a wet stamp and four minutes and a woman who said *don't* before he could say thank you, and that was the whole of the room.

He wrote the last line of the day and it is the shortest thing in the book and the only one in this batch that is not a number.

> *Fifth month, the fourth. £5 16s 6d, paid, and a woman who could have sent it back did not.*
> *Forty-one heads, a year, on the Monday. Eighty-two shillings. And nobody in the Weirs will ever hear of it, and the ward's rates go up in the spring whatever the form says, and I told her the price on Saturday and she told me the price again and I have still not got a counter-argument, and I have decided that a piece of paper with a name on it that can be refused is worth a great deal more than a form with forty-one heads on it that cannot, and that both of those things are true, and that I do not have the other one.*
