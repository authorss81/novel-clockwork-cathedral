# Chapter 51 — Forty Minutes and Fifty Seconds

The line is not broken. That is the first thing, and it takes him the whole of a morning to establish, and it is the reason the whole of what follows happens.

---

On the Thursday morning of the ninth of the sixth month a clockmaker walked out of the Weir Two pump house at about ten o'clock with two watches, a notebook, a rule, a pencil, and no licence of any kind, and he wrote four things on the first page of the notebook before he wrote a single figure on any page of it, and the four things are these.

> *Ninth of the sixth month. Weir Two branch, root to end.*
> *I have no licence. I cannot certify a machine and I cannot certify a toll and this book of readings is not a certificate and is not a return and is not a schedule and is not in anybody's office.*
> *These are my own figures, taken with two watches, one on the district reference and one on the machine, in daylight, on the date at the top of each page.*
> *I am writing them down because I can check them again and a feeling cannot.*

He had written that last sentence in the second month of the year about a hospital pump, and he had been wrong about the pump, and the sentence had still turned out to be the only rule he had that worked.

---

The branch leaves the pump house under the canal road and comes up the Quarter hill and runs out along Weir Lane and on to Weir One gate, and it is about four and a half miles long, and there are six clocks on it that anybody in this city keeps time by, and he did them in the order they are connected, which is the order a signalman would do them in and not the order that is convenient, because the convenient order is the order that makes the numbers come out looking like a story.

**The first machine is the station clock in the pump house, and it is three minutes and fifty seconds slow of the district reference, and it has been that for as long as anybody has measured it, and there is nothing wrong with it.**

That is a true sentence and it is the reason the morning went the way it went. A station clock at the root of a branch is the datum. If the root is out, everything out of it is out by the root's error as well, and the root's error is constant, and a constant is the one thing on a clock line that a man can take away again. He took it away, in his head, in about four seconds, at the pump house door, and then he stopped taking it away, because the second reading was going to be nine minutes and twenty seconds worse than the first and he wanted to see whether it was.

The second machine is in the outer room of the pumping ward on the canal road, and it is **thirteen minutes and ten seconds** slow.

Nine minutes and twenty seconds between the root and the second machine. He wrote both numbers down and wrote the difference under them in brackets and did not say anything, because a man who says something out loud on a canal road at ten in the morning is a man who has decided what the number is before he has got the next one.

---

The two public clocks on the Quarter hill are four hundred yards apart and about level with each other, and on the first of the fourth month a man in a window could see that they disagreed without a gauge and without a watch, and by the first of the fifth month every other window on that hill could see it too, and the number had been 8 minutes 20 seconds on that Monday morning.

The north one is **twenty-four minutes and ten seconds** slow.

The south one is **one hour five minutes** slow.

He stood in the middle of the road between them with a watch in each hand for a while, and then he wrote down the difference between them, which is **forty minutes and fifty seconds**, and then he did the arithmetic twice on the back page, because it is the figure he has been carrying since the first of the fourth month and it is the only figure in this city he can be sure of.

> *8 min 20 s, Monday the first of the fifth month. On the page, in a grey book, and in two other books that are not mine.*

He got it wrong the first time, on the back page, and he crossed it out rather than rubbing it out, because a man who rubs out a wrong figure is a man who has decided his own book is worth more to him than it is to anybody else.

> *~~Nights since the first: May twenty-six plus June eight, thirty-four.~~ Wrong. The day of the month is the count, and not what is left of it.*
> *First of May to ninth of June: thirty and nine nights. Thirty-nine at fifty seconds is one thousand nine hundred and fifty seconds, which is thirty-two minutes and thirty seconds.*
> *Eight minutes and twenty seconds, plus thirty-two minutes and thirty seconds, is forty minutes and fifty seconds.*
> *Forty-nine nights back from this morning is the twenty-first of the fourth month, and the committee's notice is dated the seventeenth, and four nights is about a week, and I have looked for that week on the ward board and in the works' file and I have not found it, and I am not going to guess at it here. It is somebody's night. It is in a book somewhere with a date in it.*

He had begun to write the Toll Stair into it, and stopped, and crossed out what he had begun, and then wrote the second half of it, and the second half is the whole of what there is to say:

> *There is no public board in Morrowfen with a column for this. There is a board on Cathedral Rise with two columns for minutes and a district, and since the third of the sixth there are two more, and one of them has a man's name in it and the other one is empty on purpose.*
> *There is nothing anywhere with a column for a clock being wrong.*

He stood there for about a minute with the notebook shut.

Then he went on down the hill, because he had two more machines and a train of thought he was not going to finish in the road.

---

The three tenement clocks off Weir Lane agree with each other, which is the one piece of good news in the morning and it is not good news, because three clocks that agree with each other and are one hour and twenty-seven minutes and thirty seconds wrong are three clocks that agree with each other.

**One hour twenty-seven minutes and thirty seconds.**

And the gate clock at Weir One is **one hour forty-five minutes and ten seconds.**

He wrote the six figures down in a column in the order they are connected, and then he wrote the five differences under them, and the differences are these.

| | | Slow of the district reference | Difference from the machine before it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Weir Two pump house, station clock | 3 min 50 s | — |
| 2 | Pumping ward, canal road, outer room | 13 min 10 s | 9 min 20 s |
| 3 | Quarter hill, north public clock | 24 min 10 s | 11 min 00 s |
| 4 | Quarter hill, south public clock | 1 h 05 min 00 s | 40 min 50 s |
| 5 | Weir Lane, three tenement clocks | 1 h 27 min 30 s | 22 min 30 s |
| 6 | Weir One gate, gate clock | 1 h 45 min 10 s | 17 min 40 s |

And then he sat on the low wall by the Weir One gate house for about half an hour with the notebook open on his knees, and this is what he wrote, and it is the most important thing in the notebook and the reason the notebook exists.

> *It is not an offset. Every machine on the line is inside tolerance of its own local beat and a fault does not do that; a fault is a number that stays the number it is.*
> *It is a ramp, and the far end is the worst end, and it is the shape the branch has been in since the committee put the fourth draw back on the seventeenth of the fourth month.*
> *The two hill clocks were six minutes and forty seconds apart on the twenty-ninth of the fourth and eight minutes and twenty on the first of the fifth and forty minutes and fifty seconds this morning. Fifty seconds a night, both times, exactly, and I have now got the same number three mornings running and it is the only number in this business I would stake a bench on.*
> *I have the shape and I have not got the division. The differences are nine-twenty, eleven hundred, forty-fifty, twenty-two-thirty and seventeen-forty, and the far end's difference is nearly twice the near end's, and the line divides by eight and takes its beat off the odd teeth, and the ratios of these five numbers are not the ratios of anything I can name. Either the teeth are not all the same size or I have missed a machine in the chain, and I have walked the whole branch and I have not missed a machine.*
> *I am writing down that I do not know, and I am going to leave it written down, and I am not going to guess at it in a book later.*
> *And the other thing, found on the Friday, and I want it in ink because it is the part I did not expect. The committee's own schedule ran out at midnight on the thirtieth of the fifth and the district went back on to the standing rotation, and the standing rotation is the one with four draws in it.*
> *So the schedule that made the line step expired and the step did not stop, and nobody was told anything by anybody, because nobody had to be told anything: the standing rotation is what happens when nobody decides anything at all. I have been eleven years in a trade where the way to stop a machine behaving badly is to change what somebody is supposed to do, and in this city you can leave the machine behaving exactly as badly by doing nothing at all, lawfully, for ever, and there is no form on which a district can object to a thing nobody did.*

---

The projection was right, and there is a line about it in the grey book with a date on it, and nobody has ever read it but him.

On the eleventh of the fifth month, at about half past seven in the morning, before the light was properly on, this man had stood on the Quarter hill road with a watch and looked at the two public clocks four hundred yards apart, and had written down **16 min 40 s**, and had written underneath it, in the same hand, **projection from the step, not a reading, ten nights at fifty seconds off the first of the fifth.**

Sixteen minutes and forty seconds is what ten nights at fifty seconds gives you, and it is what the two clocks were, and nobody in Morrowfen knows that except him.

He had gone back on the eleventh of the fifth month to check his own prediction for the same reason he had once gone back to check a governor: because a man who cannot check a thing he said in advance is a man who has stopped being able to tell the difference between being right and being lucky. It had been there. It was right. It is in a notebook and in a grey ledger, and in eight weeks every window on that hill could see it without a watch, and being right about it bought him nothing whatever, and there is no column for what a man is paid when he was right a fortnight in advance.

---

At about twenty past eleven he went back up Weir Lane on foot, because the standpipes are on Weir Lane and he had put the standpipes off and forgotten about them, and there are four of them between Bellweather Road and the Weir One gate.

The one at the corner of the second court is a standpipe and not a tap. It is a two-inch riser off the main with a valve at the bottom of it, and the valve is a clock-driven valve, and the clock that drives it is on the branch.

It is one hour twenty-seven minutes and thirty seconds out.

He had a bucket under it and a woman holding the other one, and she was about fifty-five and she had a shawl over her head and she had clearly been there some time, and the tap was running, and nothing was coming out of it into the bucket.

"Sit there half an hour," she said, before he had said anything. "I have been sitting here half an hour. My neighbour's is the same and hers is running and mine is not and I have had a bucket under mine since the Tuesday."

"Since the Tuesday," he said.

"Aye."

He got down and looked at it without touching anything, which is a thing he has made himself do for eleven years and which he has never once regretted. The riser is wet about eight inches up from the bottom and dry below that, and there is a stain on the brick behind it at the level of the eight inches that has been there long enough to have gone the colour of tea.

"That is a service pipe running dry below a valve that is open," he said. "Your valve opened. Your water is on the main and the branch says it should open at four o'clock this morning and it opened at about half past five, and there are two floors of pipe below that valve that have been full of air since about half past five, and if the pressure comes back on before somebody runs a pipe down to the main then you will get your water back in about four minutes and you will also have the top floor of this building coming down on your kitchen."

She said, "Right," in the way people say right when they are going to deal with it later.

"There is a man coming at four," he said, and then stopped, because there is no man coming at four. There has been no man coming at four for as long as the branch has been a branch. The valve opens when the branch tells it to and the branch is one hour and twenty-seven minutes and thirty seconds out, and what he had just said was **a sentence out of a document in a drawer about four miles away**, and it had come out of his mouth without his permission, and it was the first time in six months that he had said a thing in front of a person that he knew to be false and said it in the voice of a man quoting a document.

"That is not what I meant to say," he said. "I beg your pardon. Nobody is coming at four. Nobody is coming at all at the moment. The valve opened at about half past five and the pipe under it has been dry since, and the reason is a clock and not the water, and I will not pretend to you that I can tell you when the water comes back because I have not got a way of knowing."

She looked at him for a moment. "You are the clock one," she said. "From the shop on Verge Lane. My sister had one cleaned at Michaelmas and he came and he told her what was wrong with it and he did not charge her for telling her."

"Yes," he said.

"Then you will tell me what is wrong with it and you will not charge me for telling me," she said, "and I will want to know why nobody else has come."

---

At about half past one he was back in the Weir Two pump house and his hand went on the Weir One gate clock's case before he had decided to put it there.

He took it off again in about four seconds and did not ask anything.

That is the discipline and it works, and it is worth saying exactly why, because the reason is the whole of the argument between a sense and an answer and there is not one more word in it. If he had asked, he would have got a true answer in about a second. He would have felt the next short-term failure of a named load on one connected machine, which is the only thing he can do, and the thing he can do is real, and it is worth something. It would also have cost him something he could not file, it would have told him what happens **next** on one machine, and it would have told him **nothing whatever about the forty-nine nights.**

He could not feel a thing that was not connected, and this line was connected, and the ramp was in it, and the ramp was forty-nine nights old and spread over four and a half miles and six machines and the branch did not know he was standing in the room.

---

Bevin Sarr's board is on the wall of the Weir Two pump house and it has been there eleven years and it is a painted board about the size of a door with the nights of a shift crew chalked on it in a hand that is not hers, because she does not chalk it, one of the men does, and she keeps it, and she will not let it leave the room and has given a reason for that which she has never changed.

"There is a piece of paper in this city with a column for minutes and a column for a district," she said, when he told her what he had. "And since last Friday there is a column with a man's name in it and a column beside it with nothing in it, and that is the best thing anybody in this city has done in a year, and I have got three words for you about it. It is on a wall. People walk past it and stop."

"Yes," he said.

"So write it on my wall," she said, "and do not tell me what for, and do not ask me to initial it, and do not put a name on it, because the day this board has got a name on it somebody will come and take it off the wall and take it off me, and I have had it eleven years and I am not having it off a nail."

He wrote it on the board on the Thursday afternoon, in chalk, at about four o'clock, in six lines, with the times and the differences, and he wrote the date at the top and the two words *my figures* under the date, and he did not put a name on it, and he did not ask her to initial it.

She read it standing, the way a person reads a thing they will have to answer for later.

"Forty minutes and fifty seconds," she said, "on the two on the hill."

"Forty minutes and fifty seconds on the two on the hill this morning at about eleven, and an hour and three-quarters at the far end of the branch, and the far end is a gate."

"Right," said Bevin Sarr. "That is a number on a wall in a pump house and it is worth exactly what a number on a wall in a pump house is worth, and I have had eleven years of those, and the reason mine were worth something is that nine people stood in this room and read them out loud on the seventeenth of the fifth month at ten to five in the morning and wrote them down in a night book and there were names on it."

She turned back to her board and picked up the chalk.

"Chalk it high," she said. "There is a man of sixty-eight on the night side of this ward who cannot read at the height you have written it."

---

On the Saturday the eleventh of the sixth month he went up the Quarter hill at about eleven in the morning with no watch at all and stood in his own doorway on Verge Lane and looked at the two public clocks four hundred yards away.

They were forty-two minutes and thirty seconds apart.

He wrote the figure on the back of a scrap of wood and read it twice, because it was the only instrument in the business and it was a man in a doorway, and then he went and got a watch out of his coat and checked it, and the watch said the same thing.

The ninth had been forty minutes and fifty seconds. Two nights had gone by. Two nights at fifty seconds is one minute and forty seconds, and forty minutes and fifty seconds plus one minute and forty seconds is forty-two minutes and thirty seconds, and it is exactly that.

He had watched a number go from one figure to another with nothing in his hands, on his own doorstep, and there is no form anywhere in Morrowfen for a man to write that down on except a grey ledger in a shop with a lease on two benches, and the licence in his coat is of the third class, and it expires on the nineteenth of the third month next year, and since the first of the sixth month it does not let him certify anything at all.

He went back inside and wrote it in the grey ledger, and the last paragraph of the entry is the one that matters and it is short:

> *Eleventh of the sixth. Checked from the doorway, by eye, and then with a watch, and the pair on the hill forty-two minutes and thirty seconds apart, against forty minutes and fifty seconds on the ninth, and two nights have gone by and two nights at fifty seconds is one minute and forty seconds. I saw it go twice and had nothing in my hands on either occasion, and I did not have to be a clockmaker to see it, and I have no standing to say so to anybody.*
> *My own hand corrections on this city's machines: two. Eleven minutes on a lane in the Weirs and eight on a bracket at the Ferrywright corner, both in the second month of the year, both now inside an institutional form. Two, and it is two, and nothing this week was a third.*
> *Six readings, taken with two watches, in daylight, on the ninth. They are in a notebook and six lines of chalk on a board in a pump house and nowhere else. There is no column in the Office of Public Reckoning for a clock being wrong, there is no column in the works' return, there is no column in the guild, and there is no column in the Assembly, and there is no form in this city by which a man with two watches and no licence can make anybody answer for six numbers he wrote down himself.*
> *And there is one of them for a number of minutes, and a district, and since Friday a name and a duration. So the city has three columns for what a Reading costs and not one for what the machine was doing while it cost it.*
