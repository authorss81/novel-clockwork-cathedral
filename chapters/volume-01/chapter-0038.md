# Chapter 38 — Sealed, and for What Reason

The list of matters sealed is published on a board on the ground floor of the Rise, in a frame, on a Wednesday, and it is updated once a week, and it is the most-read object on that hill and the least-read object on that hill, and about forty people walk past it every day of their lives.

*Matters sealed under the continuity powers, week ending the nineteenth inst.*
*1. Pumping district records, Weir Two floodgate and second-stage pumping station, Lower Weirs. All shift returns, signal tables and gate-handling records, 11 years to date. Classified. Reference CS/114. Appeal: none.*

That entry went up on the morning of the twenty-fourth of the fourth month, and Mara Venn saw it at about a quarter past nine on the way to a room she was not going to be in, and read it standing up, and read it again on the Tuesday morning, on the way to the same door, because the first time she had not believed it, and the second time she had believed it and had to go and stand somewhere.

---

She did not telephone the works. She did not telephone the pumping ward. She did not telephone the guild, or the Office, or a single person, and the reason she did not was that she has spent nine years learning that a person who telephones you about a thing you have just read on a board will tell you what they want you to know, and that the whole of her trade is the difference between what a thing says and what a person says about it, and that the *Archive room* is the only public thing in this city she has ever been able to trust, because it is a room with a keeper in it and the keeper cannot be got at.

So she went and read it. The list is public; the *schedule* is not; and between the list and the schedule there is a form, and the form carries a reference and not a reason, and the reference is what a person gets.

CS/114. The classification is a Continuity Office security classification and it is applied under a section of the regional scheduling act that has been on the books for thirty-one years and has been used eleven times in that period, four of them on hourlines, and the standing instruction that goes with it is four inches wide in a printed column and reads *security of a continuity work*, and it means that a record may be sealed where disclosure would prejudice the operation, the security, or the continuity of a work essential to the city's water, power, or communications.

*Weir Two is a continuity work.* It is one of four gates in a pumping district at the bottom of a reclaimed basin, it controls the front, it has a gate, a second stage, a signal system, and a hand valve, and the hand valve is the sentence that Perry Vance died on in the first month of this book, and the district works knows that, and the Continuity Office has an inspection authority over every hourline in this city and an interest in every gate on a water main that there is.

The signal tables are the honest ground. A gate's signal table is the code by which the gate is opened, held, and released: a sequence, a count, a set of lamps, and an interlocking that will not let a gate move out of a known position. It is a security matter in exactly the way that a bank vault combination is a security matter, and nobody in this city has ever published a gate's signal table, and no one ever will, and there is no argument available against that and Mara Venn did not make one.

The trouble is that a classification is applied to a file and not to a line.

The Weir Two records in the pumping works' custody are one file. The file is a file because a station's records are kept in a station's file, and a station's file contains the signal tables and the gate-handling records and the shift returns and eleven years of maintenance, and the classification instrument is a stamped sheet and the stamped sheet has one box on it and the box says *records*, and there is no discretion to classify less.

So the maintenance line went in. So the shift returns went in. So the district's own returns for the station — the quarterly returns the ward files with the works, which are the ward's own arithmetic, compiled by the ward, and which are the only thing a ward has — went in with everything else, in a box, in one afternoon, by a man doing his job.

And the district's own returns are the six pages that a woman in Green Span paid four pounds ten to have printed in full with the Assembly's minutes, and they are in a binder on the Rise, and anybody may read them, and the binder says *Lower Weirs pumping district, district returns, four months to the end of last quarter*, and *three hundred and eighty-eight hours worked at night at the Weir Two station, compiled by the district, unverified.*

The number is in the minutes of the Assembly. The source is in a sealed file. And the Office of Public Reckoning, whose entire function is to know what a decision cost, cannot require the production of a sealed file and has no function in respect of one, and could not audit a rate base built on a return she is not permitted to see the working of, and knew that on the Wednesday morning, standing at a board, and went and sat down in her own office and wrote a note she did not send.

---

Tobias Rill came out to the pumping district on the Tuesday of the twenty-fifth of the fourth month with the case and the folding handle, and Orrin was at the Weir One gate at eight in the morning because Ivy Pike had telephoned him about a lamp on an indicator that was flickering, which is a lamp on the end of its life and which a man in a hall of clocks is fetched for, and which was a filament, and which took four minutes and a half and cost nothing and was done with the hall's man standing there holding the cover.

Rill was in a good coat and he had a folding handle and he was forty-four and he was the most reasonable man in this city and he had never once in his life behaved as though that was an achievement.

"I am not here about the lamp," he said, before Orrin had got the case down. "I am here to tell you what I did on the Wednesday, because I would rather tell you in a pump house than have you read it on a board in a frame, and because I have read about nine pages of yours in the last four months and I owe you a conversation and I have not had one."

"Go on."

"I classified a file." He put the case down on the sill of the gate-house window, which Orrin thought was not a good place for a case. "It is lawful. It is correct. It is the correct application of a rule that was written in a year when this city had a different arrangement with its water, and the rule is thirty-one years old and has been used eleven times, and I have the authority, and I applied it on Wednesday morning without speaking to anybody, because that is the practice, and because if I had spoken to the ward first the ward would have had a fortnight in which to prepare, and a fortnight is enough to move a file."

"You did not ask Sel Kade."

"I did not." He said it without apology and without defiance, in the tone of a man reading a reading. "Mr Vale, I want to set out the two halves of this properly, because in about four years there will be a file about it and I would like the file to be correct.

"If I under-classify it, and it turns out to be a matter of signal security, then I have put a gate's signal table and a crew's hours on a public file, and the next man who wants to break a gate open at three in the morning has the table, and there is a ward on the bottom of this basin and a hundred and forty thousand people in the district, and that is a thing I will not do. Not once, and not for a friend, and not for a clockmaker who writes things down.

"If I over-classify it, and it turns out to be a matter of hours — which is what it is, of course it is, we both know what it is — then a district has lost a record it compiled itself. Not a record of the city. Its own. Eleven years of a ward's returns, in a box, for ever, and the district cannot be asked for them and cannot complain and cannot appeal, and there is no appeal, and I have signed the paper that says there is no appeal, because the section says there is no appeal and I have no power to create one.

"I had to pick one. I picked one. I would make the same pick in a week, and I have made it before, and I will make it again, and I am not going to pretend to you that I would not, because you have read enough of my files to know exactly what I do with a file, and I would rather you had it from me."

Orrin stood in the gate-house doorway and looked at a cast-iron governor thirty years older than the works' standard, and did not say the thing he had been carrying since the eighth of the third month, and did not say it because there was nobody in that room who could refuse it and there was a rule about that and he had written the rule himself.

"When did you last review the interlock delay at Ferrywright?" he said.

"The fifteenth of the third month, and I wrote four lines about it in a public file, and you have read them." Rill picked the case up off the sill. "Mr Vale, I am going to say one more thing and then I am going to go and read a signal table, because I have to read it, because I sealed it and the law says a classification is not a verdict, it is a rule about who may read, and the office that sealed a thing is entitled to read it, and I have read the one on that file today and I have read it every year since I have had this post and I have never once read it as a person.

"It does not touch anything under the Cathedral. I do not have the authority to classify anything under the Cathedral and I have never been asked to and I would decline if I were. If anybody in this city ever tells you that this file is a route to anything that is under the Cathedral, then they are telling you a thing that is not true, and I will put it in writing, on this piece of paper, with a reference and a date, and I will sign it, and I will not enjoy signing it and I will do it."

"That is not what I asked."

"It is the only part of the answer I know for certain," said Tobias Rill, "and in nine years of inspections I have learned to lead with the part I know for certain."

---

She went and read the index on the Wednesday morning, because the index is public and the index is the only place in this city where a hole in a record is required to be distinguishable from a lie, and if a hole is going to be sealed then somebody ought at least to be able to say where it is.

The Archive room on the ground floor of the Rise is open to petition and staffed by a keeper who can decline, and the keeper on the Wednesday was Sindri Lake, who has been the keeper for nineteen years and who refused Orrin's request in the twentieth of the first month in three sentences, kindly, and did not enjoy it, and who has not been asked about it since.

He got the volume down himself. He does that; it is on the shelf, in sequence, and it is about four hundred pages of transfers out of and into the public record in a small upright hand, and Mara Venn has read eleven pages of it in nine years and he has read all of them.

*Batch 21/4 (Weirs series), forty-one entries. Transferred out of the public record eighteen years. Form GH-9, standing withdrawal, maintenance-deferred class. Requested by the hall on behalf of the district. Price, two shillings the head for a year. Routing: the hall, the ward, the Continuity Office inspection file, a public-health rota. Countersigned for the affected district by the pumping warden of the Lower Weirs, deputy: S. Kade.*

And in the margin, in a different hand, in pencil, dated Wednesday:

*Records at the other end of this transfer sealed. Index entry remains open.*

"That is mine," said Sindri Lake. "I wrote it at about four o'clock on Wednesday with a pencil because I do not have authority to do it with anything else, and I have written four of them in thirty-one years and three of them were in a different month."

"You are the only person in this building who can say where the hole is."

"I am the only person in this building who can say where the hole is and I have said it in pencil to four hundred people who will never look at it." He put the volume back on the shelf and squared it. "The entry is not the record, Ms Venn. I have said that to a maintainer in this room in the twentieth of the first month and I have said it to a great many people since and I will say it to you because you will not repeat it. An index entry is a statement that something was taken out. It is not the thing that was taken out, and it never was, and if the thing is sealed then the statement is all that is left, and a statement is not a hole you can look down and it is not a name you can hold anybody to and it is certainly not a way back in."

"I know that."

"I know that you know it, and I have watched you work for three years and you are the only investigator in this city who has ever said the words *I know that* to me before I said them." He turned round to the door. "And it is a good index entry, for what it is. It is the only document in the city that says a district was taken out of the arithmetic. Forty-one entries, a form number, a price, four routing copies and a woman's name, and it has been in a public book for eighteen years, and nobody has ever read it, and now the thing at the other end of it is in a box and the box is the only part anybody will be able to point at. So the statement stands and the record is gone, in that order, and both of those are correct, and if you want my opinion as a keeper and not as an officer, it is the most ordinary Wednesday of my working life."

---

The cost did not arrive that week. It arrived on the Thursday of the twenty-seventh, in an envelope from a firm of insurance brokers who had insured nine blocks for a landlord of fifty-four and had said in the first month of the year that the district's returns were insufficient.

Hanna Stroud read it in her own office and telephoned the shop about four o'clock, which she has never once done in nineteen years of letting.

"Mr Vale, I am going to read you the fourth paragraph and then I am going to tell you what I think of it, and you are going to tell me I am wrong, and I am going to let you, and I am going to be unreasonable, and I would like it noted that I telephoned a clockmaker on a Thursday afternoon about an insurance letter instead of telephoning an office, and I know exactly what that is."

"Read it."

"It says: *we are unable to verify the returns submitted in respect of the Lower Weirs pumping district in respect of the period stated, the source records being unavailable to us, and we must advise that in the circumstances the schedule of premiums for the properties at 12 to 20 Ferrywright Row and the eight other blocks requires to be reviewed, and we shall be glad to receive the certified returns at the first opportunity.*" She read it twice, exactly the same way both times. "Source records unavailable. That is you. That is four months of my life. The word is *unavailable* and not *withheld* and not *lost*, and it is the only thing in that letter I can get my teeth into, and I am not going to get my teeth into it, because a man in a good coat at a broker's office is not going to go to the Rise and read a board in a frame, and even if he did he would be told there is no appeal."

"You want me to—"

"I want you to tell me what a *certified return* is, and I have been a landlord for nineteen years and I have a word for it and it means the same thing it means to you, which is a return a man signs." She stopped, and started again. "I want you to understand what has happened to me. In the first month of this year I was asked by an insurer for the pumping district's returns and I could not produce them, and I wrote eleven weeks of letters, and a woman in Green Span paid four pounds ten to have them printed with the Assembly minutes, and I produced them, and the broker accepted them, and the premium on nine blocks went down eleven per cent in the spring, and I have been a landlord in this district for nineteen years and that is the only time in nineteen years that a piece of paper anybody else produced has ever been any use to me.

"And on Monday of last week a man in a good coat put a stamp on a box, and my premium is going up again, and the reason is not that anything has changed and not that anybody has done anything, and the reason is that the thing which was useful has become unavailable, and I am being asked to pay for a rate that was set on a figure I could check, and I cannot check it, and I am a rational woman and I do not know what else to be."

"That is right," said Orrin Vale.

"I know it is right. I did the arithmetic on the drive down and I got here and the arithmetic did not help." A pause. "I am not angry at you and I want that said before anything else. I telephoned a clockmaker and not an office because a clockmaker answered, and that is a thing I have noticed about this city this year and I am a landlord and I notice things."

"You could ask for the classification to be reviewed."

"By whom. There is no appeal, and you have told me that yourself, and I have read the fourth paragraph about four times and it does not say who to write to, and a letter that does not say who to write to is a letter that is not a complaint, it is furniture." She put the telephone down for a second and picked it up again. "I have nine blocks and thirty-one households on one of them and a woman on the fourth floor of one of the others who has carried four clocks up a stair with a lamp in her hand every Monday for eleven years, and I did not know her name until a fortnight ago, and I sign her rent book every month and I have never once been up that stair. That is the whole of what I have. I have not got a method and I have not got a book of initials. I have got a rent book and an insurance letter and a woman in a good coat. So I am going to ask you the only question I have got, and I have asked you it in a shop on Bellweather Road about a stair light and you answered it."

"What is it."

"Did the sealing change anything? Not the law — the law is lawful, I have read it and I understand it, and I am not a fool about what a signal table is. Did anything *change*? Is the water any different? Is the gate any different? Is the front any different? Or has a stamp gone on a box and everything in this district is exactly as it was on the Friday before, and the only thing that has happened is that a piece of paper which used to be true can no longer be checked?"

"No," said Orrin Vale. "Nothing has changed at all."

"Then I shall tell my broker that, and he will write it down, and it will be a letter of the same length as his, and in about a year the premium will be what it is going to be, and the only thing in this business that has happened to anybody is that they have stopped being able to say *I know*." She stopped. "Mr Vale, in nineteen years I have had nine buildings and two bad winters and one fire and I have never once been able to prove that anything I paid for was what I got. I have always been able to say *I know*. That is the whole of what a landlord has."
