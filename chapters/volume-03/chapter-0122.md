# Chapter 122 — The Notice

On Saturday the first of the fourth month a notice went up on three boards in the Lower Weirs, and it was the first works' schedule notice in this district in three years, and the reason it was the first is that for three years the district's return was nil, and on Friday the twenty-eighth day of the third month a clerk in the district works' accountancy office entered a figure where there had been a blank, and a district with a figure in its return is a district a schedule can be made about.

The notice was dated the twenty-fourth of the third month and the seven days it had to run took it to the thirty-first, and nobody could be dissatisfied with the date because the date was the works', and it was on the yard board on the canal road, and on the ward board outside the Weir Two pump house, and on the ward board at Green Span hospital. It was not anywhere else. It was not on a fourth board, and there is no fourth board in this district that anybody reads, and one of the two boards it was on is read by about eleven people a day and the other by about nine before lunch, and a notice is not a thing you can strengthen by putting it up more often.

It said, in the works' own form, that the district works' schedule for the pumping district of the Lower Weirs and the Green Span hospital branch would be adopted in the fourth month, that the schedule in force was the schedule of the last adoption, that the notice was given under the standing provision of seven days, and that any ratepayer of the district might attend the sitting at which objections would be heard and might make them.

---

The sitting was on the same afternoon, in a room over the works' yard office with the window open, and it is worth saying at once that a committee in this city may sit on a Saturday about twice a year and that this was one of the two. It had to be a Saturday because the works' engineering staff are all on the payroll on a Saturday and none of them is there on a Sunday, and a schedule cannot be objected to by a room in which the only men who know what the items are are not present.

Hollis Nunn put the sheet on the table face up, which is a thing he has done about four times in nineteen years and which took him about a second and a half, and said, "Item six. Standpipes, six hundred brackets, one thousand eight hundred and ninety pounds, over two years. That is on the sheet. It has been on the sheet since the sheet was made and I cannot put it in and I have not been able to put it in since the day I inherited the post, and I would like anybody here who has been told otherwise to look at the sheet."

Nobody had been told otherwise. That is the thing about item six: it is the only item on the sheet that anybody in this district has ever spoken of, and it has been there for the whole of the programme, and it has never been in anybody's mouth, and that is not an accident either, because a bracket is a fire thing and a fire thing is the easiest kind of money in the world to ask for and the easiest kind to be given, and the reason nobody asks is written on the same page in a different column and has been for nineteen years.

"Give us the reason," said Ivy Pike, who was there as a ratepayer of the district and had come on her own account and had told nobody she was coming and was asked by nobody.

"It is the money."

"It is not the money," said Ivy Pike, who is sixty-one and chairs a body she did not want to chair and has said for nineteen years that the district needs one thing in it that can be refused, and who has the particular calm of a woman who has already been told the wrong answer in three rooms this year.

"It is not the money and I have said the money, and I will now say the other thing and I am going to be brief about it because I have been brief about it in every yard I have ever stood in." Nunn took his hand off the sheet. "A schedule line is a thing somebody owns. That is the whole mechanism. When a thing goes on a schedule it belongs to somebody, somebody's money pays for it, and somebody has to be able to say in ten years' time whether it was done. Item six is a fitting on a service. A service is on a property. A property is in a return. And the thing that would own six hundred of them is a list of six hundred properties, and there is no such list, and there has not been one since the storm month, and there is no form in this city on which a man may go and compile one, and I have looked, and I have asked twice, and the second time I was told that the returns are made by the district and not by the works."

"It is not that it is expensive," said a woman of about fifty-one with a thin file of accounts in front of her and her name on the file. "It is that nobody can produce a list."

"It is that nobody can produce a list," said Nunn. "That is what I said."

"You said it a step short," said Doreen Tilley, who had been in the district works' accountancy office eleven years and had come up from it at ten past two with a folder and had asked whether she might speak and had been told she might, which nobody remembers doing. "I am the one who enters the return, and I entered the figure on Friday, and I would like to say why the return is not nil, because if I do not say it then this room will go away from here believing that something has been fixed."

She put the folder open and turned it round so that it faced the window and not the room, which was the way anybody in an office shows a thing they do not want read at a distance.

"Until Friday this return carried a blank. Not a zero. A blank, with a line under it, and the line has been there since the storm month and I have initialled it every year for eleven years, because a blank is what the form says when a district has not been assessed. On Friday I entered the district's rateable figure as at the first of the month, because the district is on the line, the line is a public line, and a public line that goes through a district is a thing a district has, and the district having it is what made the return non-nil. That is all it is. It is a total. It is a number of pounds' worth, and it is a true number, and it is the only thing about this district that the works has been able to write down for eleven years."

"And a total is not a list," said Nunn.

"A total is not a list," said Doreen Tilley. "Item six needs six hundred addresses and the return has one figure in it, and the distance between one figure and six hundred addresses is the distance between this notice going up and the six hundred brackets going in, and the distance is not eleven years of money. The money is there. It has been there for the whole programme. The money is £1 890 and it is the easiest sum on the sheet and it is the only one anybody in this district can say out loud without being asked where they got it. The thing that is missing is not money. It is a list, and nobody may go and make one, and I would like that written down in whatever comes of this afternoon, because in four years somebody is going to look at item six and see one thousand eight hundred and ninety pounds sitting on a sheet and conclude that somebody did not want it very much."

Somebody wrote it down. Doreen Tilley had brought a copy of the form and the copy went on the file, which is the only kind of writing down this district has ever been able to do and which is worth exactly as much as the day it is read.

---

The tell-tale came up second, because Teo Arden had put it on the agenda in a note of three lines and a half that he had handed in at the works' counter on the Wednesday, and the counter had given it to the engineer, which is the entire amount of machinery available to a man of twenty in a district of this kind.

"I am not asking for six hundred," said Teo. "There is a bracket on the front on Weir Lane with a quarter inch of iron round the two good fixings, and the works' own drawing says cast, three off per span, pattern 4B, see box 41, and the pattern is in box 41 and there is one of them left in the world and it is in the yard. I have read that. I was there. What I am asking for is one brass pointer on that bracket and every bracket of that class in this district, four inches long, on a hardened pin, showing a fortieth of an inch, and it is eleven shillings and fourpence, and it is the only instrument in the whole of this business that nobody in an office has to be asked to look at, and it does not make a bracket safe. It tells you the bracket has moved. That is all it buys and I am asking for it anyway, because in February that bracket came off in a flood and a woman in this district wrote down that the bracket on the works' sheet is item seven at two pounds fourteen shillings and sixpence and that she has a book of hours worth twenty-seven shillings, and neither of those two numbers is in anybody's return, and a brass pointer would have put a quarter of an inch in a file in a drawing somebody could hold."

"No," said Hollis Nunn.

"You said no in the yard in September and you were right about the answer then as well," said Teo.

"I was right about the answer then and I was wrong about the reason, and it took me until about the middle of the fourth month to work out which of the two I had been, and I have never told anybody, and it is the reason I am going to be quick now." Nunn turned the sheet round so that the item-six line was under Teo's hand. "It is the same answer and it is the same reason and the reason is that the pointer is on a bracket and the bracket is on a service and the service is on a property, and the property is not in a return, and if I put eleven shillings and fourpence on item six then item six has a fitting on it, and the fitting is on a bracket, and the bracket is on the same missing list, and I would have taken the argument I have just made about the six hundred and applied it to one brass pointer and been a man who said no to a four-inch length of brass to save himself the sum of four hundred and forty pounds' worth of somebody else's negligence. I am not doing that. It cannot go in for the same reason the six hundred cannot go in, and I would sooner be refused for a real reason than allowed for a small one, and that is the first sentence of that sentence I have ever said out loud in this room."

Teo Arden put his note back in his pocket before he said the next thing, which was a thing he had worked out in the yard and not in this room, and which was the only observation made that afternoon that nobody in the room had thought of making, and which was not about the money or the list or the pointer.

"A notice is the only instrument in this city that tells anybody anything before it happens," he said. "That is all a notice is. It is a thing with a date on it and seven days on it and nothing else in the world has those. And it is on three boards, and one of them is eleven people a day and one of them is nine before lunch, and there are two hundred and forty households in four streets in this district and not one of the three boards is in any of them, and I have been in all four of those streets and there is not a board in any of them and there is not a shop in any of them with a window on the street, and if you put your notice on a board in one of those four streets nobody would read it, because there is nothing to read it with, and a person who wants to know what you are about to do to their street has to walk to a shop in another district and stand at a counter, and every one of them has done that, and the reason they have done it is that there is no form on which they may be told."

The room did not take that as an argument, because he had not made it as one. He had made it as a count, and a count in a room like that one is not refused, it is put to one side, and it stood there for the rest of the afternoon and about two years later it was the reason a room of about nine people in four streets worked out that the only paper in this city about their own clocks was a sheet of printed card given away free at the end of a covered arcade a mile and a half away, and that the four figures on it belonged to four streets and the four street names were under them, and that the two together were the only bridge in Morrowfen between a person's own front door and a number.

"Then nothing goes in," said Ivy Pike, "and we have had this afternoon for a blank."

That is where the afternoon turned, and it turned because a man of forty-four who had been the district's engineer for nineteen years said, out of a folder, in the flattest voice anybody in that room had heard all day, that he was not prepared to send the sheet to adoption with nothing on it.

He wanted an eleventh item.

"It is a complaint," he said. "It is not a repair and it is not a rate and it is not a ratepayer's thing. It says: clock, public, in this district, found to be out of the district reference by more than one hour, report. Report to the works, on a form, in a book. That is all. I am not asking the works to mend a clock it does not own. I am asking it to be told. One hour is not an exact figure and I do not care: the branch is three hours and a bit and it has been stepping since the twenty-first of the fourth month and by the end of the programme it will be nearer five. Nobody is going to do anything about that. But somebody is going to be able to say, out of a book, that on a stated night a public clock in this district was more than an hour out, and a person in a street will be able to read that, and the reading is the thing. It costs a form and a book and about forty minutes a year in a clerk's time and it will be ignored, and I expect it to be ignored, and I want it on the schedule anyway, because in about four years the argument we are having this afternoon is going to have been had by nobody, and this is the only instrument in this district that can say *we knew in April*."

The objection came from the middle of the room and it came from a woman of thirty-eight who was not a member of anything and had come because she had heard there was a form being discussed.

"That is a form," said Wenna Pruitt. "And a form is a way of asking without the rule. I have written that sentence in a minute and I have had it read back to me twice and I am not going to pretend I have stopped believing it because it is convenient to be able to tell a ward in Green Span when a clock is an hour out."

"It is a form about a thing nobody can be refused on," said Nunn. "That is the difference and I will give you the whole of it because you are right and I would rather say it than have somebody find it in four years. A form with a person in it takes a person and says something to them. A form about a clock takes a clock. There is nobody at the end of it. It is the only form I have ever tried to write that has nobody at the end of it, and if I am wrong about that then the thing to do is to take it off again, and it will be easy to take off because it is item eleven and item eleven is a sheet anybody can scrape."

"It is thin," said Wenna Pruitt.

"It is thin," said Nunn. "I have been in this district nineteen years and it is the first thing I have proposed that I would describe as thin. Take it or leave it."

They took it. Fourteen days later, at the adoption sitting, item eleven went onto the schedule of the pumping district of the Lower Weirs and the Green Span hospital branch in a hand nobody in that room recognised, and the works' district secretary said afterwards that he had been doing the job for twenty-six years and had never before put a line on a schedule on the strength of a conversation he had been in, and that he had done it because the engineer had brought a form, and that a form is a thing you can look at afterwards.

Nobody thanked Hollis Nunn. He is the only man in this district whose name is on a published schedule and he was not thanked for item eleven, and he was not thanked for the refusal on item six, and both of those are on the same afternoon, and he said on the Monday that he had not expected to be and was not going to say so in a room, and that the useful part of a notice is not the asking.

---

The other half of that Saturday was at half past ten in the morning, before any of it, in two rooms over a chandler's shop in Bellweather Road, and it was not a meeting, because there was nothing to meet about, and there were two people in it and one of them was not a member of anything.

There is a coat on the hook behind the door of that room. There has been a coat on that hook since the seventh of the first month, when the person who wore it gave up an appointed post in nine lines and sat down on the other side of the table, and the coat is a good coat and it is not a warden's coat any more. In the inside pocket of that coat there is an envelope, and in the envelope there is a fee of forty pounds exactly and a draft memorandum in a folder, and the memorandum has one clause in it that anybody can read, and the clause is about representing the pumping district of the Lower Weirs in all questions concerning the water supply of that district.

The forty pounds has been in that coat since the Sunday after the Saturday in March on which six people met in a room over a tobacconist's in Weir Lane and decided not to do a thing and wrote down four reasons why not, and no company was formed, and the fee was not incurred, and it was held against the day.

On Saturday the first of the fourth month, at about half past ten in the morning, Ivy Pike looked at the coat on the hook and said, to nobody in particular, "That is still in there."

It was.

"There is forty pounds in that coat," said Ivy Pike, "and forty pounds is not money. It is a signature. A body could be formed on Monday for forty pounds and it would be a body with a director's name on a public register for ever, and a body is the only instrument in this city that can be asked a question on behalf of things, and there are six hundred standpipes in this district sitting on a list of properties that does not exist, and a body is a door into a list of properties, and I have thought about it for three weeks and I have decided that I am not opening that envelope and I would like it written down somewhere that I did not open it and did not want to."

Nobody asked her why not. There was nobody else in the room, and the person who was in the room was the council's organiser and had been in the room in March and had read the four grounds out twice, and she said, "I have got nothing to say about it and I am not going to say it, and the reason I am not going to say it is that the answer is four lines long and four lines is the shape of the thing we have been protecting since the fourteenth of the tenth, and if I say the four lines out loud in this room then somebody in nine days is going to write them down and put them in a minute and we will have extended a four-line undertaking about twenty-six doors into a rule about six hundred standpipes, and a rule is a form, and I have said that to three rooms this month and I am not going to say it a fourth time to a coat."

"So it sits in the coat," said Ivy Pike.

"It sits in the coat," said Wenna Pruitt, "and there is no form in this city on which anybody may be asked for it, and nobody has asked, and nobody is going to ask, and if somebody asks me in four years I am going to say that a woman looked at a coat and said she did not want to, and that will be the whole of the ground, and it will not be a good enough ground, and it is the one we have."

---

Sunday the second of the fourth month, the arcades were shut, and there was nothing in Morrowfen to attend, and the three boards stood on their walls with the notice on them, and the ward board outside the Weir Two pump house was read that day by about eleven people, which is the ordinary number, and the ward board at Green Span hospital was read by about nine before lunch, and a hospital worked, and nobody anywhere in this district sat down and decided anything.

On the Monday the about two hundred and eleven households in nine blocks, and the rest of the pumping district in proportion, received the ordinary bill. It is a rate and not a schedule, and the two have never been in the same book, and that is not an oversight in either of them.
