# Chapter 100 — Two Books

On Monday the ninth of the first month, in a room over a chandler's shop in Bellweather, seven people gave this district a right over what may be asked of the clocks of a street of twenty-two houses off Weir Lane, and the right is a right to refuse, and it is held by the Weirs tenant council, which is a body the district already had, on terms the district can be held to, which means the council may be refused by the same people it may refuse and there is nobody anywhere who may appeal either refusal; and a woman of sixty-one who signs for a gate chairing it and did not want it, and a warden of forty-four on it and not chairing it, and a man of thirty-two not on it, and a landlord of nine blocks and a tenant council's organiser of thirty-eight not on it, and nobody thanked; and on Thursday the twelfth, in the outer room of the pumping ward, standing at a ledge with a glass case on it and a key on a string and two books beside each other in two different hands, a woman of forty-nine who is the Director of the Cathedral and who has been named in this district once, on a signature, offered him the sealed rooms, or a sealed unit, or the machine, and he did not answer, and the offer is the last line of this and is neither taken nor refused.

---

**The room, on the Monday the ninth, at about half past seven in the evening, with a stove in it and a bell that has not worked since 1949 and a shelf of minute books in which the most recent volume is 1948, and no minute of any of this in any of them, because there is no book in that room for it and what there is instead is about two hundred yards away on a ledge in a pumping ward and anybody may read it.**

Seven members of the Weirs tenant council. The chair is a woman of sixty-one who signs for the Weir One gate and has read a gauge four times a year on a Sunday for eleven years and traced five offices and a sixth link that is not an office and named the one honest use of a chain, which is that a person who knows the chain can be walked, and that being walked is not the same as being refused.

"I do not want this chair," said Ivy Pike, "and I have said so twice and I am going to say it once more and then I am going to take it, and the reason I am going to take it is that I am the only one of the seven who has been to all five places and I know there is no sixth counter, **and a body that cannot be refused is not a body, it is a room, and I have been in this district for nineteen years and I would like there to be one thing in it that can be.**"

The seven, and two who are not on it and why:

**Sel Kade**, forty-four, warden of this district from 1938 to the first of September, and on the body since Friday the seventh, and not chairing it, and **she asked for a seat and not an office on the sixth and got it, and the difference is on the sheet and is the difference between a thing that can be asked a question and a thing that can be asked a question about water, and a tenant council can be asked about a room and a rent and a light on a stair and it cannot be asked one question about the water of this district, because a pumping district is not a body and has no standing under the water act.**

**Hanna Stroud**, fifty-four, nine blocks, about two hundred and eleven households, is not on it and gave the reason in one sentence before anybody asked her: "**Because I am a landlord, and the day a landlord is on a body that a tenant council has voted on, the body is a thing that can be got at, and I would rather be the person it can be got at from outside, and I would rather that by a good margin.**"

**Wenna Pruitt**, thirty-eight, organiser of that council for nine years, is not a member and has never been a member and is not going to start: "**I write down what this room decides. I have written it down for nine years in a book of my own and not in the minute book, because the minute book ends in 1948 and I did not ask why. If I am a member then I am a person who writes down the decision and is inside it, and I have watched four councils in this city do that and lose the only thing they had.**"

**Orrin Vale, thirty-two, is not on it, and was not on it, and did not ask to be, and the reason is one sentence and it is the reason a man of thirty-six said to him on a Thursday in the eleventh month at a counter and he wrote it down at the time.**

"Because a person who can be refused is the whole of the instrument, **and I am the man who cannot be refused, and that is not a gift and it is the reason nobody is going to help me, and it is also the reason I am not on that body and it is the only thing about this week I am proud of.**"

---

**The terms. Five of them, and the fourth one is the one that makes it a right instead of a permit, and it is the fourth one that nobody in that room argued about and everybody in that room noticed.**

> **1.** That no question may be put to this district, or to any person of it, about the clocks on Sluice Row, or the row's water, or any hour belonging to a person of the row, by any person outside this body, **unless the question has been put to this body in writing with a date on it, and this body has had the seven days that a schedule gets, and this body has answered in writing.**
> **2.** That this body may refuse. **A refusal binds the person who asked and nobody else.** It is not a form, it is not a resolution, it is not a vote, and it is not minuted as a decision of this body. It is entered in the row book and it is published with the minutes.
> **3.** That the minutes are published where anybody may read them, and **that no person is thanked in them**, and that the clause forbidding thanks is not a courtesy and is not a style and is the fourth clause of a process written in the back room of a shop in the fifth month by the woman who is standing at the back of this room with a book that is not a minute book.
> **4.** **That this body may not put a question to the row either.** That the same seven days' notice is given to the row that a schedule gets. That the row may refuse this body, in the same words, in the same book. **And that there is no office, court, committee, form or appeal in this city or any other to which either refusal may be carried, and that both of them stand.**
> **5.** That this right is a right to refuse and nothing else. That this body may not issue, grant, sell, lend, register or certify anything to anybody, and has no instrument for doing so, and **the word for the thing this body is not does not exist in this district and is not to be coined here.**

"It is a licence."

It was the man from the district works' accountancy, who is about fifty, and who had come about the third clause of a four-line letter dated the third of the instant, in which the low-level alarm on the ward's reserve was to be restored at the earliest convenience, and he had a piece of paper in his hand with a pencil on it and he was doing two things at once.

"**It is not a licence**," said Sel Kade, "and I know exactly what a licence is, because I have not had one in nineteen years and I have wanted one about half of them."

"Then it is a permit."

"**It is not a permit, and there is no permit in this city and there has not been one in my lifetime, and if anybody in this room has been waiting four months for one then that is what four months has been for and I am sorry for it and I am not going to pretend I am not.**" She had her hands flat on the table, which is a thing three people in this district do and has never meant anything. "A permit is a thing that a person gives you so that you do not have to answer anybody. **What has been made on Monday is a thing that makes you answer everybody.**"

"Then it is a certainty instrument."

"**It is not, and if anybody in this city wants one of those they should go and ask a man in a good coat what it is for and should be told that the original safeguards were written to make it impossible, and I have been on the wrong side of that for a decade and I have never once known what I was on the wrong side of.**"

"And is there a Council of Hours."

"**There is not a Council of Hours, there is not likely to be a Council of Hours, and if anybody in this room has been told there is one, they have been told it by a person who wanted something.**" Ivy Pike did not raise her voice. "And the fifth of those words, and the last I have got to say about it, is a thing that happened in this district twice and both times it was a person in the wrong mouth and both times there was a person standing in a doorway who corrected it, **and the words are not forbidden. They are only not true, and there is a difference, and a district that bans a word has stopped being able to say what it is frightened of.**"

The man from the works' accountancy put his pencil down and did not pick it up again.

"That will want a form," he said.

"**It will want a form and there is not going to be one, and when somebody comes in about seven years with a form for it they will be right, and the form will be about something else.**"

Then Wenna Pruitt read out the whole of the five clauses from the back of a 1948 minute book that is not a minute book, twice, and nobody thanked her, and nobody has thanked her this year, and she did not look for it.

---

**And on the Wednesday the eleventh, in a back room in Bellweather Road with one window in it and nine people in it, a woman of fifty-one told nine people a thing about a card in a box of bills, and nobody asked a child a single question, and there is no form in this city on which a person may be asked about a document in a thirteen-year-old's hands.**

The index went on sale at a penny on the thirty-first of the December and **Dorothy Vance, fifty-one, of Bellweather Road, who has kept a box of bills in a kitchen for nineteen years, bought a copy on the Monday the fourth of January with a shilling in her hand, because the card in her box of bills has been in it for four years and it came from a keeper of sixty-one in a public room on Cathedral Rise who wrote down what a girl of nine said to him because he is a man who writes things down, and it has a letter on the left of it and a diagonal stroke through a number on the right of it, and she did not know what it was and had not asked.**

"**I have not asked and I am not going to ask,**" she said, "and I am telling this room so that somebody else is holding the same piece of it. She is thirteen. I am her aunt and I am the responsible adult and **there is no form in this city on which responsible adult is a thing anybody can be, and I have been one for four years by accident and I am going to go on being one by accident, and the accident is a card in a box of bills.**"

"You telephoned the Archive."

"I telephoned the Archive on the Tuesday and a keeper of sixty-one told me three things and I wrote them down, which is what everybody in this district has been doing since the fourth of April."

> *One. The diagonal stroke is an execution mark and it is in the margin of the book because the batch was executed, and it is not a name and it is not a signature and it is not a person.*
> *Two. The countersignature on a line in that book is an office and not a name, and the office is a holder of the first class of the Clockwork Quarter hall, and there are four hundred and eleven of those countersignatures and every one of them is that office and no other.*
> *Three. And he said this, and he said it slowly, and he said it as a man who has been a keeper for a long time and has never once had to say it: **"The girl is not to be asked. If anybody asks her, including me, the answer is that nobody in this building is entitled to, and the price of a printed line is a penny and she is thirteen and she may buy one the same as anybody and nobody in this building has any business knowing whether she does."***

"She was not asked anything," said Dorothy Vance. "**She has not been asked a question since the twenty-first of the June and she is not going to be asked one by anybody, and I have said so in a room with nine people in it so that it is not one woman's promise in a kitchen.**"

And a man of thirty-two, who was in that room and who has a card in his own hand with a letter and a slash on it in a different kitchen about two miles away, wrote one line in his own book that evening with the date on it, and it is in a grey ledger in a shop in Verge Lane and it is worth nothing in any room that is not the one it is in, and it is this:

> *She is thirteen and she has a printed copy of four hundred and eleven lines in a house in Bellweather Road and the responsible adult is a woman of fifty-one and there is no form on which either of those is a thing, and the answer to the question nobody asked me is a document and a person, and the person is her aunt, and the document costs a penny, and I have been asked nothing about it and I am not going to ask. **The count of my own hand corrections is two and this is not one of them, and it is the sixth time this year that the correct thing was to write a thing down in a book in a shop and put nothing at the bottom of it.***

---

**And on the Thursday the twelfth of the first month, at about four in the afternoon, in the outer room of the pumping ward, a clerk from the Cathedral came up the canal road on foot at about half past three and stood in the yard for twenty minutes and gave a letter to Bevin Sarr and went back down the road, and the letter is four lines and it is on the Cathedral's own paper and it is signed.**

There is a case on the wall with a glass front and a key on a string on a nail, and the string is eleven inches long and the key opens a lock that is older than anybody in that room. Inside the case is a book, and the book has two sets of columns: **MINUTES and DISTRICT, carried for forty-three years and on the stair on Cathedral Rise in chalk every morning of the world, and NAME and DURATION, new, required by a one-line audit requirement of the Office of Public Reckoning, and the first entry under the new columns is VANCE, P., and the duration column beside it contains no figure, no word, no mark and no rule, and the five lines on the inside cover of that book say that an empty column is a statement that the interval was not recorded, that it is not an omission of that office, that it is not a mystery, and that any person who fills one in by inference commits an offence against that office and against the person whose name is in the column beside it.**

**And beside the case, on the same ledge, and not in it, there is a second book.** It is about a hundred and forty pages and it is bound in a piece of sacking and it is called *THE ROW: WHAT WE WERE ASKED AND WHAT WE SAID, AND WHAT IT COST,* and it is in a hand that nobody at the Office of Public Reckoning can read, and it has in it a timetable of houses and clocks and winders made house by house by going along and knocking, and twelve names and twelve clocks in thirteen houses, and nine households with no clock and no column, and five hours and three hundred minutes, and a paying district line with nothing in it, and a column for durations with nothing in it, and about sixty hours of winding of which five are the hours of one household in a court whose clock is wound by a person who does not live in it.

**The Office's figure is nowhere in it.** Not fifty-one thousand four hundred and eighteen, and not the four hundred and eleven, and not the range, and not a person.

**And at the head of that book, in the same five lines and the same shape as the five lines on the inside cover of the ledger a foot away, there is a line about a column with nothing in it, and it says that where no instrument recorded the duration the column is empty, and that the empty column is a statement and not an omission, and that any person who fills one in by inference commits an offence against the people in the column beside it.**

Two books. Two headnotes. The same rule, written twice, in two buildings, by people who have never met. And the ledge has a chair on it and a pencil and a jar of ink, and the case has a key on a string, and nobody in this city is asked to pay anything to sit down and read either of them.

He read the letter standing up, with the ledger in the case a foot from his left hand and the row book open on the ledge, and Bevin Sarr was in the room because the case has to be unlocked and the key is on a string, and nobody was thanked, and there is nobody in the pumping ward's outer room this afternoon who has been thanked for anything this year.

The letter is four lines and it is dated the eleventh of the first month, and it is signed **Anja Voss**, forty-nine, Director of the Cathedral, who has been named once in this district, on a signature, in the fifth month of the first year, and who has taken no scene and made no offer and been in no room since that signature until this afternoon, **and whose door has not been opened in this volume and is not opened now, and the Continuity Office is an inspecting authority and the Minister is not named in this district and is not named here.**

> *Mr Vale,*
> *You wrote to this office in the first year and it was answered by a form and you were right that the form was the answer and wrong about everything else, and I have read the four hundred and eleven lines since the fifteenth of the ninth and I have stood in the room on the Rise twice.*
> *The rooms under this building that are sealed, and the records in them, and the unit on the floor below the north gallery that is not described in any paper I have signed, and the machine itself — any one of those three, or all three, and on a day of your choosing, and with no condition and no form and nothing asked of you and nothing asked of you again.*
> *I am aware that the answer to a Reading is that a question must name an act and I am not asking you a question and there is no spindle in this letter and nothing here is a reading and nothing here is admissible, **and I am aware that a room is a form and a form is a way of asking without the rule, and this is a door and I cannot make anybody walk through it and neither can anybody in this building, and that is the whole of the difference between a door and a permit and it took me four years to be able to write that sentence down.***
> *If you would come on a Tuesday I will have the north door open.*
