# Chapter 121 — Four Hours and Ten Seconds

On Tuesday the twenty-eighth of the third month the line in the ward's own register, in the case on the wall of the second landing at Green Span hospital, read **four hours and ten seconds**, and Nella Rook read it standing up with a lamp in her hand because the case has no light in it, and she read it twice, and then she went and woke a sister.

The working is worth putting on the page because there is no rule in that building that says it has to be and because the thing it establishes is that nobody had to do anything at all.

The line for the fourth of the third month is in the same hand and reads three hours, forty minutes and ten seconds. There are twenty-four nights between the fourth of the third month and the twenty-eighth of the third month. The branch steps fifty seconds a night. Twenty-four times fifty is one thousand two hundred seconds, which is twenty minutes, exactly, and no more. Three hours, forty minutes and ten seconds with twenty minutes added to it is four hours and ten seconds.

She did not need a watch for that and she did not need a clockmaker and she did not need anybody in Weigh Lane to put a seal on it. The twenty minutes had arrived by itself, overnight, the way it has arrived every night since the twenty-first of the fourth month of last year, and on the morning of the twenty-eighth of the third month of this year it had carried the hospital branch past four hours for the first time, and the only person awake in the building who knew it was a hospital clock nurse of twenty-eight with a lamp and a cold hand.

Nobody in that building said anything about it for about an hour, because there was a ward round at seven.

---

What four hours does to a ward's arithmetic is not a thing anybody would guess from the outside, and the first thing it does is that it makes two books disagree.

The ward keeps the intervals on two watches. Two people, two watches, set against each other in front of the ward at every handover, and the difference written on the card: four seconds, six seconds, and eleven seconds at three in the morning. The free master on the landing has been fouled since before the second of the third month and it does not run slow, it lets go, and there is no rate to a thing that lets go, and every interval written off it after half past eleven is wrong by an amount that nobody in that building could write down and that four minutes an hour over the whole of a night does not describe.

That was true at three hours forty minutes and it is true at four hours and ten seconds, and it is not what changed.

What changed is the other book. The hospital keeps three records and reconciles them against each other, which is the whole of what a hospital clock nurse is for and it is a specialty that exists because the hospitals found out what a Reading costs. Three records: the ward's own card, the staff book, and the hospital's central entry, which is keyed to the line and not to the ward.

At three hours forty minutes ten seconds, a dose given at eleven at night on the ward's card was stamped at twenty to three in the morning in the central entry. At four hours and ten seconds, the same dose is stamped at three in the morning.

Which is the hour on the card with the eleven seconds in it.

"There is no number in this building that frightens me the way that one does," Nella Rook said, on the Wednesday morning, in the corridor, to a ward sister of about fifty who had been doing this for four years and had never once asked where the numbers came from. "Not the four hours. The three o'clock. Because at three o'clock the two watches differ by eleven seconds and I have got two records of that hour and they are four hours and ten seconds apart, and both of them are true, and the card does not say which is which, and it has never once had to say."

The sister of about fifty had the card in her hand. She turned it over and looked at the column, which is headed with a date, and which carries the total for the night, and the worst single interval, and the difference of the two watches written three times, and which carries nothing whatever about which of the two clocks any of the eleven intervals in it was read from.

"It has a column for the date," she said. "It has a column for the total. It has a column for the worst one and a column with the two watches in it three times, and it is four columns and every one of them is about a number of minutes, and not one of them is about a clock."

"You could put one in," said Nella Rook. "You could head a column *read off* and you would have two entries under every interval on the whole ward and you would know by the end of a week how many of the mistakes were mistakes and how many were arithmetic, and it would take a ward sister about an hour a day for a month and then it would not."

"That is the fourth column," said the sister of about fifty, "and you know what the fourth column is. It is the recommendation column. It is a third of an inch wider than the other two because the hall worked out in about 1908 that men will say anything at all if the box is big enough, and there has never been a column for *I am not sure* and there never will be, and a column headed *read off* is a column for *I am not sure*, only wider."

They were both right, which is a thing that happens about four times a year in that building and which nobody has found a procedure for.

---

The number the hospital wanted came out of this and it was not a clock number.

Leda Vale came in at about half past eight on the Wednesday and Nella Rook had written on the back of a requisition, in pencil, the thing she had been asked for by three people in two days, which was a number.

Not the branch. Everybody in the building knows the branch, and the branch is in the case on the wall and anybody may read it with a lamp.

"A number I can put in front of the board," said Nella Rook. "That says what I am allowed to do about it."

"Allowed by whom?"

"That is the whole of it," said Nella Rook. "There is no version of this I can take up there that does not come back with a question attached to it, and the question is always the same one, and it is always asked in the same tone by a decent man, and the tone is the reason I have not taken it up there. *Where did you get that?* And if I say a case on a wall, then I am a nurse with a wall, and the board does not want a nurse with a wall. It has never wanted one in its life. It wants a thing."

"The branch is a thing," said Leda. "It is in a case with a glass front and a line in a book in a hand."

"That is exactly it," said Nella Rook, and she was not being clever, and she was not building an argument, and she said the rest of it in the flat voice of a woman who had been thinking about it since the Tuesday morning. "I am not asking for a paper. I want to be able to say in a room, in front of people who have the power to take the line off this landing, that on the twenty-eighth of the third month the branch stood at four hours and ten seconds and that it is fifty seconds a night and that in a hundred nights it will stand at five hours and twenty-three minutes and thirty seconds. Fifty seconds a night times a hundred nights is five thousand seconds, which is eighty-three minutes and twenty seconds, and four hours and ten seconds with eighty-three minutes and twenty seconds added to it is five hours and twenty-three minutes and thirty seconds, and a rostering clerk can plan a winter against that, and a matron can put it in a diary."

"That is three things and a calendar," said Leda. "It is not a thing. It is the rate and a date and a sheet of paper with your own arithmetic on it, and any one of nine hundred nurses in this city could do that on the back of a requisition, and none of them is me and all of them are right."

"I know. That is why I am telling you and not asking you to do it."

"Then what do you want."

"I want to be told whether I am frightened of the right thing," said Nella Rook. "Because I have worked out what the answer to *where did you get that* has to be, and the answer has to be a piece of paper that somebody cannot alter on the day, and there is nothing in this city that is that, and I am not asking for one, and I am not asking you to go and get me one, and I would like it written down somewhere that a hospital nurse worked that out in April and did not do anything about it."

Nobody wrote it down anywhere.

And the sentence she had said, which was the whole of the ground, and which she said twice, once to Leda and once on the Friday to a man of thirty-two who had come in with a lamp he did not need, was this: that what a number needs in order to be acted on in a room is not to be right, and everybody in the building already knows it is right, and it is to be unalterable by the person who is asked about it. A rate and a calendar are alterable by any person who says *where did you get that*, because the answer is a person. A rate and a calendar with a hall's seal on it are not, and there is no such thing, and the only office in this city that has ever put a seal on a number about a clock is a licensing floor three quarters of a mile away, and it does not put seals on rates, it puts seals on machines, and a ward is not a machine, and if it were a machine it would be in a different building and would not have eleven patients on it.

"So in about six weeks," said Leda Vale, "somebody is going to ask for one."

"Probably sooner," said Nella Rook. "It will be somebody sensible. That is the part I have not got used to. Everything I have got used to wanting has been wanted by somebody I could have said something nasty about."

---

The rest of that week was arithmetic and it was checkable, and Orrin Vale did it in the grey ledger with the working out longhand because he no longer has a licence and has not had one since the twentieth of the third month and cannot put a name under a figure that certifies anything, and wrote the date at the top of the page and *no person thanked* at the foot of it in the way he has written it at the foot of a ledger page since the first year.

At the Weir Two pump house the line stood at three hours, five minutes and thirty seconds on the twentieth of the third month, and it steps fifty seconds a night, and from the twentieth to the thirty-first of the third month there are eleven nights, and eleven times fifty seconds is five hundred and fifty seconds, which is nine minutes and ten seconds, and three hours, five minutes and thirty seconds with nine minutes and ten seconds added to it is **three hours, fourteen minutes and forty seconds** on Friday the thirty-first of the third month, and the separation between the pump house and the ward's outer room is nine minutes and twenty seconds and has not moved by a second in a hundred and three nights, and the separation between the ward's outer room and the Quarter hill south is fifty-one minutes and fifty seconds and has not moved by a second either, and both of those are the reason a district can be argued about in a room and neither of them is a reason the district is right.

The branch on the Friday morning was four hours and two minutes and forty seconds, and Nella Rook wrote it in the register in the same hand as the line before it, and two hundred and forty households in four streets went on not knowing any of it, and the woman at number four in the second of them went on holding a brass disc that had been in a pocket since the seventeenth of the first month, and nobody in the hospital was thanked for the twenty minutes, because nobody in the hospital had done the twenty minutes.
