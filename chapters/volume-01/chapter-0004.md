# Chapter 4 — No Unseen Cost

He got to the Rise at half past one with the crest due tomorrow afternoon and the gate already standing open, and by the time he had found the Question Hall and stood in it, most of his afternoon was gone.

The Hall was the width of a church and a quarter as tall, with the counter along one side and the engine along the other, and between them a floor of iron grating over the lower works so that a person filing a question could stand in the middle and watch the machine that answered it. That was the argument the building made about itself, and it was a good argument, and it worked on the crowd that came up the stairs.

The Consequence Loom was not one machine. It was a city laid out in brass and glass and moving water: water columns the height of a man for the rivers and the canals; weighted arms for the bridges, each one riding on its own line and dipping; copper paths branching across the whole width of the floor for the clock network, with small travelling beads running along them; and behind all of it the drums, which were schedules, and the drums were the part the public never looked at, because a drum is not beautiful and a bridge arm is.

The queue was eleven people long. Orrin took his place at the back of it and worked out the vocabulary by listening, and by eleven forty he had learned that *the Loom's mind* was a thing people said, and that it was said by people who had read the display, and that the display said nothing of the kind.

The woman in front of him, a tram conductor's union man in a wet cap, reached the counter and asked the clerk whether the district should be flooded to save the hospital.

The clerk did not argue. She put his slip into the spindle.

The spindle was a brass throat about the size of a hand's width, set in the counter at chest height, with a slot above it and a small window below, and Orrin watched a man try to say *should* into it. There was a dull sound, like a coin dropped on a table that is padded, and a strip of paper came up out of the slot with four words on it.

*QUESTION NOT ACCEPTED. NO NAMED ACT.*

"Write it as a thing you'd do," the clerk said, not unkindly. "A gate, a schedule, a load. Open this at this hour. Close that ward. That's what it takes."

The man stood there with his cap in his hands. "That's not the same question."

"No," the clerk said. "It isn't. Sorry."

He did not file it. They all filed those, eventually — somebody would come back with *if the north embankment sluice is closed at eleven and the tide is at the fourth mark, does the lower canal clear before two* — and it would go through, because it named a thing that could be done. Orrin watched two of them do it in the twenty minutes he waited, and understood that the machine could not be asked whether a thing was right, and that this was the most careful thing about it, and that it was also the reason it could be used for anything at all.

---

His turn came at ten past two.

"Vale. Orrin Vale. Two benches, Verge Lane, class three."

The clerk's stylus did not move. "Have you got a district proposal?"

"No."

"Then you're a private citizen and you go in on the private sheet, and the private sheet is four minutes further back in the day, and the fee is the same."

"Fine."

"And a private citizen's question is answered on private terms. It doesn't go into the week's board and it isn't argued by anybody before it runs. It goes in and it runs." She wrote *private citizen* on the top of a long printed sheet and squared it to the counter. "You'll want to write it in, not say it. The spindle takes a thing that was done, not a thing that was asked."

He wrote it in. The sheet wanted the action, the place, the hour, and the question, and it wanted it in that order, and it wanted the words *and the consequence of* because the machine worked on completions.

> *If Weir Two is opened now, and the head is at the third white mark and rising, does Ferrywright Bridge stand until the head passes?*

"And the fee," the clerk said.

"The fee is charged to a district," said Orrin. "Whichever the engine draws from."

"You name it."

He had a district of his own. It was Clockwork Quarter, and he lived in it, and he had been in it since he was four, and if the sheet had asked for the district affected he would have had to write *the Weirs* and then argue for an hour with a man about whether a clockmaker had a standing in a district he did not live in.

So he wrote the Quarter.

He was aware of what he was doing while he was doing it, and he was aware of it clearly: he was writing the name of the district with the fattest reserve in the city, the district that would feel a draw of three minutes the way a full tank feels three pints, the district that would be behind by three minutes for a day and would be evened up by noon tomorrow without anybody losing anything. He was not writing the name of a district that wanted the answer. There was no line on the sheet for that. There was no line on the sheet for that, and no rule anywhere required one, and the whole of the Compact that had been agreed forty-three years ago did not contain a single word about whether the district that pays must be the district that asked, or the district that is hurt, or a district at all that has an opinion.

He signed it and pushed it across and felt exactly as bad as he expected to feel, and the amount of feeling was nowhere near enough to stop him, and he noticed that too.

Then the sheet came back with two more things on it.

*The fee will be drawn from the named district's reserve. The draw is in proportion to the reserve held. — Office of Public Reckoning, notice 11-C.*

*Witnesses are required at the rail. Three. Operator, affected district, record keeper. A district standing may be entered in place of an affected resident.*

He had never filed a question in his life and he read those two lines eleven times.

---

The rail was the length of a dining table and made of the same brass as the counter, and it had three stations on it, and each station was a plate with a hinged cover and a slot and a place to lay a wrist.

Pell was at the first one, operator, in the same dry grey coat, with the flat unhurried hands laid out on the brass like a man cutting bread. There was a band round his left wrist, dull, a little worn, the width of a finger: a witness bond, and every person in the room who had ever seen one knew what it was for, which was that if the engine had to take an interval out of somebody it would be allowed to take it out of the man at the rail.

"A private citizen," Pell said, reading the sheet upside down. "You get the same machine and none of the arguing, which is the whole of the difference and the whole of the privilege." He took the sheet and turned it the right way round. "Weir Two. Ferrywright. You want the whole afternoon."

"I want the answer before the light goes."

"Then you have until the light goes, and I would not spend the last hour of it in a queue." He held up the sheet. "Read your own margin."

Orrin read his own margin. The sheet said the trace was valid *for the conditions and records present when the question was entered,* and everything after that was a formality.

"A Reading is a model," Pell said, in the voice of a man who has said it a thousand times and has meant it once. "It is not a promise and it is not a prophecy and the Loom cannot tell you what people will do. What it can tell you is what these machines will do, today, to the water and the load and the clocks, given what we have written down about them. Everything it knows, it knows because somebody wrote it down, and I mean down. Not remembered. Down." He tapped the sheet with one finger. "So before we start. If you have a question about a thing somebody has not written down, you do not have a question. You have a hope."

At the second station sat a young man in the record office's dark green, with a ledger the size of a paving stone, who took the sheet, copied the whole of it into the ledger in a small tidy hand, and said his name was Ostrand and that he was here to keep it, and that he could not be asked a single question and did not wish to be, which Orrin respected more than anything else that happened on the Hill all day.

The third station was the affected district.

"The Weirs," the record clerk said, and had the form out, and Orrin's stomach went down through the floor, because he knew what was coming before it came.

"No standing, sir."

"They have four hundred thousand people."

"They have no standing, sir. What they have is a pumping ward with a schedule and no ordinance. A standing is a written claim that a district's interests are represented in a question, and the Weirs do not have one, and there is no mechanism to make one, and the board has not had one since the forties." The clerk turned to Pell. "It is a Green Span question, Mr Pell, if you want a resident."

"It's a Weirs question."

"It's a Green Span question for the purposes of the rail, and here is a Green Span resident."

And a woman of about fifty-six got up out of the bench along the wall, where she had been sitting with her handbag on her knees, and came forward, and Orrin understood then that she had been sitting there since before he arrived, because people sit there, because the Hall is open to the public and there is a bench and the engine is worth watching.

"Alma Festinger," she said, to him, pleasantly. "Fenwick Road. I'm on the public-health rota, Wednesdays, so somebody had to be asked, and I'm here. Is there a fee? Somebody said there might be a fee."

"There is not," said the record clerk. "You are identified and you sign and you may leave after the notice is read."

"May I leave after?"

"If you wish."

"Well, I shall stay." She smoothed her coat. "My sister's house is on the far side of Ferrywright. She's seventy-one and she doesn't cross it in weather. I'm glad something's being done about the bridge, whatever else."

They put a bond on her wrist. Pell did it himself, without fuss, the way a man puts a coat on a stranger's shoulders, and told her what it was: that if the calculation ran deep enough it would need a moment out of a living person to run at all, that she would feel nothing she could call anything, that she would come round, and that in the forty years he had done this she had not known anybody to come back changed. Alma Festinger said that was a comfort and that her sister would be insufferable about it, and settled her handbag on her knee.

The submitter's wrist was bound too. Orrin felt the band close and thought: *and me*.

The clerk lifted all three covers.

"Question received," said the record keeper, Ostrand, in a voice that had clearly said it several hundred times and had never once been bored by it.

---

The Loom did not answer. That was the first thing Orrin had to unlearn, and it took the best part of an hour.

What the engine did was make the city happen several times. The water columns came up together and began to move, and the bridge arms began to answer them, dipping and lifting as if under load, and the copper beads ran out along their lines and stopped, and ran again, and stopped. It was not fast. It was the speed of a careful man doing sums, which is faster than a careful man writing them down and slower than a person waiting. Two of the columns went over hard and one of them went over slowly and the difference between the two was the difference between the gate opening now and the gate not opening, and the machine showed him both of those at once, in brass, without comment, and let him watch.

Above him, the Cathedral's public clock lost a beat and took it back.

Twenty minutes. Thirty-five. The drums turned; a small window over the counter showed the draw going out along the network, district by district, and one of the lines was labelled in small engraved letters, and it was *Clockwork Qtr.*

A tram signal two streets down the Hill came in late and went out late, and did not apologise.

The notice came up out of the spindle on a long strip of pressed paper, and Ostrand read it aloud because that is what a record keeper is for.

```text
QUESTION RECEIVED
TRACE: Opening Weir Two now holds Ferrywright Bridge through the crest.
PRESSURE: Weir Two's second stage will not stop before midnight, and the hospital's night clock slips with it.
BLIND MARGIN: Unregistered night work is not represented.
TOLL: Three district minutes and a witness interval.
```

He read it twice, the way the form requires, and turned the strip so that the room could see it, because that is also what the form requires, and eleven people who had come up the Hill to find out something read it over his shoulder in the space of a breath.

The type under the rule was half the size of the type above it, which was the blind margin, which was a required line on every notice the Cathedral had ever printed, and which was set below the rule in small foot and carried no name, no address, and no shift, and read: *unregistered night work is not represented.*

"Third station, affected district." Pell's hand was on the cover. "Read it or record it, madam."

Alma Festinger leaned in and read it. "Unregistered night work is not represented," she said. "What does unregistered mean?"

"It means work nobody has written down," Pell said. "For the machine. Not for the law."

"Oh." She thought about it for a moment with real seriousness, because she was a woman who took a rota seriously. "Then somebody should write it down."

Pell said, "Yes," and Alma Festinger signed the rail, and Orrin thought: *not today.*

The record keeper read the toll.

"Toll. Three district minutes and a witness interval."

Three minutes. He had known it would be a number and not a sum of people, and three was small enough to be a rounding error in a district that could afford one, and it was the smallest thing that had ever been spent on the survival of a bridge, and he had spent it, out of the Quarter's clocks, without one person in that hall, including himself, having asked him to.

"Question run and answered," said Ostrand, and closed the ledger with a sound like a door in another building, and unhooked his wrist band and put it in its box.

The countersignature on the copy that went down the pneumatic tube to the Lower Weirs pumping ward was the Directorate's, in a hand he had not seen before and would not see again, and the name at the foot of it was printed in the small type of a form.

*Anja Voss, Director.*

He looked at it for the length of time it took to understand that he was looking at a woman's name on a piece of paper, and that it had told him nothing at all, and that he would go on thinking about the strangeness of that for a long time afterward without ever learning a single useful thing about her.

---

The Toll Stair was the one place in Morrowfen where people stopped to read things, and it was full.

The stair went up out of the Hall into the daylight in three flights, and the wall beside the middle flight carried the week's account in chalk on a slate: readings, days, minutes, and beside each line the district the minutes had been drawn from. Forty-three years of that stair, and the arithmetic on it had never once been in doubt. Eighteen minutes, Northglass, a Tuesday. Two minutes, Green Span. Six minutes, Weirs, in the spring, against two minutes, Green Span, the same week, which was a district whose reserve was very nearly empty and which therefore paid more for less, every time, and had done for twenty years.

And on the slate, under the day, in a fresh clean hand:

*3 — Clockwork Quarter.*

Three minutes off the best clocks in the city.

The man in front of Orrin on the stair was reading it, and so was a woman behind him, and the woman said, "Well, that's the least anybody's complained about this week," and the man said, "That's the Cathedral clock, that is," meaning it as a compliment, meaning that if the Cathedral's own clock could give three minutes to save a bridge, then three minutes was nothing, and they went up into the rain pleased and a little proud of the city.

Orrin stood in front of the slate and worked out, the way he worked out everything, with numbers, because numbers at least pretended to be the same shape as the truth.

The Quarter held one of the largest reserves in Morrowfen. The draw was in proportion to what a district held. So a district with a full reserve paid a rounding error, and a district with an empty one paid a fraction of itself, and the Weirs, whose reserve had been scraped down to nothing over twenty years of work nobody was paid for, would have paid a large piece of what they had left for this same question, and would have been asked nothing either way.

The name on the question was the name on the bill. Nobody had made him do it. Nobody had made anybody do it. There was no rule that said otherwise, because in forty-three years no one had ever needed one, and the Compact's fourth safeguard said only that the toll be recorded, and it was recorded, in chalk, in the middle of a wet street, in the correct hand.

Three minutes. The smallest receipt in the building.

And the notice, upstairs, where the machine had spoken and the witnesses had signed and Alma Festinger had said somebody should write it down, said nothing whatever about who had paid. It gave a quantity and an interval. It could not have told him, because he had not told it, and the hall would not have asked him, and a man on a bench two districts away had paid three minutes for a bridge he had never heard of, in a district that would forget it by Thursday.

The real figure was larger. He knew it was larger, the way you know a sound was louder than the room let out. Three minutes off a clock is a number. What he had actually spent was the private hour of a witness who had come for a Wednesday rota, and the third of Alma Festinger's attention on a wet afternoon, and whatever a night stage would take from a man who was going to be on a platform at half four in the morning.

He could not write any of it down. There was no column in Morrowfen for it. The best available column was the one in the middle of the stair, and it said *3*, and it was right, and it was the whole of the truth, and it was two words long and one of them was the name of a district that was not hurt.

Orrin went down the Hill in the rain. In the first street he passed, a man outside a chandler's shop had his own watch in his hand and was holding it up against the window clock, and the window clock said a quarter past three and the watch said a quarter past four, and the man said, *that's the second time this week*, and put the watch away and went in, cheerful, because a man in a good coat being slightly wrong is not a thing anybody minds. A shift bell in a yard three streets off let go three minutes late and the men in that yard came out on the old hour anyway, because a bell is not a clock, and nobody in Morrowfen has ever watched one.

He had been right. That was the part he kept coming back to on the walk, unable to put it down and unable to be pleased with it. He had been right, at the age of thirty-two, on his first question in his life, about a bridge, and a crowd in a hall had been pleased, and a woman with a hat pin had said she was glad something was being done about Ferrywright, and the engine had said it plainly and truthfully and the Loom had shown him both worlds side by side in brass, and the toll had been three minutes from a district with too much and a witness from a district with a rota, and a man forty-one with red hands had stood in a pump house forty feet under that water and told him that the clock said the valve was shut.

Three minutes. The whole price of a bridge.
