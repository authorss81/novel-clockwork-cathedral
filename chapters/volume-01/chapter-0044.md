# Chapter 44 — One Reserve and Two Districts

It came up the canal the way weather does in this city, which is to say that the wind got there before the rain and the rain got there before the water, and by two in the afternoon of Tuesday the sixteenth of the fifth month there was a running sea across the towpath from the Ferrywright bend to the weir steps and about four feet of extra water against the trestle's east pier, and the whole of the canal front was indoors with its lights on at three in the afternoon.

The front had four draws that night because nine men with a property qualification had given it four on the seventeenth of the fourth, and the fourth draw was at six in the evening, and at half past six Bevin Sarr stood in front of the ward board with the tide sheet in her hand and a pencil behind her ear and said the thing she has said four times a month for eleven years, which is the hour.

The tank was under two days at the start of the month and it was one and a half days on the sixteenth, and there was nobody in that district who was alarmed about a tank, because a tank in the pumping ward is a thing a man looks at on a fixed day.

At about eleven o'clock that night the wind dropped for about four minutes, the way it does, and the trestle's east span made a noise that Bevin Sarr heard from four hundred yards away through a pump house wall.

It was not a bang. She was very particular about this afterwards and she made three people write it down separately and compared the three accounts, which is a thing she learned to do from a man who learned it from nobody. It was a crack, and then about two seconds of a hiss, and then a longer hiss that did not stop.

The cast fixing let go because cast fixings let go, and the load went onto the third fixing alone, and the third fixing was a bracket of a pattern that had not been made in thirty years, in grey iron, with a bright spot the width of a hair where the load had found the metal.

The conduit joint at the far end of the run blew at about eleven o'clock and the hospital's clock pressure went out of a mile and a quarter of pipe in about four minutes, and the clocks on that branch stopped being driven and started being free-running, and in a building four miles away a girl of nineteen who has been paid at the ward rate to do a clock by hand for eleven weeks had a telephone call and put on a cardigan.

Bevin Sarr telephoned Vale's Bench at about half past eleven. There is one number on that shop's card and it is in a book in a cupboard and it is a number nobody has had to use in an emergency before, because a shop with two benches and a lease is not in anybody's emergency list, and the man who answered it was the man whose name is on the card.

He was on the crossing at twenty past one in the morning.

He went down the slipway in boots he did not own, which were in a boot locker on the landing at the ward for flood work and which he put on because his own were on his bench in Verge Lane, and he went down them because the stair from the bank is eleven feet above the towpath and on the sixteenth night the towpath was the bank.

The joint had blown about six feet beyond the third bracket and the pipe was spitting a fine grey jet into the dark at an angle you could not have put a hand in front of, and the water was four feet deep on the deck boards and going.

He got his hand on the pipe and asked it one question in plain words, out loud, on a wet bridge at twenty to two in the morning with the wind on his back, which is the practice and which is the thing that has cost him a minute, a hand, a ring, and thirty-eight minutes in four months.

*If that joint is closed tonight, what happens to the load on the third bracket tomorrow.*

And it came up the copper and it was short and it was not a vision and it had no light in it: the clamp, and then the bracket, and then the joint, and then the whole east span going into the canal, and after that nothing in the picture at all except a ward clock two minutes fast and a bell that rang by itself, which is the same picture as the last time and it is going to be the same picture every time until something changes, and nothing changes, because he did not change it.

He took his hand off and said, out loud, to nobody, in the rain, on a bridge:

"Right. Measure it."

And then he did the thing he came within about eleven minutes of doing, and it is the correct thing, and it is what a good repairer does, and it is the thing that killed a man in this district in the first month of the year.

He could close the joint. A saddle clamp, a split band, two bolts and a strap, forty minutes with the tools he had in the roll, in the dark, in a flood, on a bridge, with nobody watching, and the ward would have its pressure back before three, and Elsie Cobb could go off the ward clock, and the three clocks in that building would be three clocks with a grip again, and none of that would be in any form in any office in this city, and no person in four districts would ever know that anything had happened, and the front would carry on winding its clocks by hand until six in the morning, because the front has to be wound by hand whatever happens, and the front would not know why.

And the load would stay on the third fixing, because closing a joint does not take a load off a bracket, and the bracket is in single shear, and in four months' time the fixing would go and take the clamp and the conduit and the line with it and there would be nothing on the earth with a name on it.

He got the drift out. He got it into the third fixing's second hole, because you can feel a hole that is there and you cannot feel one that has not been drilled, and there was a hole there, and he leaned on the drift for about nine minutes with the water round his knees and got it to within a quarter of an inch, and he was thinking about the plate — not the shunt plate, the other one; a sacrificial plate, flat, soft, fourpenny iron, on the front of the bracket so the load finds iron to bend instead of a hole to grow — and he had a piece of soft iron in his roll and a hammer and he could have it on in an hour.

And at about ten to three he put the hammer down on the deck and stood there with the rain coming off the parapet in a sheet and worked out the whole of it, in about nine seconds, standing up, the way a man works out a sum.

*If I close this joint tonight, then the hospital gets its pressure and the ward gets its clock and nothing anywhere says a word, and in four months a bracket that is carrying a load in single shear on a casting with a bright spot in it comes off a canal crossing in the dark and takes a line with it, and there will be no plate and no name and no number and no room, and I will have spent the four months by doing a competent job for nobody, in the dark, where it cannot be objected to, because there is nobody in a position to object and the only reason there is nobody in a position to object is that the district cannot be asked.*

*And the last time this district settled a thing in a room with nobody in it and no rail, a man went down a ladder on his own initiative to close a valve the model had already said was shut, and the pump that should have stopped did not stop, and I was on the floor above him at a quarter to two in the morning with a certificate in my hand and no standing on that main, and no lamp, and nobody who could say no.*

*And I have the numbers. That is the whole of the difference between this and the first month of the year and it is not a better man.*

He got back to the pump house and rang the ward at ten past three and said one sentence to Bevin Sarr, who took it down, and it is in the ward's night book in her own hand, and it is the only line in that book about anything anybody has ever promised anybody:

"Ask the people. I am not doing it tonight. Ring Weir One, ring Ivy, ring the works' night man, and get Mrs Kade, and get the hospital, and tell Leda Vale to come down, and say that a licensed man is on the canal crossing with a drift in a bracket and is asking for a room in about twenty minutes, and that if nobody comes he is going to go back and put a clamp on it, because that is what he does."

They came. It took them fifty minutes and it is the longest fifty minutes of the batch.

---

The pump house floor at four in the morning in a flood, in a flood lamp and a paraffin lamp and a lamp on a stand that Marta Dey would not let anybody take off her, with nine people in it, and the rain going over the roof in a sheet you could not hear a person think through.

He stood in the middle of it, because that is where a pump house is loudest and because he wanted the words to be the same in four different people's mouths afterwards, and he said four things, and the four things were not the same as the four things he said in the second month of the year, and he had thought about that on the crossing for an hour and had got it right.

"One. I can close the joint. I have a drift in the second hole of the third fixing and I can have a clamp on the pipe before three. It is forty minutes' work and it will hold for weeks and it will hold under a load that is in one fixing, and I am not going to do it tonight without a room, and I am telling you that I want to, so that nobody in this room can say afterwards that I pretended I did not.

"Two. What I do not know. I do not know how long the ward can be on free-running clocks, and that is not my figure and it belongs to a woman who is standing near the door, and I am not going to say her number for her.

"Three. Which of this is a guess. The number I have is a number off a coping stone. Any person with a rule and a level can check it in nine minutes. I have no instrument in this city that will tell me how much longer that bracket lasts, and I have asked it with my hand on the pipe and what it gave me is the same four things it gave me in the second month, and it is not a certificate and it cannot be defended and it costs me every time I do it.

"Four. What it costs. The bracket is on line seven of a published schedule at two pounds fourteen shillings and sixpence in programme year three and I have read that line about four times a week for a month. It has been carried forward twice. It is the furthest forward it has ever been and the furthest forward it has ever been is the third time it has been deferred, and the reason it is on a sheet at all is a woman in an office who beat a document on its own terms in eleven minutes, and none of that has put a single hour into anybody's ledger, and all of it is why there is a crack in a bracket on a bridge at four in the morning instead of a foundry in the Sloughs with a Monday on it."

He stopped there and let the pump house make its own noise for a while, because he had never once in his life been able to do the last part without it and it is not a technique, it is just waiting.

Leda Vale said, "Then I will say the number, and I am saying it in this room and not in a corridor, and there are seven people in here and two of you are not going to understand a word of it and I do not care, because I am not spending it out of a corridor in a fortnight's time.

"Forty minutes. That is the total slack in that ward between the free clocks and the line clocks, across eleven patients and three staff, and it is forty minutes on the best night of the year. It is not a philosophical figure and it has never been a philosophical figure and I have asked for a column for it four times.

"From tonight it is not spent, it is *gone*. My clocks are free and they are going to run free until somebody gives them a grip, and every hour of that is a dose drawn at a moment the building has no opinion about. We have three clocks and they will disagree, and the pharmacy will compare them by hand every four hours, and Elsie Cobb will do the ward clock by hand and be paid for it at the ward rate, and I have written that down, and her name is on the card, and nobody took it off.

"Six days. If it is six days then I need two hundred and forty minutes of slack and I have forty, and I have said the number out loud in a room with seven people in it at four in the morning on the seventeenth of the fifth month, and if that ward does not have its grip by the twenty-first I am going to be writing to somebody I have never written to, and I have got the address because I keep the address of every office in this city in a book, and it will not be a letter I enjoy writing."

Ambrose, who had come out from the works' night man and who had been standing by the door in a coat over pyamas with his hat in his hand the whole time, said one sentence on his way in, and it was the sentence that finished it, and he said it before anybody asked him anything.

"There is no order for any of this. There is a permit to inspect and it says a maintainer may take measurements, and a clamp is not a measurement. A clamp is a repair on a works' asset at two in the morning in a flood by a man with no order and no standing and a certificate nobody can rely on, and when the inspector comes out in the spring there will be a steel band on this bridge with no maker's name and no date and no paper attached to it, and he will write *unauthorised* and he will be right, and then the next one that comes off will not be a clamp, it will be this span, and there will be a hole in the file where a decision was."

"Which is the same as the ward's clock," said Leda Vale.

"Which is the same as the ward's clock," said Ambrose, "and you worked that out about a fortnight ago and wrote it in a card in your own building, and I have read it, because it is on a board in your building and I go past your building."

He sat down on the bench by the second stage with his hat on his knee and looked at the water coming under the door.

"Nowhere has a column for a district," he said. "I have been a clerk in that building eleven years and I have looked for it and it is not there and I have said so to a man in a shop in the Clockwork Quarter and he wrote it down, and this morning I have found out that the reason it is not there is that the district would have to be a person, and the person would have to be at a meeting, and there is no meeting. So: what does a man do with a bridge at four in the morning."

---

The room took until about ten to five. It did not take long, because there was very little in it, and the reason there was very little in it is that nobody in the pump house had a better idea and the four of them who had one had all said it out loud already.

Nobody touched the bridge that night, and that was the first decision and it was Ambrose's and he made it in eleven words: *it is a flood, it is night, and there is no order, and I will not have an unauthorised repair on our iron in weather.*

The front's clocks were hand-wound through the night and through the morning, and the men whose hour begins at five began at six, and at ten to five on the morning of the seventeenth of the fifth month Bevin Sarr wrote it in the ward's night book with the names against it, in front of nine people, and read it out twice, and that book is a painted board and a copybook and it is not a record of the guild and it is not a record of the works and it is in a pump house on the canal road, and in the second month of this year the same six hours were spent by the same men and the same woman and not one hour of it was written down anywhere by anybody, and there was no room.

That is the whole of what was won on the canal crossing on the night of the sixteenth, and it is one page of a night book with nine signatures or marks beside it, and it cost a hundred and forty thousand people an hour each of the morning of the seventeenth of the fifth month, and it is the first time in this district's history that the cost of a decision was in a book in a room where the people who paid it were standing up.

The second night was the eighteenth and the third was the nineteenth, and the ward ran free on all three, and the cost of that belongs to a woman in Green Span and it is on a card in the nurses' room with a date on it and four names on it, of which one is a girl of nineteen who is being paid at the ward rate and who has asked twice to be named and has not been taken off.

---

The tank was the thing that nearly finished it, and it nearly finished it on the Friday, in daylight, in the ordinary way, with a dip stick.

Sel Kade read it herself at about eleven on the morning of the eighteenth, in front of two witnesses, because it is her tank and her duty and because the alarm on it has been inhibited since the second of the third month and under the district works' own tables an inhibited reserve alarm requires a report and no report has been made. She read it, and she wrote the reading in her own book with the hour on it, and she told the room about half past eleven, and nobody in the room had known.

A ward of eleven thousand people draws about four thousand gallons a day out of that tank, and the canal front has been drawing off the same main four times a night since the seventeenth of the fourth because nine men with a property qualification said so and could not ask anybody, and the tank was at four thousand on the sixteenth, which is one day, and about seven thousand on the eighteenth, which is one and three-quarter days, and the district works' own tables say two.

The reason it rose at all is that the make-up stopped, because the hospital's feed was dead, and a tank fills faster than it empties when the biggest thing drawing off it goes off the line by accident. For about thirty-six hours that tank was filling instead of emptying, and it was the only good thing that happened in the whole of that week, and it was the good thing happening for the worst possible reason, and it is the only reason the ward's reserve has not been reported empty since the second of the third month, and nobody in this city has told the district works anything.

The works' tables took the diversion away on the nineteenth.

That has to be set down properly because it is the only time in this batch that an institution does the right thing, and it does it because a table requires it and not because anybody said anything. The low-level alarm on the pumping ward's reserve has been inhibited since the second of the third month. The ward's reserve was at one and a half days. The district works' tables say that an inhibited reserve alarm requires a report, and that a reserve below two days requires the diversion to be ended and the reserve reported, and that is the whole of the rule and it is eleven years old and it is on a card in the district works' yard.

So on the Friday morning the nineteenth the diversion from the ward's reserve to the Carrow Street main was ended by the district works, on the works' own tables, in a form, with a reference number, and the ward's reserve was reported, and the galvanising and wire works on the Carrow Street main was told in a telephone call that its water goes off at noon, and there is a certificate dated the twenty-ninth of the second month on a file four feet away that says the main is not to be shut.

Ivy Pike took it in the pump house and put it on the table face up.

"Sixty-one of them live in this district," she said, "and they have just had their water taken off by a form on a table, and I have got the pay book, and I am going to be honest with this room: I do not know whether that is justice or the loss of ninety-one jobs. I have watched a works close for want of water in a winter and two of its men go to the docks and not come back, and I know what a rate rise costs because I have got thirty-one households in it and I have seen what thirteen shillings a year does to a woman with two children, and I am not going to stand in this room and pretend one of those is bigger than the other. It is the same size. That is the whole of what I have got."

Nobody argued with her, and Bessie Crane was in Green Span and did not argue with her, and the two of them had been asked to choose three times this year and had refused three times.

And the tank began to come back, and the make-up for the hospital had not begun, because the hospital's line was still dead, and the front was still drawing four times a night because the schedule said four and a schedule is binding and protects the front from the spring tide, and the spring tide was still making on the Friday afternoon, and the committee cannot be asked, and there is no form on which eleven hundred people at the bottom of a canal can be asked whether they would rather have water in the spring or a hospital's clocks in the summer.

So there was one tank, at about a day, doing two jobs, with a dead line on a bridge and a pattern of 1911 in a drawer in a works' store on the canal road, and the estimate on line seven of a published schedule was **two pounds fourteen shillings and sixpence**, and it had been that figure for two years and three months, and it was the cheapest thing on the sheet.

"The foundry works Saturdays," said Ambrose, from the door, with his hat in his hand, because he is a man who has been standing in a room for three days and has not once sat down. "And the original drawing is in the store behind me with the tramway's name in the corner and the year on it, and nobody has had it out since it went in, and the pattern comes off that and not off the casting, because the casting is what we are taking out and a pattern off a casting is a pattern of the fault."

"Saturday the twentieth," said Orrin Vale. "At ten, in your yard, in daylight, with the bracket on a bench and the number on a board, and anybody who wants to come and check the number with a rule and a level can come, and if the works want to do the work at night then the works does the work at night and I will not be on that crossing at night again, and if nobody will do it at night then it does not get done at night and it gets done on a Tuesday in the autumn in the rain with a notice, and I will be the one who signs that."

He had said it in a room with nine people in it at about four in the afternoon on the Friday the nineteenth of the fifth month, and the only new thing in that was that he had said it out loud in front of people who could refuse it, and being able to refuse it was the only thing that had ever been new about him.
