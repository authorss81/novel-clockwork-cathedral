# Chapter 7 — Three Minutes

The clock on Halloway Street was a pillar clock, cast iron, painted municipal green, and it said twelve minutes past ten when the sun was actually on the horizon in the other direction.

Orrin got there at ten past eight with the receipt in his case and a lamp and a bar, and the Office of Public Reckoning's investigator was already standing at the foot of it in a good coat with her hood down, holding a piece of chalk and looking up.

"Venn," she said. "You're earlier than the girl said. Good. Do you know what I am?"

"You're the girl who sent for me."

"I'm the woman who sent for the girl." She held out her hand and did not smile and it was not unfriendly. "We audit the Toll Stair. Not the Cathedral, not the Assembly, not the water. The stair. The stair is a public display of minutes and districts and it is the only part of that machine anybody can check, and so it is the part this office was given, and the office has existed eleven years and has four staff and a written right to be ignored that is printed on my letter of appointment. Mr Vale, that clock is five minutes slow. Your certificate is in my bag. Walk me round it."

He walked her round it. It took ten minutes and it told her everything and told him nothing new: the movement was sound, the escapement was clean, the weights were correct for the hour, the line had come through a district draw and a clock that had been corrected by hand had been corrected *in the wrong direction* on top of it.

"Here," she said. "When they came to put it right, they wound it three minutes forward, because the file said three minutes. It was five slow. So they wound it forward three, because the file says three, and it is still two behind where it belongs. That may be a hand correction. Hand corrections are not tolls and nobody is charged for them, and it is half the reason the stair is short of the truth every week of the year." She looked at him. "I am not asking you to agree with me. I am showing you what the stair says and what the street says, and they are not the same document."

"They're the same document," Orrin said. "The street just knows more than the file does."

"Yes." She wrote something down. "That is either the first useful sentence anybody has said to me about this stair, or it is a man who has decided what he wants and is going backwards to it. Tell me which, and I will decide how much time to give you."

He thought about lying, and decided against it, mostly because it would have been a waste of a woman's afternoon.

"I don't know yet," he said.

---

Her office was two rooms on the second floor of a building on the Rise that had been given up by the works department and had not been given to anybody else, with one window, a gas jet, a table, four chairs of which one was a stool, and a wall of pigeonholes with paper in them. There were nine books on the table and three of them were open.

"Sit. Don't apologise for the state of it, everybody does." She sat down opposite him and put her bag on the table and took out three things in order, and laid them in a row. "The certificate. The receipt. And your question, as it was filed, with the countersignature on it."

He had not seen his own question since the clerk squared it to the counter. It was a printout-thin sheet of pale green, and his handwriting in the middle of it looked like a stranger's careful attempt at his handwriting.

"You certified the Cathedral's crown case on the fifteenth," she said. "Condition: within tolerance, no defect observed, no recommendation. I have had a senior operator read that certificate and he says it is the work of a careful man and that it is true."

"It is true."

"It is." She put a finger on it. "You filed this question on the seventeenth, at ten past two in the afternoon, as a private citizen, and you paid the fee out of the Clockwork Quarter's reserve, and the draw was three minutes, and the trace held Ferrywright Bridge through the crest, and it did. I have the schedule. The bridge stood."

"That's all true as well."

"Everything you have done is lawful, Mr Vale. I want you to understand that clearly, at the start, because I am not here to catch you doing anything wrong. I am here because a man was found dead in a well at Weir Two on the night of a question you filed, and because your district's clocks went three minutes back to buy it, and because the clock on this street is five minutes slow and the file says three." She put both hands flat on the table. "So the question is not whether you did it. The question is what you knew, and when, and what you did with it, and who else you told."

He told her. Not all of it, and he could hear himself choosing as he went, which was new and unpleasant: the day list, the certificate, the file, the gate, the time of the crew coming on, the fact that he had stood at a locked door at half past six on the evening of the seventeenth with a lamp and a feeling, and had been refused entry by a boy with a whistle.

She wrote all of it down in a small tidy hand, and when he had finished she read back four of the details to check, and two of them he got wrong, and both times the correction was in his favour and neither time he cared.

"You put your hand on the second stage," she said.

"Yes."

"And what did you get?"

This was where it stopped, and he had known all day that it was going to stop here, in a room with a woman who had a pencil and a pigeonhole full of paper and absolutely no instrument at all.

"A feeling," he said. "That the stage would not stop that night."

She put her pencil down. She did it carefully, and aligned it with the edge of the table, and for a moment he thought he had got away with something clever.

"Right," she said. "I am going to tell you three things and then I am going to stop, because you have been polite and you have come in cold on a wet morning and you have not once asked me what I think of the Cathedral, which everybody does, and I would rather help you first and disapprove of you later."

"I'm listening."

"One. This office audits a stair. There is a rule about the stair, and it is not a secret rule, and it is forty-three years old." She turned a book round so that a page faced him. He could not read it upside down and did not try. "A question may be charged to any district in the city. The district named on the question is the district whose reserve is drawn. The Compact requires that the toll be recorded. It does not require that the district paying be affected, and it does not require that it consent, and it has never once been amended to say so, and there are four hundred and eleven pages of Assembly debate about the Compact's *wording* and not one word in any of them about that."

"You want to object to the charge."

"I want to object to the charge on the receipt in front of me, on the ground that the district charged was neither affected nor consenting, and I am going to do it, and it will be accepted for filing, because an objection on a toll receipt is a form of receipt and I am very good at forms of receipt." She wrote something at the top of a fresh sheet. "And when it is accepted, it will change nothing. Nothing will be repaid. No clock will be wound forward. What it will do is put a piece of paper in a drawer in this building that says, on a particular day, by a particular person, that the district charged had not asked. That is the whole of the power this office has. I would like you to be appropriately disappointed."

"Two?"

"Two." She held up a second sheet, and he recognised the print, because he had been looking at print like it all morning. It was a copy of a notice, the long strip, and there were four lines on it, and she put her thumbnail under the fourth. "The blind margin. *Unregistered night work is not represented.* You have read that line about nine thousand times, and so have I, and so has every operator in this city, and it is the only line on that notice that nobody reads, and I would like you to tell me something I can check."

"What line?"

"Exactly what it says, in its exact words, and how big it is."

"It is set in the footer, below the rule," Orrin said, "at about half the type of the trace, and it is about a quarter of an inch deep on the strip, and Pell's signature is twice the height of the whole line." He stopped, because she had gone still in the way people go still when they have been handed something they can put a ruler on. "You can measure it."

"I have measured it. Eleven to the point against twenty-two for the trace line, and the signature at fourteen." She wrote it down. "I have a notice from the eighth of the month with the same measurement. And one from two years ago with the same measurement. So that is not a fault of a single operator, Mr Vale, that is the form. It is a form, in every sense of the word."

"You said two."

"I said two and I have said three and I lied about the number because I wanted you to be still for a minute." She put the notice down and folded her hands. "Three. There is a missing crew at Weir Two. There is a pump that should have stopped and did not, a valve that was open, a man who went down a ladder to shut it and a man in a well. If that crew existed on paper, then there is a wage book, a shift book, a certification entry, a name, a widow or an orphan, a landlord, an employer, a district standing, and a hundred other things that can be looked at. If it does not exist on paper, then that night is a private matter between a district and its own drainage."

"It exists on a board," Orrin said. "Chalk. By the pump."

"Yes. Written by a person, in a hand, and kept by a person, and worth exactly nothing, because a board has no counter-signature, no form number, and no office that is obliged to receive it." She was writing again, fast. "I have asked for the Weir Two night roster. I put it in this morning, on the standard form, at ten o'clock, and it has gone up, and I will be told something by the middle of the week."

"Then you'll get the names."

"Mr Vale." She did not raise her voice, and the flatness of it was worse than a shout. "I have been doing this for nine years. I have sent up two hundred and six requests for records that exist and forty requests for records that are held where I am not permitted to name. Of the forty, four came back, and all four of those had been asked for by somebody with more standing than me before I was born." She turned round the second book. "There is a body of records under the Cathedral. It is called the Archive of Unlisted. It is not an office and it is not a room you can petition, and I am not able to tell you what is in it, and neither, as far as I can find, is anybody who is not dead."

Orrin looked at the wall of pigeonholes.

"The Weirs have four hundred thousand people," he said. "The pumping ward file on the desk in front of us has the population of a small city in it, and a works, and a school, and a market, and a tide clerk who telephoned me about the weather. What is the standing of a district like that?"

"Forty to sixty thousand residents of this city have no standing in the civic model. I have that figure from a clerk who would not give me a document and who gave it to me anyway, and I have believed the clerk." She closed the book. "In the Weirs, more than half of them. Nine in ten of them are working a shift that some public clock is recording somewhere. That is not a conspiracy, Mr Vale. A conspiracy would at least require somebody to have decided. What it requires is that the machine is a model, and a model does not need to decide anything about you in order to leave you out. It simply has a number of things it can hold, and you are not among them, and it is not being unkind. It is counting."

She said it in the voice of somebody quoting a rule, and it was the third time that day he had heard a person in an office say a phrase that meant far more than it had been written to mean, and this time the phrase was *calibration strain*, and she said it in a tone that had a whole room behind it.

"You said a phrase a moment ago," he said. "Calibration strain. That's what your office calls the intervals a body toll takes."

"Yes."

"Does it mean what it means in the guild's hands?"

"No," she said. "It means the same thing and it is a different entry on a different form in a different office. A body toll has a patient in a bed and a health form. A man listening to a machine without a witness has no form at all, and no office, and no one to answer for it. The word gets used for both because nobody has to answer for either." She looked at him, and for a moment something behind her face moved and was put away. "Rule. Not my rule. I am quoting a rule, and I am quoting it badly on purpose, and I would be grateful if you did not make me quote it again today."

He let it go. He was not going to get it out of her today, and he understood, in a way he would be embarrassed about in a week, that she was not withholding it for leverage. She had said it in a corridor once, in a voice she had not planned, and she had closed the door on it, and he had been invited to notice.

---

She was writing the timing of his arrival when she stopped, and looked at her own sheet, and then at him.

"You stopped."

"I did."

"You were answering something. I asked you when you had last slept, and you began to tell me about the weight on the street clock, and you finished a sentence about the third mark, and it was a good sentence." She turned the sheet so he could see it. His words were in her hand in five lines, all of them about the clock, and the question at the top of the page was in her hand, and it was not about the clock.

"I have not lost an hour," he said.

"I did not say an hour." She put the pencil down and did the thing that frightened him, which was to stop writing altogether. "I have read a hundred and forty health-office forms in this job. I know what an interval looks like in a person's account of an evening. You did not lose an hour. You lost about a minute, and you did not notice it happening, and you have been reconstructing since." She said it without any drama at all. "How long have you been doing that?"

"There has been nothing to do."

"What do you mean."

"I put my hand on a machine and I felt what would happen to it," he said, "and then I lost a minute, and I have had a whole day trying to get the minute back and it is gone, and the only account of the evening that anybody can check is the account of a man who is dead, and he cannot be asked."

The room was very quiet, and the gas jet hissed, and Orrin understood that he had said more in that room in four minutes than he had said to anybody in six years, and that this woman was not going to do anything at all with it except watch him, and that watching him was, at that moment, the most frightening thing anybody had done to him since his father went into a wall of water eighteen years ago.

She was frightened for him. Not of him. He could see it plainly in the way her hand had stopped on the table with the pen between two fingers, and it was worse than suspicion, because suspicion he could have used.

"You are not to be doing that on your own," she said quietly. "Do you understand what you have described? There is no rail, there is no proposal, there is no bond, there is no toll on the stair, and you have been drawing on yourself for years, and nobody reimburses it, and the guild calls that self-inflicted occupational strain, and the guild is not entirely wrong, and I am not able to do anything about it, and I am telling you this because I would like there to be one witness in Morrowfen who thinks you should stop."

"One witness who is not a person who works on a stair."

"I am the person who audits the stair." She picked the pencil up again. "Now. I am going to write the objection, and I am going to write it in the strongest words the form allows, and then I am going to send it, and the answer will be that the charge was lawful, because it was. And then you are going to go down to the Weirs and get me that board of Bevin Sarr's, and the names on it, and whatever wages were paid to those men by anybody for that week."

"They were not paid. That is the—"

"I know that it is the whole of it. That is why I said *and whatever wages were paid.*" She was writing. "And I will tell you now, so that you do not spend three days finding out and can spend those three days on the board. The objection will not give you the crew. Nothing in this city will give you the crew, because the crew is not held anywhere, because there is nowhere it is held. If I could get the roster I would not need the board. I asked for the roster because it is the only thing that would make the board unnecessary, and it went up this morning, and I will let you know when it comes back, and I am telling you now because I would rather you hear it from a person than from a letter."

"All right," said Orrin.

"I am also going to say one more thing and then I am going to stop, because you have been in this chair for an hour and a half and you have not once asked me for anything, and that is either very good manners or a very long habit, and I would like it to be the first." She did not look up. "If you go down there and you find a name, you will want to do something with it. Do not do anything with it. Not yet. Not on your own, not in the newspapers, not on the Cathedral steps, and certainly not in a way that you can be asked about. Bring it here and we will make it a thing that is written down, in a room, with my name on it and yours. I can do that much. I cannot do any more than that, and I cannot promise that what you give me will be permitted, or admitted, or believed. But I can make it exist on paper in a building with a door that shuts, and that is a real thing that this office has done four times in nine years."

Orrin looked at her for a moment longer than he meant to.

"You do not know me," he said.

"No," said Mara Venn. "That is why I am offering."

---

The reply came up on the pneumatic tube at half past four, and it was courteous, and it was two lines long, and the second line was the whole of it.

*Referred to the sealed archive.*

*M. Venn, Office of Public Reckoning.*

Orrin read it standing up, with his hat in his hand, in a corridor on the Rise that smelled of hot brass and cold stone, and understood that he had just been told *no* in a form that was designed to be read without a pulse rising, and that it had been designed that way by somebody who had read a great many of them and had decided that the design was a kindness.

He took the tube-strip down to the pigeonholes himself and put it in the tray marked *returned*, which was not his tray, and which she watched him do without comment.

Then he went down the hill in the rain with a certificate in his pocket that made him answerable and an objection on a receipt that changed nothing, and a street clock that was wrong, and a form number that had a price schedule, and a sentence from a man who kept a public archive telling him that believing a district is not a thing that happens in an archive. He got as far as the bottom of the Hill and understood that the only thing he had gained was a piece of paper with a name of a building on it, and that the name of the building was one of the two reasons nobody had believed the Weirs in twenty years.
