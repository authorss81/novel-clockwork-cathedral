# Chapter 91 — The Six Days Before, and What the Minute Did Not Say

Thursday the first, Friday the second and Saturday the third of the twelfth month, and nothing whatever happened in any of them, and a man of thirty-two was in bed until nine on the Thursday and did two clocks on a bench for a pound one shilling and sixteen shillings and refused to do a third thing he could have done in eleven minutes, and read a printed minute of the fifteenth of the tenth for the fifth time and found in it the only instrument in this city that has ever told anybody anything in advance, and a woman of twenty-four telephoned a works' counter about a bus and did not say why, and the whole of what a district's ordinary condition is in December is a room with a stove in it and a man with a bench, and a year of asking about it has been a story about what happens in that room.

---

**Thursday the first. In bed until nine, and the shop open at ten, and it is the first of the twelfth and the shortest day of the year is in nine days and nobody in Morrowfen calls it the shortest day.**

He had not slept properly since the first of the October and he had nine hours on the Wednesday night and eight on the Tuesday, and he wrote both figures down in the grey book, and the reason he wrote them down is the reason he has written every figure down in that book for eight months and it is not a good reason and it is the only one he has: **a grey book is worth nothing in any room that is not the one it is in, and this room is his, and he is the only person who will ever read that line, and he has decided that this is allowed.**

The stove draws. The bench is cold until about eleven and warm at noon and cold again at four. There is a chair, a rule, a bench light on a bracket, two drawers, a lease to Michaelmas on the wall behind the door in a frame he has never taken down, and a sign in the window that has said **HOROLOGER. REPAIRS TO ANY DESCRIPTION.** in the same type for eleven years.

He opened the case on the Thursday at about eleven.

**A long-case of 1903, brought up the outside stair by two men on the Wednesday evening at a price agreed before the case was opened.** It is at number four, Green Row, and it came into that house off a cart in 1936 with the house, and it has been in the same family since, and the family have known for four years that it disagrees with the world and have never once asked anybody why.

He took the reading himself on the Thursday morning, at about eleven, with a watch in his hand and the case on its side on the bench, and the working went on the back of the order pad because the working is the argument and it always has been.

> *Number four, Green Row. Long-case, 1903, 8-day, going train sound, striking train slow by about a minute and stopping when it is wound, which is a fault and not a fault and is a thing about 1903 and not about today.*
> *Against the Cathedral public clock, the first of the twelfth, about eleven in the morning, my own watch against the Cathedral and the Cathedral is the city's reference and not the Quarter's, and my right ear is what it is and I read the seconds off a dial and not off a sound.*
> *Nights since the sixth of the tenth: 26 in the tenth, the sixth to the thirty-first inclusive, and 30 in the eleventh, the first to the thirtieth inclusive. **Fifty-six nights at fifty seconds is 2,800 seconds, which is 46 min 40 s.***
> *The clock reads **2 h 58 min 20 s slow**, and the figure is the district's own figure for a clock on that line at that date, **2 h 11 min 40 s plus 46 min 40 s, which is 2 h 58 min 20 s**, and the clock is on the line because the house is on the line and has been since the house was built, and nobody in that house has ever known that, and neither, until this week, has anybody else.*

Then he did the work. Six hours. Four of them on the going train and two on the striking train, and the striking train wanted a piece he had not expected and made from a file in about twenty minutes, and the invoice is the estimate and the estimate was six hours at the hall's out-of-shop rate of four shillings and sixpence, which is twenty-seven shillings, and fourteen shillings of material, **and the addition is exact: twenty-seven and fourteen is forty-one shillings, which is one pound one shilling and not one penny over it, and the figure at the foot of the order pad was the figure on the invoice and the figure on the invoice is the figure at the foot of the order pad.**

And at about four o'clock on the Thursday he took the long-case off the bench and laid the pendulum against the back board and the whole of the error came out of the case in about eleven minutes, which is what it takes, and which is why the counting of his own hand corrections is what it is.

**He did not do it.**

He wrote one line in the narrow book on the shelf behind the bench, in pencil, with the other one, in the hand he uses for the narrow book and no other.

> *Could have. Did not. Two is two.*
> *And there will not be another one after Saturday, and I know that now and I did not know it on Wednesday, and the knowing is the only new thing in three days.*

And the reason is on the same page and it took him about four minutes to find, and it is this: **a clock that is right is a clock that tells a man his wife is late. A clock that is two hours fifty-eight minutes slow tells him something else, and the something else is that the district in which he lives is running on a line and the line is out, and the figure is the same figure in every house on that line, which means that any man on Green Row can go out of his door with a pencil and a bit of string and find out that his neighbour's clock says the same as his own, and two streets away it says something else, and a street that can do that is a street that can see.**

**The moment I make it right it stops being that.** And a household that has had a clock three hours slow for four years and has never asked is a household that has a public instrument in its front room that cost the district nothing, and I am the last man in this city who is in a position to take it out of there, and I have taken two out of this city already and I am not going to take a third out of a house in Green Row on a Thursday in December because it would look like a repair.

He left the case open on the bench with the door off and the hands off, because the family were coming for it on the Friday evening and a man who takes a clock away and brings it back right has done a job, and a man who brings it back wrong has done something else.

---

**And on the Friday the second he did the second clock, which is a bracket clock of about 1904 off a shelf in a kitchen in Bellweather, and it is three hours at the hall's out-of-shop rate of four shillings and sixpence, which is thirteen shillings and sixpence, and two shillings and sixpence of material, and sixteen shillings, and the estimate on the card was the figure on the invoice and the addition is exact.** It was a week's work and it was an ordinary week, and the whole of it came to **one pound one shilling and sixteen shillings**, which is less than the price of the eight pounds of material he used on the long-case in the summer and more than a man can take home in a week at that rate, and it is what a district's ordinary condition is in December and it is the only money that has come into that shop since the middle of the November.

**The printed minute of the fifteenth of the tenth, read for the fifth time, on the Friday the second, in the middle of the afternoon, with nothing else on the bench.**

It is four lines and it is the Water Users' Committee's minute of a Saturday sitting in a room above a chandler's shop in Bellweather, and he has a copy of it because the clerk, Nadia Erridge, gave him one at the counter in the fourth month without being asked, which she says she has done eleven times in four years and has never told anybody about, and which he has never thanked her for and is not going to be able to and has written down that he cannot.

> *1. Ordinary business, nine minutes.*
> *2. That the committee was unable to produce the winter rotation, the only copy being held in a store, and that the copy is not produced.*
> *3. That a copy of the winter rotation be made at fourpence a page, in four hands, with the disagreement between the two prints stated on the face of it, and that the cost be borne by the district.*
> *4. That the committee has not directed that any person be asked what the difference of fifteen minutes is.*

He had read those four lines five times since the middle of the October. He had read them in a shop, on a bus, standing in a works' yard, and once in a room where a woman of thirty-three was doing something else entirely. He knew every word in them and he could have said them from memory in front of the committee, and on the Friday the second of the twelfth he read them again and found the thing in them he had not seen the first four times, **and the thing is not in the minute.**

**It is on the notice.**

The notice went up on the ward board outside the Weir Two pump house on **Saturday the eighth of the tenth**, for the fifteenth, and it is a printed sheet with the business on it and the date on it and a line at the foot in the clerk's own hand, and there are **seven days** between the eighth and the fifteenth, and Nadia Erridge posted it herself on the Saturday morning with a drawing-pin and a scrap of lino, and it came off the board on the Saturday after the sitting and went into a drawer in a room over a shop, **and there is no copy of it anywhere and she has not kept one, and the reason she has not kept one is that a notice is not a document and a document is a thing you may be asked to produce.**

He stood in the middle of his own shop on the Friday afternoon and worked out what he had spent eight months doing, and this is what it was:

**He had spent eight months reading minutes.** Minutes are what a body does after it has done a thing. A minute is a record of an act, and an act is over, and a person cannot be asked about an act that is over, because there is nothing left to ask. He had a file of them now — the Water Users' Committee, the district works' inspection schedule, the maintenance programme with nine items, the Assembly's committee, the hall's standing orders, the Office's four-line cards, the toll ledger's headnote — and every one of them is a record of a thing that has already happened, and not one of them was ever read by anybody before it happened.

**And there is one instrument in this city that is read before the thing happens, and it is a sheet of printed paper with a date on it and a week between the date and the day, and it is posted on a board, and the posting is the law.**

Seven days' notice. That is the whole of it. That is the only clause in the water act, in the works' ordinances, in the hall's standing orders, or in any instrument in this city that gives a person time to know what is about to be done to them. **A schedule is made or varied by the Water Users' Committee on seven days' notice, and the notice goes on a ward board, and the third line of the notice says the district cannot be consulted, and the third line is the reason the notice exists: it is the sentence that tells the people the notice is for that they are not being asked, so that nobody has to feel obscurely singled out.**

He had read that board every morning since the June. He put six figures on it himself in the fourth month. He had a chair pulled up to it in the eleventh with a ladder. **He is one of about eleven people who read a ward board in a district of a hundred and forty thousand, and on the eighth of the October that board said, in the clerk's own hand, that the committee was going to try to produce a document it had not produced in four years, and there were seven days, and nobody in this district knew it except him and the clerk who posted it.**

And then he did the other half of it, and the other half is the finding and he wrote it down that evening in the grey book and the finding took him nine minutes:

**A rehearsal is not a schedule.**

Nobody in this city can post a notice of a rehearsal. There is no form on which a notice of a rehearsal could be asked for. There is no committee whose standing reaches one. The Water Users' Committee could not post a notice about the seventeenth of the twelfth **even if it wanted to**, and it does not want to, and it would be right not to: a rehearsal is not a thing it makes, and a thing it does not make is a thing it cannot give notice of, and the whole of the seven days' notice in this city is a promise that the body making the thing has thought about the thing.

So: **the asking on the tenth is not a courtesy before a rehearsal. It is the notice, and there is no other notice, and the only way this district can give twenty-two households the seven days it has never given anybody is to walk down a row of twenty-two houses and say it out loud on a Saturday morning, and the six days between the fourth and the ninth are the days in which the notice has to be made, and there is no form for making one and no clerk to make it and nobody can be made to make it and nobody can be stopped from making it.**

> *Second of the twelfth. I have been reading minutes for eight months.*
> *A minute is what a body did. **A notice is the only thing in this city anybody is given before a thing happens, and it is a week, and it is posted, and I have read it every morning for six months and I never once noticed that the thing I have been waiting for all year is a piece of paper with a week in it.***
> *Six days, the fourth to the ninth. The seventh day is the tenth, and the tenth is a Saturday, and the asking is at about ten in the morning in daylight by more than one person because a single person standing on twenty-two doorsteps is a thing that can be refused.*
> *And the rehearsal is the seventeenth, and no form anywhere in this city on which anybody may be told that it is going to happen, and that is not a defect in the rehearsal. **A notice of a rehearsal would be a form, and a form is a way of asking without the rule, and I am not going to spend six days inventing one.***
> *The six days are mine and the sixth day is the ninth and I have to find four people to walk with and I have to be able to say the price on a doorstep without being asked a question I cannot answer.*

---

**And on the Friday the second, at about eleven in the morning, a woman of twenty-four telephoned the counter of the district works on the canal road and asked one question and did not say why she was asking it.**

There is no switchboard in the district works. There is a counter in a yard, and a shed, and a line that goes through a window to a clerk, and on the Friday the second of the twelfth a woman telephoned it.

She had given her name because she had to, and it was **Nell Sayer**, and she works at the galvanising and wire works, which is at fifty-eight of ninety-one, and she has lived at number nine, Weir Lane, for six years, and the whole of that street is in a book in a kitchen under a signed undertaking that says nobody is to go to any of those doors, and she lives at the ninth of the twenty-six and **she telephoned a counter about a bus, which is not a door, and nobody in this city would have called it one.**

What she said, according to the clerk's own day book, in his own hand, with the date on it, is eleven words long:

> *Saturday morning. What time's the first bus up the canal road. And does it stop at Weir Lane.*

And what **Ambrose** said back is a time and a place and nothing else.

> *Ten past eight off the Bellweather Road stand. Stops at Weir Lane, the second flag, about ten to nine.*

And she said thank you, and he said nothing, and that was the whole of it, and he wrote it in his day book, which is a thing he has kept for eleven years and in which there are, by his own count, four lines with a line number against them, all of them about a main.

> *Fri. 2nd Dec. Woman telephoned, N. Sayer, re bus. 8.10 a.m. first off Bellweather Rd. Weir Lane yes, second flag, c. 8.50. Nothing further asked for.*

**Nothing further asked for.** He underlined it, and underlined it again the following week, and when a works' inspector in the yard asked him in the January why he had underlined it twice, he said: *because in eleven years there has not been a line in that book that is not about a main, and a clerk is the only person in this city who can answer a question without asking what it is for, and I would like that underlined while I am still the clerk who is here.*

He did not telephone her back. He did not write her name in the ward's book, and Bevin Sarr, who heard about it from her on the Monday, wrote nothing down at all, which is the first time in a year of this that she has heard a thing and deliberately written nothing, and she gave the reason, which is that **if I write it down it is a record, and a record of a woman asking about a bus is a record of a woman being in a room where she should not be, and she was on a telephone in a yard and she asked about a bus and that is the whole of what happened.**

And the second of the December, at about ten past eleven in the evening, in a room in Bellweather that had a stove in it and a bell that had not worked since 1949, the two of them worked out together — she on a telephone with a lamp against the window, because there is no light in that room after the bulb — what a Saturday morning at ten o'clock with twenty-two doors on a row of houses about four hundred yards long actually consists of, and **the answer is that nobody in this city has ever done it and the two of them had to invent it, and the invention is not a method and has no name and is not going to be in any book except the two of them.**

> *8.10 off Bellweather Road. Walk from Weir Lane. Twenty-two doors, and a street about four hundred yards, and eleven clocks and a cistern vault and a handwheel on it.*
> *She said: how long.*
> *He said: three hours if nobody opens the door to anybody and two if they do, and he has done the arithmetic about nine times and it is not arithmetic, it is a woman of twenty-four who has been the loudest person in about four rooms a year for six years doing it in her head on a Friday night.*
> *She said: then we start at half past eight and we finish by one and if we are still going at one we stop, and we come back, and nobody opens a door to anybody after one o'clock on a Saturday because **a person who has had an afternoon's sleep in is not a person you can ask a question of and if you ask it anyway you will not be told the truth and you will go home with a lie and write it on a wall.***
> *He said: that is the whole of it and I have been trying to say it since the fourteenth of the eleventh and you have said it in nine words.*
> *She said: I have not said it in nine words, I have said it in nine words on a Friday night because I am tired and it is obvious, and you have had it on a wall since November and you are the slowest man in this district and it has taken you a month and a bus.*

Nobody thanked anybody, and the first of the three is true, and the second of the three is not a thing either of them would have written down.

---

**The grey book, on the Saturday the third, in the middle of the afternoon, with the shop open and nobody in it.**

There are two pages open to it in the whole of two thousand, and both of them are in the same week of September, and one of them has a number on it that is wrong and one has a number on it that is right.

> *Twentieth of the ninth. To the first of the fifth next year: a hundred and two days to the thirty-first of this year, and a hundred and nineteen from the first of the first month to the first of the fifth, and a hundred and two and a hundred and nineteen is **two hundred and twenty-one**, and it is a figure with a working on it.*
> *First of the twelfth. To the first of the fifth next year: thirty-one in this month, thirty in the first, twenty-eight in the second, thirty-one in the third, thirty in the fourth, and thirty-one plus thirty plus twenty-eight plus thirty-one plus thirty is **a hundred and fifty days**. **I checked it against the page above with a pencil and it is the same figure counted forward and that is the whole of what I did and it took me nine minutes.***
> *Bill: **£4 2s.** Form GH-9, forty-one heads, a year, two shillings the head, lodged on the first of the fifth of this year at a counter in four minutes with a wet stamp, my own money, and I have told one person and the Office of Public Reckoning is one person and there is nobody else alive who can put the form and the four-line card together except a woman of sixty-one at a counter who sold a form number for four shillings in coin with no receipt in the second month and whom I have not asked and am not going to.*

**A bill and not a deadline.** That is the line under it and he has written it four times in four books this year and it is the same sentence every time and he is not going to find a better one.

The reason he does not write the date at the end of a hundred and fifty days is not modesty and it is not carelessness. It is this: **the day that form runs out is a day with nothing on it.** No hearing, no vote, no notice, no review, no clause. On that day the forty-one heads fall out of the standing again as silently as they came out in 1939, and the four lines on the hall's four-line card go back to being true, and nobody will be told, because nobody is ever told anything, and the reason nobody is told anything is that **telling requires a person who can be asked whether the telling was correct, and there has not been one in this district for nineteen years.**

A date written on a page is a thing a person can be held to. He has got nobody to hold it to. So it stays a number and the number is in a book in a shop and the book is worth nothing in any room that is not the one it is in, and that is the correct condition of it, and he decided that in the June and has not improved on it.

---

**The shop. Saturday the third, from ten until six, and nobody came in except a man who wanted to know whether he could have a clock cleaned, and was told he could, and was told what it would cost, and did not have it cleaned, and said that a man who is told a price ought to be given the price in writing, and was given it in writing, on a card, with a date on it, and did not take the card and left it on the counter and it was still on the counter at six.**

He did not sweep up at noon. He cleaned a movement that was not for a customer and would not be for a month, on the second bench, with the bench light on, because a bench with nothing on it in the middle of a working day is a bench a man looks at.

At about half past four he wrote out the six days. He wrote them out because they were the only thing in the whole of the arrangement that was not written anywhere, and because he had worked out in the last ten days that in this city the things that are not written anywhere are the things that decide.

> *The six days, and what is on them, and I have got this wrong twice today and am writing it down so that I cannot get it wrong a third time.*
> *Sunday the fourth — the shop shut, as it is shut on Sundays, and it has been shut on Sundays since 1931, and there is one Sunday in this month in which the rehearsal is set and on that Sunday nothing whatever will happen, as nothing happened on the Sunday the thirteenth of the eleventh, and the reason is the same reason and I am not going to write it out again.*
> *Monday the fifth, Tuesday the sixth, Wednesday the seventh, Thursday the eighth, Friday the ninth.*
> *Six days to make a notice that nobody in this city is entitled to make and everybody on that row is entitled to receive, in a form that does not exist, by people who can be refused and have not been refused, because I have been told the cost out loud and I have not been asked for anything, and that is the only reason I am still doing this.*

And then, at about six, when he shut the shop and turned the sign and put the day's card on the counter where it was still lying from the man who would not take it, the last thing he did before he put the light out was to stand for about a minute in a cold room on a Saturday evening in December with a stove in it that draws, and a bench, and a chair, and a rule, and two drawers, and a lease to Michaelmas in a frame behind the door.

> *Third of the twelfth, six in the evening.*
> *The whole of a year of this has been a story about what happens in this room, and I want that written down while I am the only person who will ever read it, and I want it written down on a Saturday, in the plainest words I have, because it is not a discovery and it is not a finding and nobody is going to be shown it.*
> *A district's ordinary condition in December is a room with a stove in it and a man with a bench. Everything that happened this year happened in rooms. A rate was struck in a room. A review was held in a room. A form was lodged at a counter. A boy read four hundred and eleven lines at a table. A woman of twenty-four came up an outside stair at a quarter to eight in the evening and refused to be a name on a piece of paper, and a man of fifty-five is a strip of form pinned inside the front cover of a book he published for a shilling and twopence, and a girl of thirteen has had a card in a box of bills for four years, and a man at a works' gate has had a sheet of paper in his coat since the second of the ninth, and a warden put the only copy of her district's water in a store and filled in the form in her own hand and the form says the store and not a shelf.*
> *None of them is a machine. **Every one of them is a room, and the only thing a year of this has established, and it is not much of a thing and it took a hundred and forty questions to find, is that a room will hold a person, and that a person will hold a figure, and that a figure with a working on it is worth exactly as much as the room it is in and not one penny more.***
> *I have a hundred and fifty days and four pounds two shillings and one name told and two hundred and twenty-one days in a book and a bench, and on Saturday the seventeenth at about half past seven in the morning I am going to stand in a pump house and read a clock in front of two people who will not sign their names to anything, and on Saturday the tenth at about ten in the morning I am going to stand on a street I have never walked and say a number out loud, and I do not know how long twenty-two doors takes and neither do they, and neither, as far as I can find out, does anybody in this city.*
> *The shop is shut. The bill is a bill. Nobody has been thanked.*

> *First to the third of the twelfth. Two clocks on a bench, one pound one shilling and sixteen shillings, the estimate the invoice, a third clock that can be corrected by hand in eleven minutes and was not, and **the count of my own hand corrections is two and it is two this morning and it is two for ever if I am careful, and after the seventeenth there will be nothing left in this shop to correct and I have not told anybody that and I am not going to until I know it.**
> *A printed minute of the fifteenth of the tenth, read five times, and the thing in it I did not see the first four times is **the notice of the eighth**, and the notice is the only instrument in this city that gives anybody time to know what is going to be done to them, and it is a week, and it is posted, and there are about eleven people in this district who read a ward board and I am one of them and I have been one of them since June.*
> *A rehearsal is not a schedule. **Nobody can post a notice of a rehearsal and nobody is entitled to and there is no form on which one could be asked for, and a notice of a rehearsal would be a form, and a form is a way of asking a question without the rule.** So the asking is the notice, and the six days from the fourth to the ninth are the making of it, and it will be made on foot by people who can be refused.*
> *A woman of twenty-four asked a works' counter what time the first bus up the canal road goes on a Saturday morning and did not say why, and a clerk of about thirty with eleven years at a counter answered her in nine words and wrote it in his own day book with the date on it and underlined *nothing further asked for* twice, and there is no line in that book that is not about a main.*
> *Nobody was thanked. The clerk does not want to be. The woman has not been thanked and does not want to be, and said so, and I did it anyway, and she told me what I look like from the other side of it and she was right.*
> *The sixth day is the ninth. The seventh day is the tenth, and it is a Saturday, and it is at about ten in the morning, and I have four days to find the people who will walk it and a number to say on twenty-two doorsteps and I have not got either of them.*
> *And the whole of a district's ordinary condition in December is a room with a stove in it and a man with a bench, and a year of this has been a story about what happens in that room.*
