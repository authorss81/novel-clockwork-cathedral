# Chapter 30 — What a District Decides

The tenant council met in the back room over the chandler's shop on Weir Lane, which it had been using for about eleven years because none of its members could pay the door of a licensed hall and the chandler had a bench and a great deal of unpaid goodwill, and the whole of the district's business was done in there on the Wednesday morning of the twenty-ninth, and the reason the district had got as far as a Wednesday morning is that it had spent four weeks discovering what it was.

There were nineteen people in the room and one of them had the chair, and the chair was a rule from before the war that had not been used since the pump house got its second motor, and the woman holding it had not wanted it and had said so twice, and it was hers for the morning and she got up from the table and gave it to Bevin Sarr at nine o'clock and did not take it back at eleven.

Nobody was a leader of anything, and that is the first thing to be said about the room, because everything else in it depends on it. In that room on the Wednesday morning there was a second on a night pump crew, a tenant council organiser, a rigger, a woman who ran a laundry, two mutuals, three blocks' worth of residents, a landlord, a boy of twenty, a woman from the Weir One gate, and a clockmaker from the Clockwork Quarter with no standing in the room and a bench in it because the chair had said he could sit at the back.

There was no committee. There was no minute book except Wenna Pruitt's, and there was no constitution, and there was nothing to resign from.

---

The first thing on the Wednesday was Teo Arden, and he had it written out, and he read it out.

"That the Weirs be represented, by the tenant council and by such delegates as the council may appoint, in the schedule of the water users' committee now in force; that the pumping district's night hours be entered in that schedule as work performed; and that the district's count of three hundred and eighty-eight hours be printed with it."

"That is a motion to be a party to a water schedule," said Wenna Pruitt.

"Yes."

"That is a motion to put a name in a form where there is a box for a name, in a room, and to be held to it for as long as the schedule runs." She had her hand flat on the ledger. "The schedule runs to the thirtieth. Today is the twenty-ninth. So we would be putting a name in a form for one day, and then the form would be on a shelf, and the next time anybody asks us what the Weirs think of anything there would be a piece of paper with us on it saying we had a view about a water rotation, and there would be no getting out of it. That is not a motion, that is a *standing*."

"A standing is what we want."

"It is what we have never had and it is the one thing we must not have, and I am going to say why and then I am going to be finished." She looked round the room, and she took her time about it, and she was a woman of thirty-eight who had been keeping four ledgers on one table for eleven years and who had never in her life been the most important person in a room. "I have been printing things in the Advertiser for seven weeks. I have stood in that print shop and I have set type with my own hands twice, because Bessie could not spell *maintenance-deferred* and I would not have it spelled wrong. And I am going to tell this room what a printed page is worth.

"It is worth nothing. Not because it is untrue. Because a printed page has no address. It cannot be served. It cannot be refused. It cannot be contradicted by anybody, because there is nobody to contradict; it is a statement in the air, and the day anybody wants a statement in the air to be *anything* — to be a witness, to be an exhibit, to be a thing a form can refer to — the printer will say that it was set by a man who is not a party to the question and that he took the words as they came."

"So the three of us in the last month have published a district's hours, and a district's hours are now in a binder in a building on the Rise, and they got in there because a woman in Green Span paid four pounds ten out of her own ward's allowance and told the clerk in a corridor that it was a district returns and not a complaint. And there is no page of ours in that binder. Ours is in the Advertiser. Theirs is in the Assembly's record. Theirs is in a room a committee sits in and ours is folded in a man's pocket on a Friday."

She took her hands off the table.

"We are not a party to the water schedule. We will not be a party to the water schedule. And I want it written down that I am not saying it because I am frightened of it. I am saying it because a position is a thing a district is *held to*, and a district that is held to a position is a district that can be asked to give it up, and there is nothing in the water act about a district being able to say afterwards that it did not mean it."

---

They argued it for two hours and ten minutes and the vote was seven to three to one, and it was not close, and Teo Arden voted against and said before the vote was taken that he would not be bound by it, and that he was not going to go and print anything behind her back, and that the two of them ought to stop pretending they were on the same side of this.

"That is nearly respect," he said, when she said the printing had begun in the second month.

"It is nearly arithmetic," said Wenna Pruitt, "and it will not be the same arithmetic in a year, and I am not going to be surprised in a year and neither are you."

"Sel Kade," said Teo Arden, from the back. "You have not said anything in two hours and you are the only person in this room whose job is the water. Say something about the water, and if it is a parliamentary point I shall go out."

"I am not going to make a parliamentary point," said Sel Kade. She had been at the window the whole morning with her wet coat over the back of a chair, and she had not sat down. "I am going to say four things about hydraulics and then I am going to go to my round, and I want the first one said before the vote is taken, because the first one is the reason the vote is what it is going to be.

"**A schedule is a machine.** It does not know whose water it is holding. It has no memory of the fifteenth and it does not care in the least whose front is dry by the summer. It is a piece of brass with a chain on it and it is the best instrument the city has and it is worth every pound it cost.

"And that is the answer to the question in this room. If the Weirs stand in a room and say *this water is ours*, we do not become the owners of it. We become a **claimant on a machine**. And a claimant is a thing a machine can be run without, and when the arithmetic goes the other way, which it will, because it always does — the tide comes up, the Quarter needs a draw, the hospital line needs something, a year comes that is worse than this one — the arithmetic does not have to persuade anybody. It only has to say that the claimant is in the model and the claimant is not required, and there is a printed line for that and it is set two sizes smaller than a signature, and every one of you has read it."

Nobody spoke.

"That is the first thing. The second is that on the eighth there were nine men in a room with property qualifications, and not one of them had ever been awake at two in the morning on the Bellweather front, and they took the water off the district and they took it off us *because nobody was there to claim it*, and that is not a conspiracy, it is a filling-in. It is the same filling-in that the hall has been doing with a form since the year before the Long Nine, and you have spent four weeks learning exactly how to do it, and today you have voted not to do it back to them, and I would like that marked as the first genuinely frightening thing I have seen in this district in twenty years."

She picked her coat up off the chair.

"The third thing is that I am not going to be asked about the reserve in this room, and I am going to say the reason before anybody asks, so that nobody can say afterwards that they were kept in the dark. I moved the pumping ward's reserve in March to Carrow Street, to seventy-one households, and I said so out loud on a street, and I offered it to a committee myself, and it is in the minutes, and it has not been reviewed by anybody, and it is not going to be reviewed by anybody who is in this room.

"And I am telling you that because you are about to go into this next business with an idea that a district can hold its own, and a district can hold its own, and so can I, and a pump warden who is asked to account for a reserve in the same fortnight that the same reserve is the reason a ward could not be got a window is a warden who will spend the rest of her life in rooms being asked about the diversion. I know that. I have watched it happen to a man in Bellweather. So I am going to say it in a room like this one, in my own words, at this table, and not in an office, and if anybody in this room thinks that is a dodge, they are entitled to, and it is a dodge, and it is also correct, and both of those can be true at nine in the morning on a Wednesday."

She stopped at the door.

"Fourth thing. If the council wants a method, it has got one and it is not in this room. It is in a kitchen and a pump house and nine buildings and it is that people can refuse. You have nine people who can say no to this resolution and you have gone to the trouble of finding nine, and I sat here for two hours listening to a room where everybody could say no, and I want to say — " and for the first time in two hours she looked at the people rather than the window " — that I have never once in my life sat in a room like that. Not one. And it is not a small thing, and it is not a thing you can put in an appendix."

She went out.

---

Orrin had said nothing for two hours and ten minutes, and he had been in the room, and the difference between a room he could be in and a room he could only be written about in turned out to be, in the Weirs, on a Wednesday morning, almost nothing at all: nobody had told him he could not speak, and nobody had asked him for anything, and both of those were the same as being absent.

He stood up at about a quarter to eleven, after the vote, and asked whether he might say one thing, and Wenna Pruitt said he could, and he understood from the way she said it that she was giving him the floor and not a vote, which is the correct arrangement and which is not a thing anybody in that room would have thought to arrange.

"The only instrument I have got," he said, "is a rule. It says that nothing of mine goes out of a room without a person named who can refuse it. It stopped me on the eighth in the Question Hall, in front of a hundred and thirty people, because a boy of twenty would not let me say a true thing with no form under it, and he was right, and it is the only piece of my own method that has ever done anything at all.

"I would like to offer it to the district. It is free. Any one of you can use it and I will put a date on it and my name against it and I will be answerable for it. If any result of mine goes anywhere, there is a person named who can refuse it. It is the whole of what I have learned this year and I am handing it over, because it is not mine any more and I have not got a district and it does not need one."

Wenna Pruitt nodded at him the whole way through, which he had not expected, and when he had finished she waited about four seconds and then said:

"Thank you. That is genuinely useful and it is not what we need, and I am going to tell you why, and I am sorry, and I am not going to pretend I am sorry.

"We have got nine people who can refuse. We have had them for four weeks. And nine people who can say no is a *room*, and a room is the only instrument in this city that everybody already agrees is a room. The trouble is that a room has no address, and a room cannot be served, and nothing in any office in this city can be required to read what a room decided." She closed the ledger. "Your rule is the method. It has been the method since the nineteenth of last month. And on the Wednesday morning of the twenty-ninth the district has outgrown it, and I do not think you did that, and I do not think anybody did it on purpose, and I have watched it happen and I could not have stopped it and I would not have stopped it."

Nobody voted on that, because it was not a motion, and there was no form for it.

---

The costs came out on the Thursday and they were not surprises, because Wenna Pruitt had said in the room on the Wednesday that they would come, and had said it in the past tense, which is how a woman of thirty-eight tells a room that she has already done the sum.

The Advertiser stopped. That was the first and it was the largest. A page in a weekly needs a subject every week and a district that has decided not to be a party to a question cannot have a page about the question, and the printing shop on the Friday took the type down and put it up again, and the man who set it was owed eleven shillings for a fortnight's work, and there are seven issues of the paper in a binder in a room off Weir Lane that describe a district in terms nobody in that district is willing to be described in, and a column that said the district had been told to be reasonable and had been.

"You are joking," said Teo Arden, on the Thursday morning. "We have just spent four weeks in that paper. It is the only door in this city. If we stop printing we are—"

"We are not stopping. We are not printing *that*." Wenna Pruitt put the Advertiser's bill on the table. "Eleven shillings for the fortnight, and I have paid it, and we are printing a page on the ninth of the month that is about the Bellweather front and the standing water and nothing else at all, and it will be a page about the district's water and the district's hours, and it will say what a water users' committee is, and it will not name a man."

"That page will be read by nobody."

"That page will be read by the eleven hundred people who live on the front and by their landlord's solicitor and by nobody else, and that is exactly what it is for, and it is worth more than three weeks of anger." She squared it. "And I have written to the man who owns the Advertiser's front page, and I have told him the district is not going to be a weekly grievance, and I have told him the reason, and he has written back and asked whether I understand what I have just done to his circulation, and I have answered, and it is in his letter, in his own hand, in ink, in a drawer in a printing shop, and nobody will ever read it but the two of us."

---

The second cost was that the better part of a hundred people had wanted it out, and there was no way to ask them.

Bessie Crane said it best and she said it in four sentences on the Thursday morning while she was putting her coat on, and then went to work.

"You have decided the district does not speak, and there are about a hundred and forty thousand people in this district, and about nine hundred of them are women over sixteen, and about sixty of those have been in this room and been glad to be in it, and I have just had a telephone call from the Bellweather Road mutual, which is nine women, and they are not in this room because they are on a Thursday, and they want to know what they are supposed to do now. And what am I to tell them? I have been printing the argument for a month and I have been in the Advertiser, and I have had a woman of fifty-eight on a stair in Northglass who has got a copy of a page that says she has never worked in her life, and I have told her we are working on it."

"We are working on it," said Wenna Pruitt.

"You are not, and you know you are not." Bessie Crane put her handbag on the table. "That is fair. I am not having a go. I am telling you what a decision is. A decision does not stop at the four people in the room. It goes out and it finds two hundred women and it is a thing that was done to them, and they were not asked, and that is not an argument against doing it. It is only the argument against believing that the room was the district."

She was gone before anybody could answer her, and she was not angry, and it was the third time in a month that a woman in that room had said a true thing on her way out of a room, and none of the three had been wrong.

---

The footnote came at about two o'clock on the Thursday afternoon, and it came from a rigger, and it was not a gift and it was not intended as one.

Renn Corby put a sheet of paper on the table on the corner of the table, squared, and said, "You'll want to see this. It's off the estimates return. The Finance committee's appendix, the one that carried the four pounds ten. My man has it at the works and it's public and it's been public since the twentieth of last month and none of us has ever read it because none of us knew it was the place our name was in."

*Assembly estimates, third month. Line: district returns, exceptional, £4 10s, public-health clerical allocation, Green Span. Source note, appendix 9, footnote 2: the returns of the Lower Weirs pumping district were compiled by the district and are unverified. Vale, class three. The discrepancy between the charge and the error observed on the street is recorded in an objection lodged with the Office of Public Reckoning on the 19th of the same month.*

One clause of his, at the bottom of a page, about as long as a sentence, in a note to a line item about four pounds ten, in a public return, in an appendix, in a footnote.

He read it twice at the table and then took out the grey book and read it a third time, standing up, with the rain going past the window of a back room over a chandler's shop on Weir Lane, in a district he did not live in and was not a member of and could not be a member of.

"That is a footnote," he said. "In somebody else's document. On page nine. In an appendix. About a printer's line."

"It is a footnote," said Wenna Pruitt, "in a document about four pounds ten, which is the amount a woman paid out of her own ward's allowance to get six returns copied out of a pump house into a binder, and the document is public and has been since the twentieth of last month, and nobody in this district has read it because we did not know that we were in it." She did not look up from the ledger. "It is the first time that anything any of us did has been in a document with our names near it, and I have been a tenant council organiser for eleven years and I am telling you honestly that I did not know that was on offer, and that if I had known I would have gone after it in the spring, and that I would not have thought of it in a corridor, and that I do not think anybody in this city knows how to go after a footnote."

"That is not the point," said Orrin.

"That is exactly the point, and I shall come to it in a moment." She shut the book. "Mr Vale. You have spent two days in rooms in which your name was nowhere. And you have spent a month in rooms in which the only thing you could be is a man with a rule, and the rule has been worth nothing, twice, and the second time it was worth nothing in a room with nine witnesses in it. And you have just found out that the only place in this city where you have ever been useful is one clause at the bottom of page nine of a printer's return about four pounds ten."

"Yes," said Orrin Vale. "That is exactly what I have found out."

"Well," said Wenna Pruitt. "I have been in this district for eleven years and I have never once been named in anything, and I have never wanted to be, and I want to say — and I have thought about it and I am going to say it and you can do what you like with it — that I think it is a good place to be. Not the page. The bottom. And that I would rather be a footnote in a document that a woman paid four pounds ten for than a paragraph in a paper that a man printed because he was angry on a Friday, and that this room decided that today, and that I am not going to be sentimental about it, and I would like the copying to be entered at fourpence a page and the bill to come to the council."

---

He went up to the Quarter on the Thursday evening about six, and went into the shop, and shut the door, and it was the end of the third month of a year in which nothing whatever had happened to him, and he was a licensed maintainer with a wet stamp on a receipt and a district that had decided in a room over a chandler's shop that it would not be a single thing.

The right hand closed, mostly. The little finger was still late. The hum in the ear came up when the light went and went away when the light came, and he did not write it down because there is nowhere to write it that is not the grey book, and the grey book is not a record and he had proved that in a room on Bellweather Road.

At about a quarter past six a man came into the shop with a carriage clock under his arm and put it on the counter and said, "It'll be about three minutes slow, my wife says it's four, and she has been counting the tram."

"Which is it?"

"That's what I'm asking you." He put his hat down. "And I'll tell you something while you're at it, Mr Vale, because you'll hear it this week and I'd rather somebody told you. That clock is not the only one. There's four on my street out, and there's the one at the corner, and my lad says the ones on Weir Lane are out as well. It's been going on since Tuesday. Nobody's come."

Orrin had his hand on the carriage clock and he had not asked it anything, and it had not told him anything, and it was going to tell him nothing, and he was going to have to get the gauge out of the case and do the whole thing the long way in about nine minutes and write it up afterwards in a shop book in his own hand.

"Where's your street?" he said.

And the man told him, and it was not in the Clockwork Quarter and it was not in the Weirs either, and it was four miles from both, and it was on the Green Span line, and there was a hospital at the end of it.

"Right," said Orrin Vale. "Then I'd not bother about your wife's four minutes. Put it on the counter. I want a gauge and I want a board and I want about nine minutes and I want you to hold the light, because I have not been able to judge a sound since the seventh of last month and I am not going to pretend otherwise to a man who has come to me with a clock."

He shut the door behind the man and did not write it down anywhere except the shop book, in a ledger with a lease, in a district that had decided that afternoon not to speak, and the rain came on the roof, and the hand closed, mostly, and it was the work.

That was the whole of what he had left, and it was worth doing, and on the evidence of the twentieth of last month it was not going to be worth anything in a room.
