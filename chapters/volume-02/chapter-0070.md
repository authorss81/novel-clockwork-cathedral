# Chapter 70 — A Nil Return Is Worth Three Shillings in the Pound

The safety levy on the Northglass works has never been collected, and it is not a scandal and nobody has concealed it, and the man who found out found it out by accident in about ten minutes with a pencil, and he is fifty-one and he manages four hundred men and his name is **Idris Vane** and he is not a villain and he is not a plant and he is the first person in this city to use the omission on purpose.

---

He wrote, and he wrote to Ambrose at the works' counter on the canal road, which is a thing that has not happened in this district in four years.

> *Mr Ambrose — I have been given your district's safety levy schedule for the year by my own clerk, who obtained it in the ordinary course, and I have a question about my own site and I am told by two people that I should not put it in writing. I am putting it in writing. I should like to be in a room with somebody who can tell me whether I am right.*
> *Northglass Works, Friday the nineteenth inst., any morning.*

Ambrose read it standing up and then said one sentence to a man at a counter and got him a room at the works for the Monday.

---

**The room is the works manager's office and it is on the first floor over the gate, and there is a gate, and four hundred men go through it.**

Nine people were in it and a works clerk of about thirty with a book. Idris Vane is fifty-one, thirty-one years in the trade, nineteen in this works, and he is not a villain and he is not stupid and he is not a man who has been waiting for an occasion. He is a man who asked a question and got an answer and did not like it.

What he had, and it is on a single sheet of his company's own stationery, is this.

> *The district safety levy: three shillings in the pound on the rateable value of properties in the district as entered in the district return.*
> *This works: rateable value, £61,000.*
> *This works' entry in the district return: **nil.***
> *Three shillings in the pound on sixty-one thousand pounds is nine thousand one hundred and fifty pounds a year. It has not been levied on this site in any year the schedule runs.*

He put it on the desk flat and did not explain it and waited, and the first four minutes of the room were people being polite about it.

Then he did the next part, and this is the part that matters, and it is the part he says he is not asking anybody to be impressed by.

"**That is not a loophole. I want to be exact, because I have already had two people tell me it is a loophole and it is not.** A nil is not a small figure. A nil in a return is a statement that the thing is not in the schedule at all, and I know that is a low thing to say about a low entry, and I am aware of it.

"I own four hundred men. I know what my hazard is. A foundry of that size with that plant and that lifting gear and that compressed-air system has an inspection programme attached to it — the district's own, the safety one, four visits a year and a report on each, and they are paid for out of the pool, and the pool is made of the levy, and the levy is three shillings in the pound on the district return.

"**And I have not paid a penny of it. Not this year, not in any year, and not once since 1949. And the four inspections a year of my own plant are paid for out of a pool that does not contain my share, and the pool is short by my share, and the shortfall is made up by somebody else's ratepayer, or it is not made up and the inspections come down in number, and either way the arithmetic has a four-hundred-man foundry in it at zero.**"

He tapped the sheet.

"That is a true sentence and I did the sum in about ten minutes with a pencil in my own office and I had it checked twice by a man on my staff who did not know what it was about, and both times it came to nine thousand one hundred and fifty. **I am not here to be told that a hazard which is not in the return is not in the arithmetic. I have known that since about 1949. I have been keeping my own inspection records on my own money since 1949 because I could not get anybody to come.**"

---

**Nobody in the room had an answer to that, and the reason nobody had an answer is the reason Orrin Vale had come, and he came to find out whether he had one, and he did not.**

"I can show you a different number," he said, and he could not, and he said so inside about ninety seconds, which was quicker than he had expected and which he wrote down that night as the least comfortable minute of his year.

"**There is no column in this city for four hundred men who are not in it.** That is the whole of it. There is no column and there is no form and there is no office that has a function in it. Your hazard is real and it is in a book in this building and it is in a book at the works and it is in nobody's return.

"And I can show you the price of being in it, and I can only show you that. If this district's return were made true, then your site would be in it, and the levy would be three shillings in the pound on sixty-one thousand pounds, which is nine thousand one hundred and fifty a year, and the district's other properties would be in it too, and **the ward's water rate would go from nine pence to one shilling and tenpence in the pound, which is two thousand six hundred pounds on the ward and thirteen shillings a year on a rated house of twelve pounds, and the notice goes up in the autumn with a number on it and no name on it.** And the name that is on it is not the hall's name and it is not the works' name."

"The laundry," said a woman who had not spoken for an hour. "The Bellweather Road laundry. Thirty-one women."

"Yes," said Orrin Vale. "And a gate at Weir One, and about a hundred and forty households in basements off Weir Lane. And not four hundred men, because four hundred men in a works are not ratepayers and have never been and cannot be put in a return at that address, and I do not know how to make them one and neither does anybody else I have asked."

**So he was right about the arithmetic and wrong about who pays, and he was wrong about it in the only way it is possible to be wrong about it in this city, which is completely.**

"The levy should be on you," said Idris Vane. "I have never wanted it not to be on me. I have wanted it to be on me since 1949 and I could not get at it. If you are telling me that the way to get at it is to correct a return in four districts in the autumn and put my site in it, then I am not going to argue with you about that, because I have got four hundred men who would rather pay a levy than have a plant go through a floor, and I can put it to them in a morning."

"Yes."

"Then I do not understand what anybody in this room thinks the difficulty is," said Idris Vane.

And the difficulty, and there is no way round it and nobody in that room found a way round it in about two hours, is this:

**A true return is rateable, and rateable is a thing that happens to people, and the people it happens to in this district are not the people whose absence from the return is the problem. If the works is put in, a laundry is put in. If a laundry is put in, a rate notice goes up in October with a number on it and no name on it, and the woman whose name is on it is about sixty and has a cloth bag and a book of her own and runs nineteen years of laundry on a penny a sheet under the trade price, and she has already paid sixty-two shillings in coin in July for the right to be counted, and she said out loud on the day that she was told not to do it by a man at a licensing counter that she would do it anyway, and she asked for it to be written down that she had been told not to.**

"She has already been in it," said Orrin Vale. "In July. She is the only person in this district who has voluntarily put herself in a return, and she did it for sixty-two shillings, and it is the only instance in eighteen years and she is not in it as a ratepayer, she is in it as a head count, and a head count is a number and a woman is not a number."

---

**And then Ivy Pike, who is sixty-one and who signs for the Weir One gate and who has kept a book of her own for eleven years and has never shown it to anybody, said the thing that had to be said and had to be stopped.**

She said: "Then it is not one works. There are other sites in other districts with the same nil in the same column, and if the same sum goes across all of —"

"**Stop.**"

It was Wenna Pruitt, and she did not raise her voice, and she has the council's books and no money and she has been in eleven rooms in this district since the fourth of the fourth month and has not once been thanked for anything and does not expect to be.

"You were about to do a sum that is about people who are not in this room," she said, "and I am going to say why that is a bad thing to do, and I am not going to say it because it is wrong, because I do not think it is wrong and I have thought about it since you started."

She put her hands flat on the desk, which is a thing she does.

"That is not a number any more, Ivy. The moment it is said in a room it stops being arithmetic and becomes a **policy**, and a policy is a thing an office can do, and in about four years somebody in an office is going to do it, and nobody in this district will ever be asked about it, and the reason nobody will be asked is that the people it is about are not in the return and there is no form in this city that reaches a person who is not in the return. **It would go into a minute in this building in about eleven minutes, because this building is a body and a body can minute things, and it is in four-inch capitals in a book that goes to an inspectorate, and in four years it is a precedent, and a precedent is the only instrument in this city that nobody has to vote for.**"

"That is his argument," said Ambrose, to nobody. "From the fifteenth of the sixth. A printed page has no address."

"It is his argument and it is mine, and I have been wrong about half of it since June, and neither of us is going to choose this afternoon." She looked round the room. "And here is the part neither of us has thought about, and Ivy has just thought about it by accident.

"**A page can be refused. A route can be refused. A number can be refused. A sum cannot be refused by anybody. It just sits in a room and waits for a meeting.** And in about four years there will be a meeting, and the sum will be in the papers of it, and it will be attributed to a person, and that person will be me or it will be him, and the man who has said the sum out loud in a room is the man who owns it. I have watched a woman say a rate figure in a room in June and carry it since."

Ivy Pike said, "I did not know that is what I was doing."

"I know you did not," said Wenna Pruitt. "That is why I stopped you and not anybody else."

---

**And the thing that happened at about half past twelve, which nobody in that room expected, and which is the reason any of it is worth writing down.**

Ambrose had a minute taken. He is about thirty and he has been at a counter eleven years and he does not belong in a room on the first floor of a works, and he had said at the counter that he had never once been asked what he thought about any of it, and he had a sheet in front of him and it was the first time in nine years that any document in this district had been made by a body that could be summoned.

He put the pen down and said:

"I want to say what this room is, and I have been waiting an hour and a half to say it and I am going to say it now and then I am going to hold my tongue for the rest of the afternoon.

"**This is the only room in this district that can produce a document with a body at the bottom of it.** Not a notice on a board, not a night book, not a chalk mark, not a return, not a form in a guild. A company. A limited company with a gate and a register and a clerk who takes minutes, and a company is a body, and a body is the first of the three requirements and it is the only one of the three that anybody in this district has ever been able to produce.

"And I am not going to let the afternoon go past without saying the rest of it. **Four hundred men, two shillings the head for a year, is eight hundred shillings, which is forty pounds.** I have done it in my head and I have not got a piece of paper and I am not going to get one.

"**And I want the room to sit with those two numbers together, because they are the whole of what I have got.** Nine thousand one hundred and fifty pounds a year is a sum. I have watched men in this district argue about a sum smaller than that and lose a fortnight to it, and Mr Vane has had nine thousand one hundred and fifty pounds in a drawer since 1949 and been right to be furious about it. Forty pounds is not a sum. **Forty pounds is a signature.** Forty pounds a year is a gate fund, and a gate fund is money, and this company could pay it out of a tin at the front office with a wages bill in it and not have a minute taken about it anywhere in this building.

"**And that is the whole of why you ought to be frightened of it, and I do not think anybody in this room is.** Anything anybody can afford is a thing that gets done on a Tuesday by somebody who has not read the question. It could be done in a morning. A form, a wet stamp, a signature at the top of it and a name at the bottom of it, and the standing would be the standing of the company, and the company's standing is the company's, and the company is not the district and four hundred men in a works are not a ward.

"That is the whole of it. That is what a body is, and it is the only one we have got, and it belongs to a man who has been in this room for an hour and a half and has been the most sensible person in it."

Nobody said anything for about half a minute.

"No," said Teo Arden, from the back, where he had been standing.

"No?"

"**No. And I am going to say why in four sentences and then I am not going to say anything else all afternoon.**" He came forward. "The reason is that you have just described a door. It is a real door. It is the only door in four districts that has got a company at the end of it, and it is a door that is open in the morning, and **it would be just as open at forty pounds a year as it would be at a hundred times forty pounds, and that is not a thing anybody in this room has said out loud yet and it is the only part of your speech this afternoon that frightens me**, and there are four hundred men who go through it every day.

"And the day this company lodges the first instrument in this district, that company is the district's representative in a room where the district is not allowed to speak, and in about four years that room will be asking it about a rate, and in about six it will be asking it about a schedule, and **it will answer, because a company answers, and everything a company answers is true and is about the company.**

"You have all been in this room for an hour and a half and nobody has noticed that the only thing that came out of it is a door, and I have been making lists since the fourth of the fourth month and I have never had one of those and I do not want it."

"Then what do you want," said Idris Vane.

"I want the four hundred of you to stop for a morning," said Teo Arden. "**That is all. Not a strike and not a ballot and not a committee and not a minute. A morning. And I am not going to call it and I am not going to organise it and I am not anybody's anything, and there is no meeting behind it, and if anybody tells anybody I gave an order on a gate they are lying and they can say so to my face.**

"And before anybody says that is pointless, I will say what it is for, and it is the only thing I have ever said out loud in a room that I actually believe, and Mr Vale will tell you it is true and he does not want to.

"**A levy is a number. A rate notice is a number. A form is a number. A company is a body with a gate and men on the gate. And a gate is the only thing in this district that a hundred and forty thousand people cannot get round, and it is the only thing here that can be stopped, and I have watched a hall, an Assembly, an office, a works and a directorate all sit round tables for eighteen years and the district has not moved one of them.**

"So the first of September, at the shift change, and I am saying it in this room in front of a clerk because I would like it in a minute in a building for once, and I would like somebody in this room to stop me."

Nobody stopped him.

---

**And on the Tuesday the twenty-third of the eighth month at about half past nine in the morning he was walking up the canal road with a works clerk's copy of the minute in his coat, and the last thing in that week happened in about forty seconds and nobody said a word about it.**

Sel Kade had said her piece at about half past eleven in the room and he had written it down and it is on the page and it is four lines and it is correct.

> *The works takes about nine thousand gallons a day off that main.*
> *The ward's reserve is the tank.*
> *The tank is the hospital's clock pressure and the hospital branch is a draw on the ward.*
> *The tank has been under two days since the second of the third month and the low-level alarm has been inhibited since the same date and no report has been made, and I am the person who answers for it, and after the first of the ninth I am not.*

He read it on the canal road at half past nine in the morning on the twenty-third of the eighth month, in the margin of somebody else's minute, in a coat, on a street, and then he stopped walking.

**The first of the ninth is the day the stoppage is called for.**

And the tank is the hospital's clock pressure, and the tank is under two days, and the alarm on the tank is inhibited and has been since the second of the third month and no report has been made, and the make-up comes out of the reserve and the reserve comes off the district main, and the district main at Northglass goes through a boundary sluice at the works' gate, and the sluice is a 1911 clock-driven valve, and it is the works' asset, and **it is opened by a works' man, because it is the works' valve and the works' men operate it, and there is a note in the warden's book, made in May, about a man of about forty at that gate, and it says he put his hand on a wheel for two hours one night while a woman with a bad hip went up and down a ladder.**

He did not write it down. He stood on the canal road outside the works' office and he did not write it down, and the reason he did not write it down is that he has been in this district for five months and he has learned one thing, and the thing he has learned is that **a thing written down in a book in a shop in the Clockwork Quarter is worth nothing in any room that is not the one it is in, and that this is a fact about the district and not about him, and that there is no form in this city on which a man with a notebook may go to four hundred people and say: stop for a morning, and here is what will happen to a hospital on the third of September, and here is the name of the man whose hand will be on the wheel.**

And there was no form, and he had looked, and a girl of twenty-six at a counter had told him the three requirements in eleven minutes in July and the first of them was a body and the second was an instrument and the third was a person, and the instrument was two shillings the head for a year, and the person was a person of the affected district, and **he could not have gone back to that counter on the Saturday and asked for a fourth requirement, because there is no fourth requirement, and the three of them are correct, and a machine that will not take a question it cannot be told the answer to is the only safeguard in this city that has never once been wrong.**

> *Twenty-third of the eighth. A company can minute things. A company is a body. A body is the first of the three requirements, and the second is two shillings the head for a year, and four hundred heads is eight hundred shillings, which is forty pounds, and a works pays forty pounds out of a tin without a minute being taken, **and a thing nobody argues about is a thing that gets done**, and there is a door in Northglass that is open every morning and four hundred men go through it.*
> *And the stoppage is called for the first of the ninth, which is the day Sel Kade stands down, and the tank is under two days and the alarm on the tank has been off since the second of the third month, and the make-up comes off a main that goes through a valve at the gate those four hundred men are going to stand in front of, and a man of about forty at that gate put his hand on a wheel for two hours in the fifth month of this year and there is no column in this city for the two hours and he was not thanked and he has not been asked.*
> *I did not write it on the page. I have written it here instead, in a book, on the night of the twenty-third, because I have got six months and twenty-four days and no form, and I have said that I am not going to be the man who tells a district what it can afford in a room where somebody can refuse it, and I have said it four times this year, and I have never once had to say it to a person with a picket in his hand.*
> *I am not going to go and tell him. That is a decision and I made it at half past nine in the morning on a canal road and I have not told anybody that I made it, and if he finds out in September from a newspaper that somebody worked it out, I will not have been the somebody. That is a coward's arrangement and it is the best one available to me, and I am recording it in the only book in this city that nobody has to answer for, and if that is not good enough then it was never going to be.*
