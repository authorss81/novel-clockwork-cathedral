# Chapter 23 — The Guild's Four-Line Reply

Three requests went into the Clockwork Quarter guild hall on the morning of Monday the twenty-seventh of the month, and they were the same question, and they went in before eleven, and the hall's answer was on the mat by a quarter past two.

That was the part. Not the answer. Orrin had a strong idea by the Sunday night of what the answer would be, because he had read the answer already, in writing, in this shop, on the twenty-fourth of the first month, and it had been four lines long and had not been wrong, and the card said the same four things in the hall's own type with the letter's own *is asked to note* and one connective with it left off to save a line.

What he did not have, and what nobody had, was the four hours.

---

The first went in from the Office of Public Reckoning, because that office was the only one in the city that had any standing to ask a guild for anything at all, and because Mara Venn had said at half past eight on the Friday, in the passage outside that room with her coat already on, "I am going to ask for the book, and I am going to do it in the morning, and I want you to understand that I am doing it because it is the correct instrument and not because I think it will work."

The second went in from the tenant council of the pumping district, on the council's paper, with a signature that was Wenna Pruitt's and a second signature that was a woman's from the Weir One gate, and the council's address was an upper room over a chandler's shop, and the clerk wrote it down exactly. The council's request carried four pounds ten in coin, which was the whole of what the tenant council had, and the clerk wrote that down as well.

The third went in from Hanna Stroud, in her own name, as the owner of nine buildings in the district, asking a question about the uninsurability of one of them, and she telephoned it in herself at a quarter to eleven and dictated it and made him read it back to her.

"That is three," said Orrin, "and the second and the third have real standing and the first has an office."

"Then the hall will answer the first one slowly, because my office is not the hall's problem, and it will answer the second and the third quickly, because they are the hall's problem." Mara Venn put her hat on. "I have asked for six days. Come and find me at four and I shall have something, and if I have nothing at four I shall have a reason at four, and either of those is worth the walk."

---

He went down the hill anyway at about one, because he had four shillings' worth of reason to walk into a hall, and because he wanted to see the licensing floor in daylight on a Monday, and because he had not been in the building since the morning he had gone in and asked Hask to reduce a governor to unrecorded scrap and had been refused.

The licensing floor at one o'clock on a Monday was a room with a counter down the middle of it and a smell of paraffin and hot brass, and there were eleven men in it, and at the back, above a door with a wired glass light in it, there was a board.

The board was five notices long. He had read the top four on the first Monday of the month and had not liked them.

---

**NOTICE — STANDING ORDERS, LICENSING FLOOR.**

*1. Applications for the alteration of a listed bench address to be made in duplicate, one month before the alteration takes effect.*

*2. Fees are payable in advance and are not returnable. A fee paid on a day the counter is shut is held over and does not carry.*

*3. Notices of loss of certificate are to be made in writing within four days of the loss.*

*4. Any holder expecting to be absent from his bench for more than fourteen days shall notify this floor.*

*5. Renewals. A renewal is complete only when the fee, the endorsement, and the work book in hall form have all been received at this counter. The counter receives renewals on the day of expiry only, and between the hours of ten and twelve.*

He read the fifth one four times.

It was not in his own notice card. His card, printed in several thousand and sent to two hundred and forty men, carried four things and asked for four things: the fee, the date, the endorsement of one master, and the work book, hall form W-4, current issue. It said *current licence expires: the nineteenth of the month after next*. It did not say ten o'clock. It did not say twelve. It did not say a Sunday, because the card had been printed in a month when nobody was thinking about which day the nineteenth would be.

And notice five did not say the fifteenth, or the eighteenth, or the seventeenth. It said the day of expiry, and two hours.

He worked it out on the back of his own hand with a pencil, which is a thing he had done four hundred times, and got it, and got it again.

A month ago a man with a class-three licence who wanted to renew had four weeks to bring three things to a counter that opened at nine and shut at four and was shut at the weekend. Since some point in the last month — since the card went out, or since the cards were printed, or since a clerk noticed a discrepancy in a column and tidied it — the whole of a man's trade had been compressed into a two-hour window on one day, and the day was the nineteenth, and the nineteenth was a Sunday.

He looked up the fifth notice on the wall for about a minute and a half.

"You've read that one before, haven't you," said a man on his left, who was perhaps fifty and had a bandaged left hand.

"No."

"Twice," the man said. "Everybody does. It's on the board and the card's on the bench and the two don't say the same thing, and the board is the one that's true." He got his licence out of his coat and put it on the counter. "I'm in the same hole as you. Nineteenth of next month, ten to twelve, and there's a queue outside by about ten past nine, and if you're in the queue you want the fee and the book and the endorsement all in one hand, because if you go out of that door at twelve you've not got a trade."

"How many are there?"

"There's no knowing," the man said, "and it isn't the master's doing, and I'd say that to him to his face and he'd agree with me."

---

He was at the far end of the floor, by the shelves of certificate stock, at about twenty past one, when Della Marsh came round the end of the counter with a bundle, and she stopped.

Neither of them said anything for a moment.

"You've come for a form," she said.

"I've come to read the board."

"You've read the board." She put the bundle down on a high shelf above her head, which is a thing a person of sixty-one can do because she has done it eleven thousand times, and then she took her spectacles off and cleaned them, which took longer than it needed to. "It's a good board. It is the only thing in this building that is not a conversation, and the master has had it up since nineteen-something, and I put the numbers on it myself, and I could not tell you the last time anybody in this hall read the fifth one."

"Who changed it?"

"Nobody changed it," said Della Marsh. "That's what I'd have you understand and I've got about ninety seconds before the floor wants me. Nobody changed it. It's printed once a year with the cards and it is set by the forms printer along with eleven other standing orders, and the slot on that board is older than the two of us have been in this trade, though the words in it are not — five years ago it said *on any working day in the last week of the term* and this year it says what it says, and that is a sentence in a printed book that nobody read until a man stood in front of a board and wanted something off it."

She put her spectacles on.

"You've still not asked me for anything, Mr Vale. Thirty-one years, and I have a very exact sense of the shape of a transaction, and you have been standing in front of my shelves for four minutes admiring a notice."

"I came to read the board."

"You came to read the board, and now you have read it, and in about six weeks you are going to come down here with a fee in your hand and a work book in a bag, and the counter will be shut at twelve, and that is the whole of what has happened to you today." She picked the bundle up again. "And the thing you have not asked me and are going to want is the work book, and I am going to tell you the thing about it because I said in that room that I would rather you got there by yourself than that I walked you, and you did not get there by yourself, and I would rather be useful than consistent."

"Go on."

"W-4, current issue, is two pages, and it is a two-sided sheet, and it is printed in the form in stacks of two hundred at a time and it comes out of that press about four times a year, and the issue changes when the press is re-set, and the issue changes without notice to anybody and without any letter, because there is nowhere to put a letter. That is why the card says *current issue* and does not give a number." She looked at the shelves instead of nodding at them. "You have never seen the current issue. I can see from your face that you have never seen one. And I am not going to hand you one off this shelf, and if you come back and ask me for one I shall not have it, and if you had it now you would fill it in wrong and I would have to look at it and I do not want to look at it."

"Then how am I supposed to—"

"Go to the counter with the fee and ask for the work book, and the counter will give you the current issue, and the counter gives it to anybody who asks and there is no fee on it and there is no form on it and that is a thing this hall has never found a way to stop." She shifted the bundle. "The fifth notice does not stop it either, because the fifth notice is about a *renewal* and a work book on its own is not a renewal. A man can come on any working day of the year, and get one, and nobody will write a word about it. And a man can do that before he decides anything."

She got a step stool out from under the counter and stopped with one foot on it.

"You will want to know whether I think that is deliberate."

"I did not say I did."

"You did not," said Della Marsh. "You stood there and you read a board and you did not say a word, and that is the single most useful thing anybody has done in this corridor in about eleven years, and I have never been thanked for anything, and I would like it noted by whoever is keeping the record that the first half of that is free."

She got up the step and put the bundle on the top shelf.

---

The hall's answer came up on a board at twenty past two and it was on three sheets, one to each request, and it was the same card.

Four lines, set in the guild's own type, on a card the size of a call slip, with a form number in the corner and no letter and no signature:

> *The hall keeps a certification ledger, which is a record of machines. The hall does not keep a record of hours for work that is not part of a certified shift, there being no such hours, there being no such shifts. Form GH-9 is a request form and not a record. The hall's price schedules are the hall's own property.*

Orrin stood in the licensing floor at twenty past two on the twenty-seventh of the month and read it, and then read the other two, which were the same card, character for character, including the bit that was about his own form number, which was the answer he had been reading for a month in a letter off the same press, and he understood that four hours had gone by in the answering.

Not because they were slow. Because the answer did not have to be thought about. Somewhere in that building there was a card in a drawer with a form number on it, and it had been in the drawer before Orrin was apprenticed, and it went out about four times a year, and the only decision anybody in the hall had ever made about it was the decision about which drawer.

He got on a tram at half past two and got off at the Rise and went up to the second floor and put it on Mara Venn's table.

---

"Four hours," she said.

"Four hours."

"Not fourteen months." She was not looking at it. "That's the requisition, and I know exactly where the requisition is, because I can see it in the tray, and it is in the tray behind six other things and it says *referred, series, awaiting production*, and it is dated today, and it will come in about fourteen months, and I have written my own name and the date on the front of it in pencil, because that is what you do with a requisition you have watched get made and you cannot stop.

"And this — " she turned the card round on the table — "this is not the answer to the requisition. This is the answer to the *question*. And the question is a free question, and a free question is one you can ask a man in a corridor, and a man in a corridor will tell you the truth, and the truth here is four lines long and has been true for a very long time." She sat down. "Orrin. This is not the hall being clever. This is the hall being *finished*. There is nothing to think about. They have thought about it. They thought about it years ago and they printed it and they put it in a drawer, and it has sat there ever since waiting for somebody to ask, and the reason it came back so fast is that it has been sitting there done."

"How many times a year?"

"Four. Six in a bad year." She put the card in her own file, which was a thing she did with cards, and he understood that she had a drawer of them.

"Whose file does it end up in when it goes back upstairs?"

"It does not go back upstairs. There is no upstairs. The Office of Public Reckoning has a drawer and four staff and one window, and I have a note on the twenty-sixth of last month with the same four lines on it in the same type, and a note on the twentieth, and a note on the eighth of the month before that, and the reason I have kept them all is that in nine years I have learned that a refusal can be appealed and a note cannot, and this is not a note, and I would like to know what on earth it is."

---

"May I say the thing nobody has said?"

"Go on."

"The hall's defence is not a cover story," said Mara Venn. "That is what I have taken three days to get to and it took me about nine seconds this morning. Everything you and I have been calling their position — the price schedule, the nine series, the form number, the *there being no such hours* — is not a thing they have been saying to keep a district out. It is a *definition*. They do not keep hours because hours are not a thing they keep. The work was not done, and the reason the work was not done is that the crew was not certified, and the reason the crew was not certified is that there was no money to pay for certifying it, and that has a form and a class and a price on it, and the form is lawful, and the price is the hall's own property.

"And a definition that is true cannot be beaten by producing more evidence." She put her elbows on the table. "That is the whole of the month. We have been producing evidence for a month. A board. Four ledgers. Eleven copies. Six returns printed in full with the minutes of this Assembly, which is the best thing anybody has ever done in this city, and I mean that, and it is going into a binder. And a binder does not argue with a definition. It just sits there being true."

"Which is what the bridge does," said Orrin.

"It is exactly what the bridge does, and I have known that for three years, and I said it to you in a corridor, and you told me you wanted a district in the frame rather than a piece of cast iron, and I was right and you were right and we have both been right in a stairwell and neither of us has won anything." She almost laughed. "So here is the other thing, and I have been not saying it since Friday, and I am saying it now because we have run out of things to say that we can print.

"A *name*."

---

"Go on," said Orrin.

"A name on the withdrawal form." She had a piece of paper and she was not writing on it, which he noticed. "GH-9. Standing withdrawal. It has a countersignature line, and the countersignature is *for the affected district*, and a district is not a person, so the hall has let a warden's office fill a line that is supposed to stand for a place. Change that line. Require a named officer. Then every withdrawal in this city that has ever been priced at two shillings the head for a year has a man's name on it, and the man's name is *a person who agreed*, and the hall cannot then say that the district was not there.

"That is it. That is the whole of it, and it is a one-line change to a form, and it is the only thing anybody has ever proposed to me that I have not been able to talk myself out of."

"Mara—"

"I know what it costs." She put the pen down. "I have known since about a quarter past four yesterday afternoon and I have been not saying it for a day and a half, and the reason I have been not saying it is that I am not the only person in this city who has been carrying that particular page around, and I watched a man read it in a file room and put it back in the drawer and go out of the building, and I did not say a word then either.

"A name on that line is the woman who countersigned batch twenty-one slash four at the age of twenty-six. It is her. It is not the hall's clerk. It is not a form. It is a woman who was not the warden of anything, who was handed a pen in a room, and who signed because she was twenty-six and in a room and nobody told her what the line said. And if the line carries names, then in five years' time, when somebody has counted properly, there is a way to find out exactly which rooms and which years and which officers, and the first name on that list is hers, and she will be sixty-one instead of forty-four, and she will have a reserve that is still empty and a tenant review still owed and a signature she did not read."

The room on the second floor of a building on the Rise was very quiet, and out of the window the light was going off the Cathedral.

"And I am not saying it is wrong," said Mara Venn. "I am saying it is *expensive*, and that it is the cheapest expensive thing on the table, and that I have been the only person in this city for four weeks holding it, and I would like somebody else to hold it for a minute so that I can find out whether I still want it."

Orrin looked at her for long enough that she said, "No. No, I did not mean you. You have a name and no standing and it is the wrong way round and we worked that out last night, in a cold room above a bathhouse, with the lamp pulled over. I meant somebody who has to be answerable for it in a chamber. And there is nobody, and that is the answer, and I have had it since the sixteenth and I did not like it, and I have been enjoying two days of not having said it out loud."

---

They got up at half past three because the box on the second floor was emptied at noon and had been emptied at noon, and the count for anything that wanted a reply ran from the day after, and there was one thing left on Mara Venn's list that had to be tried because she had promised to try it.

"Can a form be changed," she said, "by anybody in this city, this year, that is not the guild?"

"No."

"Can it be changed by the Assembly?"

"Not this year. The guild's forms are the guild's, under the ordinance, and the ordinance is older than the Assembly's standing orders, and the Assembly can request a change and the guild can decline, and a request is not a thing anybody can be made to answer, which is why there is a form in your drawer and not a vote."

"Continuity Office?"

"They inspect. They do not legislate. And Rill is a decent man and he has a column with four inches in it and it says *within tolerance*." Mara Venn put her hat on. "And the Cathedral could do it, because the Cathedral uses the form every time it certifies an omission, and the Cathedral does not legislate either. The Cathedral asks. That is what the Cathedral has got. It asks, and the guild sometimes says yes, and when the guild says yes it is because the price went up."

She put the card back in the drawer and shut it.

"There is a sealed archive under the Cathedral with forty-one entries in a single batch in it, and there is a working book in this building with nine series in it, and between the two of them they are the whole of what has been withdrawn from every district in this city, and the working book is a list of sites," she said. "I asked for the index of the sealed one a year ago and got a courteous note, and there is no form in the world that opens it, and nothing that has been done in this month to any form in this city has brought it one inch nearer being opened. I mention it because you are going to be tempted, and because it is the only room I know of where the answer is, and because it is the room you will never be in."

---

Orrin walked down the hill at twenty to four and stopped at the bottom of it, at the corner where the tram wires went over the street, and got the grey book out of his coat, and stood in the rain on a public pavement to write down a thing that was not a machine.

*Monday the 27th, the second month. Three requests, one question, three identical cards back in four hours. The hall's defence is a definition and a definition cannot be beaten with evidence. My whole month has been a binder.*

*Fourth hours. Not fourteen months. Four hours, and a card with a form number, in a drawer, since before I was apprenticed, waiting for somebody to ask.*

*Notebook, licensing floor, notice five: a renewal is complete only when fee, endorsement and work book have all been received at the counter, and the counter receives renewals on the day of expiry only, between ten and twelve. The card says the nineteenth. The card does not say ten or twelve. A month ago a man had four weeks to bring three things to a counter that shut at four and shut at the weekend. He has two hours on one Sunday morning, and he found out about it on a Monday, by typesetting.*

*It costs nothing. I have done the arithmetic three times on a wet pavement. The W-4 has to be written out of a book that is not a form and was never going to be ready in a fortnight, let alone a day, and the fee is 4s 6d and the endorsement is a signature and a signature is a man who will be in that room on the nineteenth. The whole of it was never the constraint. The book is the constraint, and the book is two thousand pages in my own hand, and a hand is the only thing I have got that a hall cannot print.*

*So the window is free, and the board on the licensing floor is a real thing, and it has cost me nothing at all, and I have read it in the wrong week. Notice five would have cost a man in an ordinary year exactly nothing, and it will cost a man exactly nothing in every year after this one, and the whole of what I have lost went out of my trade in typesetting, in the last month, while I was busy wanting a book.*

*That is what is underneath all of it. Not a threat. A bill. Hask's bill. Hask did not have to move a single thing. I have moved it myself, on a Monday, in the rain, by wanting a book, and I shall go down there on the nineteenth of the third month with a fee in my hand and a form in a bag and I shall be glad to.*

He shut the book and got on a tram and sat in it with it shut on his knees for eleven minutes.

At half past four on the twenty-seventh he went into the Clockwork Quarter guild hall and asked the man on the licensing counter for a blank work book, hall form W-4, current issue, and the man gave it to him, and it cost nothing, and it is legal, and it is not on any form in the world, and nobody wrote a word about it.
