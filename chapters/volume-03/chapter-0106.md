# Chapter 106 — The Nurse's Kitchen

Monday the thirtieth of the first month was cold enough that the ink skinned in the well if he left the lid off, and Orrin worked with the stove lit and the door shut and the letter still face down on the shelf.

Nella Rook came at eleven. She was twenty-eight, a hospital clock nurse in Green Span and Leda's colleague, and Imani Rook's niece by blood, which the ward knows and which is not going to be said out loud in a kitchen tonight. A hospital clock nurse keeps medicine, staff and public clocks reconciled against each other; it is a nursing specialty and it exists because the hospitals discovered what a Reading costs.

She had a carpet bag and a token in a twist of paper and no appointment, and she put all three on the counter.

"Miss Rook," Orrin said.

"Mr Vale. Leda said you'd tell me true whether I'm buying hope or brass."

"How does Leda put it?"

"She puts it more neatly. You put it more usefully." She took her scarf off and folded it into her bag, which is what a nurse does in a room she intends to be in for an hour. "I'll start and you can stop me. I want to buy a hundred minutes for a ward's bracket clock and I have four shillings and sixpence of my own, and every single person I have told about it has told me it is a wonderful idea except the one I most wanted to tell."

"Who was that?"

"A clockmaker. He said it was a wonderful idea and then he would not do it, and he would not say why, and I found out on the way home that the why was that he has no licence and no standing and that a man with no standing cannot spend a district's minutes however much he wants to."

He unwrapped the twist. G, a hundred, year and die on the rim. Real to the eye, weight right in the hand, but the eye was not an assay and he said so.

"You've had brass before," he said. "You've held it and you've looked at it and you've worked out what it says, and you've come to me, and I would like to know which part of that is hope and which part is brass, because I am going to answer both and I would rather you knew which one I was answering."

"The brass is hope," Nella said. "The brass is four shillings and sixpence of brass, and I paid it on a Tuesday from my own money, and it is the only purchase I have made in nine years that nobody can take back. Hope is the rest of it. Hope is a woman in a kitchen with a clock on the table and a piece of brass she has already decided the ward needs more than I have decided it needs."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I take the brass home and I keep it and I am exactly where I was on the twelfth, and I will be a woman who bought a hundred minutes she did not spend, which is not a fool, it is a nurse." She put one finger on the twist of paper. "If you say yes I will be a woman who bought a hundred minutes and put them in a clock in front of four people, and Leda will be right about it out loud in the room, and I will have to live in the room with that, and so will she. I have thought about both. I would rather have the second, which is why I am here and not at the ward."

"What do you want it for?"

"My ward," she said. "Medicine times. We run on the free master and write the line clock beside it, and the line is three hours out and stepping fifty seconds a night, and the ward's slack is forty minutes on the best night of the year. A hundred minutes is two and a half times the whole of the slack. Two tokens is five times. I want to buy a hundred minutes for the ward's bracket clock, in the kitchen, where the night staff can see it done and argue with it."

"Argument is the part that matters."

"It is the only part I am sure of. A thing that is done in a corridor is a thing that is done. A thing that is done at a table with four women round it is a thing four women can refuse to help with, and if four women can refuse to help with it then it is not a rule, it is a favour, and I would rather have the favour argued about in a kitchen than the rule not mentioned in one."

"Whose kitchen?"

She smiled for the first time. "Yours if you'll have us. No — ours. Fourth floor, 9 Bellweather? No. Green Span nurses' kitchen, off the ward, gas ring, table, list on a nail, the same size as every other kitchen in this city. Four of us, off shift, no matron, no Directorate, no guild. Leda will be there and disagree with me and neither of us will be asked to choose."

"How many of the four are paid by the ward?"

"Three. I am paid by nothing at the moment, which I will be next month, and which is why I can put four and six on a table and be told it is my money and have it be true." She zipped the carpet bag shut. "Do not say anything kind about that. It is a coincidence of a calendar."

On the first of the second month he went to Green Span at seven in the evening with his tool roll and the narrow book and no brass of his own. He had meant to write it as the thirty-first, and the first month has thirty days, and the thirty-first is a Tuesday that this city does not have, and the book in his pocket had told him so on the Monday night and he had written Wednesday anyway, which is the same class of mistake as the forty-one seconds and cost nothing at all.

The nurses' kitchen was nine by eleven like all the others, a gas ring, a table, a list on a nail for tea money. Four women off shift: Nella, Leda, a night sister of nineteen who was paid at the ward rate and whose name on the board was E. Cobb, and a student. No Assembly man, no Directorate man, no guild man. The bracket clock from the ward was on the table, eleven inches high, its back off, and Cobb had laid the screws out in a line the way she had seen somebody do it once in a paper.

Nella put the token on the table. "Four shillings and sixpence," she said. "A hundred and twenty nights. My money, not the ward's. The ward has no fund. I am buying it the way Miriam Coss bought hers, and for the same reason: official tolls took time and no instrument will give it back lawfully."

Leda leaned against the sink. "And I disagree," she said, evenly. "Not with the arithmetic. With the spending. A token spent in a ward clock that is not the clock it was cut from takes the minutes out of the Quarter's margin and puts them into the network, and the network gives them to the next clock behind, which is not this ward but the Weirs branch that is hungrier. You will have bought four months for a clock and paid for them with somebody else's margin, and the somebody else has no name, which is the whole of what the last two years were about."

"Then what?" Nella said. "Leave the medicine times to drift?"

"Say the number out loud in front of the people whose hours they are before you spend it," Leda said. "That is my rule and it has won an interval and no hours at all. Say that a hundred minutes is two and a half times forty, that two tokens cost nine shillings, that the ward has no fund, that the branch has been stepping about two hundred and eighty-five nights now, and two tokens last two hundred and forty. Say it where families can read it. Then spend if you still want to, and I will wind with you."

"You have never once actually wound with me," Nella said.

"I have wound with you every night for two years," Leda said, "at forty seconds a night, by hand, and that is the difference and you know it is."

The student wrote it out in the ward's hand, badly, and the line was: 1st second, Green Span nurses' kitchen, 4 present, 1 token, 4s6d, own money, 100 min, ward bracket, said aloud before spending — and then the two words *not spent* with a line through them, because Leda had told her to leave room to write them either way.

"Will you put my name on the card if we do it? I have asked twice."

"We will," Nella said. "Anybody who wants her name off it can come and take it off and we will write down who and when. Nobody took it off last time."

"Then put mine on now," Cobb said, "not later. Later is when a person is ashamed."

The student wrote it on and Cobb looked at it for a while and did not say anything, and Orrin discovered that he was watching a nineteen-year-old woman decide whether to be visible on a piece of paper in a room with three people in it who would have to keep it visible, and that he had no standing to comment on it and that this was the correct arrangement.

"Then whose name is on the card for the spending?" the student said. "If we are all four on it and it turns out to have been somebody else's minutes, then it is four names and a hole, and the four names are worse for the hole than one."

Nella looked at her. "Mine. Because it is my money and my argument. If it is wrong then it is my mistake in a hand people can point at, and that is what a name is for."

Orrin checked the bracket clock's beat, cleaned the crutch, set it true, and refused to spend the token, for want of standing, in the same words he had used in Bellweather. He wound the movement on the test stand, set it by the free master on the landing, and the whole table of them watched the second hand come round to the right place, and none of them said anything at all while it did it, which he noticed and wrote down later.

He wrote the refusal in the grey ledger at the table, with the date, and both nurses watched him do it.

He wrote it slowly, which was not for them. It was because a refusal is read later by somebody who was not in the room and the whole of its value is in being readable, and the woman at the top of the stairs two days earlier had asked him for exactly this and had known what she was asking for, and he had given it to her in four lines on a torn page and he was going to give these four women the same four lines and not one word more.

"I will not put it in," he said. "I have no licence. But I will write down that you asked, and that Leda disagreed, and that neither of you was asked to choose, and that no person was thanked in this kitchen."

Nella wrapped the token back in its twist. "Then I am still holding brass," she said. "And brass is honest. It weighs what it weighs. What it is for is the hole."

"Take it and spend it if you want," Leda said. "On your own name, on your own money, in a room with one person in it and no card on the wall. That is lawful and that is yours and I will not say a word about it."

"Would that be better?"

"It would be less," Leda said. "It would be one fewer person who knows."

On Wednesday the second of the second month Orrin wrote the kitchen at length in the shop, because a working woman buying a hundred minutes in a kitchen was a working woman and not a symbol, and the reason she gave was her ward and its medicine times and not a family member on a committee.

He also wrote, and this was the part he had to stop and think about, that a woman in a kitchen in Bellweather Road and a woman in a nurses' kitchen in Green Span had within four days both been told the same sentence by the same man, and neither of them had been told about the other, and the reason neither could be told about the other was that the only thing Orrin had that connected the two kitchens was a token with a district letter on it and a refusal with a date under it, and a brass disc is not a relationship.

*Green Span nurses' kitchen, 1st second, evening. N. Rook, 28, hospital clock nurse, buys G100 for ward, kitchen, four women, no officials. L. Vale disagrees, neither chooses. Refused to spend for want of standing. Token held, not spent. Nobody thanked.*

He put the pencil down. The letter was on the shelf, face down, dated the eleventh of the first month, and it was the second of the second, and it was twenty-one days old. He had read it four times and answered it once, in his own head, with a sentence about refusal procedure that was not an answer to anything she had asked, and which he would probably post on Monday, and which would be a question dressed as a letter because a question was a thing he knew how to send.

The city was buying back its own time at four shillings and sixpence a hundred minutes while he swept his step, and he had still not decided about the Tuesday, and a man who has not decided about a Tuesday and a man who has decided no are in the same place on the page, which is the trouble with a page.
