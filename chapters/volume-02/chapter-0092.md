# Chapter 92 — The Column for the Names

The seventh day is the tenth of the twelfth, and the sixth day is the ninth, and between them there are six days in which the only notice this city has ever given anybody has to be made by hand, by people who can be refused, in a form that does not exist; and the last thing that has to be made by hand on that row is a column for eleven names, and the only way to fill a column for eleven names is to go along twenty-two doorsteps, and the asking is the filling, and they have been the same act since the fourteenth of the eleventh and nobody worked that out until the fifth of December; and a man at a works' gate has been carrying the only record in this district of the level of a tank in his coat for about three months, and there is no form on which to ask him for it, and he was not asked, and he offered, and the offer was refused, and the reason the offer was refused is the reason a man in a cellar was not asked in the second month, and the two are the same sentence and the sentence is that a piece of paper in a coat is not a thing anybody is entitled to; and two operators came, one of whom had never been to this district and has now been twice, and neither of them will sign.

---

The shop is shut on Sundays and has been shut on Sundays since 1931, and on Sunday the fourth of the twelfth he was not in it. He was in his own kitchen in Verge Lane with a cup of tea and the Advertiser of the twenty-eighth of the eleventh, which he buys and does not read, and the six days were two-thirds of the way from a plan to a thing and he had found four people and three of them had said yes and the fourth had not said anything yet, and the fourth was **Nell Sayer**, and she had not said anything because on the Friday night she had worked out that a row of twenty-two doors at ten o'clock on a Saturday morning is either two hours or three depending on whether anybody opens their door to anybody, and she was not going to promise a number to a street, and she had gone to bed.

The seven days' notice is the finding of the Friday and it needed a form on Monday to be a fact.

The counter of the Water Users' Committee is above a chandler's shop in Bellweather and it is open from nine to four and on the Monday the fifth of the twelfth he went up the outside stair at about half past ten and asked **Nadia Erridge** for a copy of the notice of the eighth of October, and she said no, and then she said why, and then she said the thing he had not come for and which is the reason he came.

"**A notice is not a document.** A document is a thing you may be asked to produce and I have produced about four hundred of them. A notice is a sheet with a date on it and a week in it and it goes on a board and it comes off the board and it goes in the drawer in this room and the drawer is a drawer. I posted it on the Saturday morning with a drawing-pin and a scrap of lino and I took it down on the Saturday after the sitting and I did not keep it and I was not asked to keep it and if I had kept it I would have been asked to produce it, **and then it would have been a record of what the committee knew on the eighth, which is a different thing from what the committee did on the fifteenth, and there are people in this city who would not thank me for handing them the first of the two.**"

"Read me the foot of it."

"**There is no form for it either.** I want you to hear that twice, because you have come in here eight times this year asking me for things and I have said no to about five of them, and this is the first time I have been asked for the one thing in this building that does not have a form under it, and I did not know that until about a year ago and nobody ever asked me. **The foot of the notice is mine and it is in my hand and there is no printed line for it.**"

"Then read it to me."

> *"The committee is not able to consult the district, the district having no standing under the water act."*

"That is on every notice that office has posted since the fourth month and it is on none of the printed ones, and the reason it is on mine is that the ordinance has one line for the thing being done and no line for the people it is being done to, **and a clerk does her best inside a form with one line for it, and I put the other half in because I would rather be wrong in public than have printed a notice that nobody could tell was about them.**"

"And how many people read it?"

"**On that board?** About eleven a day, and I have counted, and eleven of those are him." She did not look up, because she does not look up. "The board is nine feet by four and it is outside a pump house and a pump house is a place a man goes when something is wrong, so the people who read a ward board are the people who already know. **I have been putting things on that board for four years in front of a man who is going to find out what is on it before anybody else in this district does.**"

He wrote nothing down in the room. He wrote it down afterwards, in a shop on Verge Lane, in about nine minutes, and the nine minutes are the entire working of this chapter and they are on the page in the grey book and nowhere else.

> *Fifth of the twelfth. A notice is not a document. A notice has no form. The foot of it is in a clerk's hand because the ordinance has one line for the thing and no line for the people, and a notice of a rehearsal has neither the form nor the line, and a person standing on a doorstep has neither, and is the only one of the three that anybody in this district can actually be reached by.*
> *So the asking is the notice. **The sixth day is tomorrow. There are six days and no form and no clerk and I cannot make anybody make it and I cannot stop anybody else from making it and if four people from two streets walk down that row on Saturday morning at about ten and say it out loud, then this district will for the first time in nineteen years have given seven days' notice of something, and it will have given it in daylight, to the people it concerns, in words, in person, which is the whole of the seven days' notice in this city with the board and the drawer and the clerk's third line taken out and the other half put back in.**
> *And I cannot post it. A ward board is read by eleven people. Twenty-two doorsteps are read by twenty-two.*

He went down to Sluice Row on the Monday afternoon at about two o'clock, in daylight, and counted the clocks again, because the eleven were counted from the pavement in daylight by two people in the middle of the November and he is one of the two and a number that was counted once in a hurry in a wet month ought to be counted twice in a dry one.

**The eleven are eleven.** Nine are on the front of houses and two are on the flanks at the alley ends and all of them are 1911 or later and all of them are off the Weir Two branch, because the row was put in in 1889 off the canal and had its own cistern and the eleven clocks came in with the houses, and there is a difference between a clock on the branch and a clock on a cistern and the difference is the entire cost of the morning of the seventeenth.

And on the Monday at number nineteen, going along the pavement on the west side, he found the **twelfth**, and it took him about four minutes and it is the whole of what the second count was for.

It is on the flank of a passage, at about shoulder height, on the wall of the second court, and it is a bracket clock of about 1890 with a bell on a bracket above it and a painted white face with the numerals gone, and it is on the cistern, and from the pavement you cannot see it, and the two people who counted the eleven in the middle of the November stood in the middle of the street and looked left and right and did not go into a passage, and that is not carelessness. **A count made from a pavement is a count of what a person on a pavement can see, and a street is not a survey, and the instrument was a pair of eyes and the pair of eyes is a street.**

He wrote it on the back of his hand and then wrote it in the shop, and the twelfth clock has no name against it and no house against it yet, because the second court is reached through a passage and the passage is between numbers seventeen and nineteen and there are two households on it and one of them is number nineteen and the other has never been counted by anybody.

**And that is the whole of the column.** A column with a number in it that was wrong by one, and no way to know it was wrong except going into the passage, and the only way to go into the passage is to knock.

Ivo Pell came on the Tuesday the sixth at ten past four in the afternoon, which is the time he came in the ninth of the November and is not a time anybody would choose twice by accident, and he did not come into the shop, and he stood in it with his hands behind his back and read the wall of figures off the top of the bench where Orrin had chalked them for want of a board, and then said the thing he had come to say and it took about ninety seconds and he did not sit down.

"**The terms for Saturday morning, and I am giving them to you on the Tuesday for the sixth time and I have given them three times and you have written them down three times and the fourth one is the one that counts, so here it is.**

"Three clocks. One question, in the same words, written down before the day, and the words are the words and not a version of the words. The minute is half past seven in the morning and I chose it in September and I have never told you why and I am not going to tell you why until after, and if you cannot wait for that then you should not have asked two men who do not sign to read three clocks for you.

"**Each of us writes down what his clock says and his own name against it, and hands it to nobody.** Not to you. You will not hold three sheets. You will be at the third clock and you will write yours and you will hand it to nobody, and if you find yourself holding three sheets you will have invented an instrument and you will not know that you have done it.

"And on the third one — and this is the whole of what I have come on the Tuesday for — **nothing about Saturday is admissible.** It is not a reading, it is not a finding, it is not certified, it is not on any rail, it is not in any file, and there is no form in this city on which any part of it could be made to be any of those things, and that is not a defect in it and it is the reason it is safe. **A thing that cannot be used is the only kind of instrument anybody in this district has ever been allowed to hold, and you have spent five months trying to get hold of a usable one, and this is what one looks like, and it will not tell you whether Saturday was right.**

"And I will not sign it. I said so in November, twice, and I am saying it now for the fourth time, and if anybody in Bellweather has told you that I have softened on it, they have told you a thing they wanted to be true."

"You have not softened on it."

"**I have not softened on anything, and there is a difference and the difference is the whole of what you are about to have on Saturday and you will not be able to see it until the middle of next year.**"

He went out at about ten past five and did not say goodbye, and he was right to, because a goodbye at the door of a shop is a thing two people say, and they did not.

On the Wednesday the seventh, at about eleven in the morning, **Leda Vale came to the Lower Weirs on a hospital bus and brought a woman of nineteen with her, and the reason is in one sentence and the sentence is a good one and neither of them pretended it was anything else.**

The ward's line clocks are on the branch. The ward's total slack between the free clocks and the line clocks, across eleven patients and three staff, is forty minutes, and it is on a card in a nurses' room with a date on it, and the card is the only instrument in this city that has ever caught a cost. **Three clocks on that branch are going to be read on Saturday morning by two people who will not sign, and the branch is the branch the ward is on, and a hospital a mile and a quarter away has no standing in anything that is going to happen to it and did not ask to have one and would not know what to do with one.**

"I have brought her and not come myself," said Leda Vale, "because I have a ward and she has a card, and there is a difference and you know what it is."

**E. Cobb is nineteen and is the night sister and is paid at the ward rate and has asked twice to be named and has not been taken off.** She had a card in the apron with about seventy columns of pencil on it, and at the end of the card, in a space she had ruled herself on the Sunday, there was a new column, and the new column had four lines in it.

The column was headed: *17. xii. ward line clocks.*

The four lines had nothing in them but the first, and the first had a time on it.

"**Three o'clock. Twenty-four to twenty-five minutes. Eleven lines.**"

"That is not a reading off a clock."

"That is a quarter past three in the afternoon and about twenty-five minutes and it is not an exact figure and I have written it as one because it is a card and not a report." She put it on the bench, flat, the way Bevin Sarr puts a card down. "The ward's line clocks are on that branch. Three of them. On Saturday morning somebody is going to stand in three places and read three of your clocks and write down what they say. **I have ruled four lines and I have put a time on one of them and I do not know why I ruled four.**"

"Will you bring the card on Saturday?"

"**No.**" She said it the way Sabine Kaye said a sentence in November and he heard the shape of it and did not like it. "Because the card is mine and it lives in an apron and it has caught four costs and a copy of three of them is in a folder on a ledge in that room, and I never gave you the copy and you never asked me for it and it is in there. And if I bring the original on Saturday morning then for one morning the record of a hospital's Saturday is in a pumping ward, and I am nineteen and I am paid at the ward rate, and the next time a ward needs a card for a night somebody is going to ask where the other one is, **and it will be in a folder on a ledge, and a thing in a folder on a ledge is a thing a room took off a person who was not in the room.**"

"You worked that out in November."

"A woman of forty-four worked it out in November in front of me and I have been carrying it about for a month." She did not look up from the card. "**I am not being clever. There is one instrument in this city that has ever caught a cost and it is a piece of card in an apron, and it works, and it is worth nothing, and the reason it is worth nothing is that nobody has standing to ask me for it and I have not offered. I am not going to offer it to a man on a bench in a shop.**"

So he did not ask, and he did not take the card, and the copy stayed in the folder on the ledge, and the three copies stayed where they were, and the original went back into an apron on a bus to Green Span.

And she asked for one thing before she went, and she asked for it as a person asking a man for a thing, and it was not a favour and it was not a gift and it was not going to be thanked: **that the copy in the folder be taken out of the folder and put back in the ward's own folder with the ward's own things in it, and that it be done that week, and that nobody write a line about it anywhere.**

He did it on the Friday morning at about eleven, and the folder went back to the ward on the Friday afternoon on the same bus, and there is no line about it anywhere, and the only record of it is a note in a grey book in a shop on Verge Lane in his own hand with a date on it, and the note says *returned, asked for, not thanked, not thanked because she said do not write it down.*

**And the four lines.** She had ruled four and put a time in one of them and did not know why. He wrote about that in the grey book on the Thursday, and what he wrote was that **seventy columns is about two years of nights at four lines a week and she has never needed a heading, and a column is a thing you rule when you expect to fill it, and ruling a column for a thing that has not happened is the only instrument in this city that has ever been used before the event, and she used it on a Sunday in a hospital in Green Span with a pen, by herself, for nothing, and nobody asked her to and nobody is going to be thanked for it and it is the sixth or seventh time this year that somebody in this district has done something that is a notice.**

**The Thursday the eighth had three things in it and the last of them was not in the district.**

Sabine Kaye came for the second time in her life to a district she has never worked in, at about two in the afternoon, and she had walked eleven miles of main in November with a folding rule she had not used and she walked about four hundred yards of Sluice Row on the Thursday with a pencil behind her ear and the twelfth clock in the passage, and she found it herself, in about two minutes, and said the thing about the count that is the whole of the column.

"**A column of names is not a timetable, and a winder who is late is a clock that is wrong by exactly as long as they are late, and on the seventeenth there is no instrument anywhere in this city that will tell you that your clock is wrong, and the only person who can tell you is the person who wound it, and they cannot tell you unless you have asked them when they will be there.**"

"So the column wants times in it."

"The column wants eleven names and eleven times and the times are the hard part, and there is a door on this row that opens onto a man who is sixty-eight and who will wind a clock at half past six and be at his daughter's by ten, and **there is no way on this earth of knowing that except by asking him, and asking him is a knock.**" She took the pencil from behind her ear and looked at the wall. "So the asking and the filling are the same door. They have been the same door since the fourteenth of November. **There was never a question of whether they were the same act, Mr Vale. There is one door and you either go through it on the tenth or you do not go through it at all, and if you go through it on the tenth then you have the names and the times both, and if you do not then you have neither and you have a column.**"

"Hanna Stroud said in that room that it was the same act and would not choose which of two was worse."

"Then she has got her answer and it was given to her in November by a man who would not go, and she knew it and I knew it and the two of us knew it and neither of us said it because it is easier to have a question." She put the pencil back. "**And a question is a thing a room can be given. A door is a thing a street can be given, and there are only the two of those in this district and you have had both of them this year and you have not once put them in the same column.**"

She left at about four and she did not sign anything on the way out and there was nothing for her to sign, and the second of the two operators had been twice and would not sign either time.

And at about ten minutes to six on the Thursday evening, at a gate on a works' boundary where a clock-driven valve the size of a small table has been opening itself on a schedule since the Friday the seventh of the October, a man of forty-one came off shift at about ten minutes to it and was standing in his coat with a sheet of paper in the inside pocket, and had been for about three months, once a day, every afternoon at about four, because a man can wash his hands.

**Corvin Tole is forty-one and has been at that gate nineteen years and has never been thanked and has never asked and does not think about it, and there is no form in this city on which he may be asked why he did a thing for five weeks, and there is no form in this city on which a man may be asked for a piece of paper he is carrying in his coat.**

The sheet is the ward's reserve. It is the tank at the pumping ward, and it has been at about fourteen hours and exactly balanced since the second of the third month, and the clock-drive has been putting in what the front and the hospital take out and putting in nothing else, and there has been no margin in it since March, and the low-level alarm has been inhibited since the second of the third month and no report of it has been made and **no form in this city requires one.** On the sheet, in pencil, in a hand of forty-one, is one number and the hour it was read and nothing else. It is the only record of the level of that tank in existence. It is in a coat.

"I did not come here to ask you for it."

"I know you didn't. **I would have brought it out if you had, and I would have brought it out in about four seconds, and that is the thing I have been thinking about for a month and I have not said it to anybody.**" He did not take his hand out of his pocket. "If you had asked me for it I would have handed you a sheet of paper with a number on it, and there is nothing in this city that could have been done with it. **You could have chalked it on a wall. And a wall in a pumping ward is where I read your six figures from, and a seventh figure on that wall with a date on it and no name on it would be a thing about a tank, and nobody in this district can be asked whether a tank is low because a tank is not a district and a tank has no standing and a tank is a thing the works make up out of the canal and put in the ward's reserve when the branch runs hot.**"

"So it is in your coat."

"It is in my coat and it has been in my coat every afternoon since the second of the ninth and there are about fourteen weeks of it and it is in a coat and not in a book, and a book in a room is worth something in that room, and a piece of paper in a coat is worth nothing at all, and I have known that for nineteen years and I could never have said why, and now I can, and the reason is that **I am not in the room.**"

He took the hand out of the pocket and did not take the paper out of the pocket.

"There is a thing on Saturday morning at seven o'clock. I know there is because it is on a board four miles away and everybody in this works has read it and one of them has been in a bad temper about it since Monday, and I open that gate at half past six and the cistern under that row is off the canal and it is the only water in this district that is not off the branch. **If a cistern is drawn down at seven in the morning then the branch runs a different way for five hours and I would like to be standing in a room on Saturday morning with a piece of paper that says what the tank did, and I would like there to be about nine people in the room.**"

"There is no form on which you may attend."

"**There is no form on which I may attend and I am going to be in the room.** And I am not going to give you the sheet and you are not going to have it, and here is the arrangement and it is the only arrangement anybody is going to be able to make in this district for about four years. **I will put it on the table at six in the morning and I will take it off the table at eight, and if anybody wants to know what it says they can be in the room and look at it, and nobody will have had it and nobody will have a copy and it will be a thing that happened in a room, which is the only kind of record this city has ever had.**"

He went back in. It was ten minutes to six and the gate opens at half past six and it was his shift at seven.

Nobody thanked him. He has been thanked twice in nineteen years and both times it was for a thing a foreman had done.
