# Premise

## One-sentence premise

When a city clockmaker discovers that Morrowfen's public cathedral is quietly spending the lives of its citizens to calculate the consequences of civic decisions, he must learn to share authority over the future before the machine chooses who the city is allowed to become.

## Working logline

A municipal clockmaker can hear the likely future strain in a living machine, but the only machine in his city that answers concrete moral choices—open this gate, close this ward, delay this bridge—does so by moving the hidden cost of every answer onto someone who cannot afford to pay it.

## Genre and tone

- **Genre:** scientific fantasy / political mystery / workplace procedural / slow-burn relationship.
- **Tone:** rain-lit, tactile, morally unsettled, and grounded in repair work rather than grand spectacle.
- **Primary point of view:** Orrin Vale, a male municipal clockmaker.
- **Scale:** a modern estuary city, then its neighboring towns and the institutions that coordinate them.
- **Length target:** approximately 700 chapters across 14 volumes.
- **Romance:** one slow-burn relationship, Mara Venn; no harem and no instant romantic resolution.

## The central promise

The Cathedral of Consequence is not a prophecy machine. It is a civic instrument that can compare a proposed action with the physical and social systems around it, showing several likely consequences and charging the city for the calculation. Its founders intended it to make the cost of public decisions visible. Over generations, the people who control it have learned to hide the people and places left out of the calculation. The drama is not whether a perfect answer exists. It is who is allowed to ask, who is counted, who pays, and whether a city can still act after it admits that certainty is unavailable.

## Opening situation

The inciting event is fixed in shape and must land in **Chapters 1 to 5**, with the death by the end of Chapter 5 and the first personal cost in Chapter 6 or 7. The volume's larger reversal comes later and is a different event.

Orrin Vale repairs civic clocks for a living. Over the course of three chapters he hears a ninth tick inside the Cathedral's public clock, just before a water-pump failure in the Lower Weirs. He files a question through the Question Hall: whether opening **Weir Two** now will save **Ferrywright Bridge** from the rising head. The machine returns a Reading that is technically correct. The gate opens. The bridge stands.

The Reading's blind margin reads *unregistered night work is not represented.* The night pump crew below Weir Two — five people, led by a tuner named **Perry Vance, 41**, who had no license and no record and whose daughter takes the tram at six each morning — is not in the model at all. The public clock loses three minutes. By nightfall Vance is dead of a preventable accident, and the Directorate calls the reading a success. It is not a conspiracy. It is gap four of the Hour Compact doing exactly what it was left free to do.

Orrin wants to prove that the answer was not luck and that the missing shift is a real person rather than an acceptable abstraction. He begins with a small repair ledger he has kept for years, a damaged regulator from the Long Nine that Gerren Hask brought to his bench to be quietly disposed of, and an old rumor about his father's name. He quickly learns that a correct forecast can still be a political act.

Three facts about the opening, fixed:

- **Perry Vance is not Teo Arden.** Teo is 20, alive, and elsewhere in the district that night. He is not the victim, and the story must not let a reader briefly believe otherwise. Vance's death is what Teo joins the Loose Minutes over; Orrin and Teo meet for the first time over a body, not a machine.
- **Hask gives Orrin the regulator.** It is a live maintenance item from the Long Nine, and a certified shop cannot legally hold it. Hask's motive is Volume 01's central cover-up, and the reader should be able to see it in the first three chapters without being told.
- **The three minutes are tracked.** Three minutes is the first entry in the toll ledger Orrin starts keeping, and the figure returns, in different units, at the end of Volume 01 and again in Volume 09.

## Ordinary life before the supernatural pressure

- Orrin earns a modest wage by resetting work clocks, hospital timers, water gates, school bells, and market scales.
- Residents judge a neighborhood by whether its clock keeps time, not only by whether its streets have electricity.
- Shift workers trade “saved minutes” in small brass tokens while employers call the deductions rounding errors.
- Public questions are technically open to everyone, but the fee for a Reading falls hardest on districts with the fewest spare clock reserves.
- The Cathedral is treated as civic infrastructure, a religious building, a tourist attraction, and a political weapon, depending on who is speaking.

## Narrative engine

Each major arc moves through a full cycle:

1. A concrete civic problem affects a named neighborhood or profession.
2. A Reading offers useful but incomplete consequences.
3. The protagonist investigates the omitted people, hidden labor, or false assumption behind the result.
4. A local faction uses the answer for its own advantage.
5. Orrin gains a larger capability, but the capability changes a relationship and imposes a physical or civic cost.
6. The immediate problem is solved or contained in a way that creates a larger accountability question.

The story should alternate repair scenes, investigations, political pressure, training, relationship work, and recovery. A fight is never only a fight: the practical consequence is what the system does afterward, and the emotional consequence is who Orrin must ask to trust him.

## Thematic question

When every answer carries a hidden cost, is freedom the right to choose badly, or is freedom the right to know enough to share the cost of a choice? Orrin's answer cannot be a single policy. It must be a practice.

## What this story is not

- It is not a prophecy story: no one receives a guaranteed future.
- It is not a game-world story: the city existed before Orrin and continues to exist when he leaves.
- It is not a story in which a higher power rewards the protagonist for efficiency.
- It is not a mystery solved by finding one evil person; the central danger is a system that becomes dangerous because many people find individual uses for it.
- It does not use a numeric leveling screen, skill tree, inventory, or quest log. A Reading is a sparse physical notice in plain language.

## Canon guardrails

The single canonical list of series rules is `outline/series.md`, “Series-level continuity rules.” The items below restate the ones most likely to be broken by accident.

- A Reading can describe likely consequences only when the relevant people and machines are physically connected to, or honestly represented in, the network.
- Hearing or seeing a Reading never guarantees that people will act as modeled.
- The Cathedral cannot be safely used by one person. The original safeguards require independent witnesses and a public record.
- Every Reading has a real toll. The cost may be delayed clocks, lost labor, fatigue, damaged machinery, or a missing interval of lived time.
- **A private sense is not a Reading.** Orrin's ability can never be cited, filed, or defended, and it is blind to whatever the network has routed away. It is a lead, not an answer, and using it alone is his flaw expressed as a technique.
- The final antagonist is the First Hour, the cathedral's original self-preservation rule, already present in the founding rules. No new cosmic enemy is introduced late in the series.
- The ending must remove the cathedral's monopoly on binding predictions while preserving some useful, limited ability to inspect machines and coordinate civic work.

## Series destination

By the end of the series, Orrin stops trying to possess the right answer. He helps build a public network of independent clocks and accountable Reading rooms, and accepts a future that cannot be perfectly forecast. The city becomes less certain and more capable of acting. The victory is measured in who can ask a question, who can refuse an answer, and who is present when the cost is recorded.
