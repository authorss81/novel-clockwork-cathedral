# Chapter 34 — A Defence of Jobs

The Water Users' Committee of the Lower Weirs pumping district heard the pumping warden at ten on the Monday morning of the tenth of the fourth month, in the back room over the chandler's shop, and the hearing took fifty minutes, and there was no bar, because there is no such thing at a water users' committee, and there was no clerk's table either, and the nine of them sat on their own chairs against their own wall with their own hats on their knees, and the fire smoked, and a woman of forty-four stood in front of them with three sheets of paper and asked to be allowed to speak.

She had set her own terms before she was asked for them, which is not a thing anybody in that room had ever had happen.

"There is a document against me, which is nine pages of yours with a page of the district works' on the end, and I have read it, and I have brought the two things I am relying on and I have not brought anything else, and if the committee wishes to put a question to me I will answer it, and if a question is not about water I shall say so and I shall answer it anyway, because I would rather answer it badly than not at all."

The chairman — a chandler with a shop on Ferrywright Row and not the owner of this room, who had been chairman for six years and had chaired two hundred meetings and had never once had anybody stand up in front of him and set terms — took his hat off his knee and put it on the table.

"Go ahead, Mrs Kade."

---

The first thing she put on the table was a certificate of the twenty-eighth of the second month, one page, the works' own letterhead, and the works' own signature, and it said two things and said them in eleven lines.

That the Carrow Street main was not to be shut. That the works employed ninety-one persons, of whom sixty-one were resident in the pumping district.

The second thing she put on the table was a single sheet of ruled paper with a curve on it in ink and a scale down the left-hand edge, and it was the water board's own gauge sheet for the canal front, taken weekly for two years, and the curve on it had a hump in it in the second week of March that came four inches above the line marked *second step* and stayed there for nine days.

"That is the whole of what I have," said Sel Kade. "That is the whole of the documentary basis on which a pumping warden of this district moved three hundred and twenty-four thousand gallons of water out of a ward's reserve in March. A certificate from a manufacturer saying his main was not to be shut, and a gauge sheet saying the street it is in was under water to the second step. There is no form. There is no report. There is no letter. I did not write to anybody and nobody wrote to me, and I did not put it in the ward's book because if I put it in the ward's book it is a thing the district works will see at the quarter, and I did not want it to be a thing the district works would see at the quarter, and I have thought about that sentence every day since the seventh of this month and I am not going to unsay it in front of you."

"You mean that as a criticism of yourself," said a member near the end of the table, who owned four houses on the front and had been in the room for all fifty minutes and had not spoken until then.

"I mean it as the reason there is a page of paper about me in a file, and I would take it back if I could, and I cannot."

---

They had the diversion note in front of them and it was a good document and it made her look bad, and she did not ask anybody to put it down, and she worked with it, and it took her a while to work with it and Orrin Vale could hear the room go quiet in the passage and then come back up.

"You have before you a note that says the ward's reserve was drawn in accordance with the standing rotation and no variation was made, and a ward return that says a variation was made from the second inst. and continued, and a note that says the reserve is under two days and the alarm has been inhibited since the second of March." She turned round to face the wall behind her for a second, which was a thing she did before she said the thing she had prepared. "That is correct in every particular. I have checked the figure. I am not going to dispute the figure and I want it recorded that I did not, because the figure is the only part of that document that anybody will ever be able to check, and I would rather be checked on a number for the rest of my life than be let off on a word."

Somebody laughed, one man, briefly, at the end of the table, and it was not unkind and it was the sound of a room deciding that this was going to be all right.

"Mr Chairman, the question in front of you is not whether I moved the water. It is whether I had anything to stand on. And I am going to answer that in two parts, and the first part is the answer you expect and the second part is the one I would rather not give, and I am going to give it anyway, and if the second part is the whole of the defence then I would rather it was the whole of the defence than a good first part with a soft second part."

---

The first part took eleven minutes and it was the defence of jobs and it was not a defence of herself, and it was the best thing said in that room in a year.

She did not say the works was a good employer. She said the works had a certificate and a number on it and that the number was ninety-one, and that a street that has ninety-one jobs on it is a street where a man who loses a place at forty can go and tell his landlord he has somewhere to be on Monday, and that in the month since March the Carrow Street end had had no standing water at all, and that the district's gauge sheet was the evidence for that and it was a gauge sheet and it was the only one anybody had.

"The ward was at eleven days of reserve on the first of the third month and under two on the seventh of this month. That is not an argument against the diversion. It is an argument for it having been done in March and not in this month, because in this month I would not have had it." She said it without any weight, the way a person reads a gauge. "I have been twenty-two years in this job and I have spent a good deal of that time working out that I have almost no money and a great deal of authority, and the only thing I can do with the authority is move water, and the only reason to move water is that somebody needs it, and in March the somebody was a street with a works in it, and I did it, and I would do it again, and I am not going to be inspected by nine men about whether I would do it again."

That went into the minute. Later.

The second part took about four minutes and it was the part she did not want to give.

"I did not divert the ward's reserve to a works."

The room did not move.

"I diverted it to a flooding street. The Carrow Street end went under to the second step of about forty houses in the second week of March and the works' main is at the low end of it, and the works' certificate says not to shut the main, and the certificate is true, and the main is at the low end of a street that floods because of a gate, and the gate is the ward's business, and the gate is this ward's business, and I am this ward."

She stopped and let it stand, and it was the only time in fifty minutes that she did not appear to be in a hurry.

"So the works is the reason the street needed water, and the street needed water because the gate did not hold, and the gate is mine, and if this committee wants a finding about the works it can have one and the finding is true. If this committee wants a finding about why the gate did not hold in the second week of March, then that finding is a matter of the district's flood performance in a wet fortnight eighteen years after a night in which nine minutes of this city's time stopped, and I will give it to you in whatever words you like, and I have the gate's own log, and it is a log, and it is the same kind of object as a gauge sheet, and it is in my office, and it is not in this room because I did not bring it and I was not asked for it."

The chairman put his pen down.

"Would you bring the log, Mrs Kade?"

"I would bring it if the committee asked for it in writing, and I would bring it in whatever form the committee's minute takes, and I would want it entered as a document the committee has asked for, and not as a thing a warden produced under pressure in a room, and I would want the request to say which fortnight." She looked at him. "And I understand that you cannot ask, because a water users' committee has no function in respect of the district's flood performance, and that is not a thing I am saying to be difficult. That is the whole of the reason I came this morning."

---

Bessie Crane asked the question nobody on the table was going to ask, and she asked it standing up, and she asked it because she was the only person in the room who had spent the morning in a different argument.

"Madame. I have thirty-one women on a Friday at a laundry on Bellweather Road and one of them has been off eight weeks this year and I have paid her out of my own pocket and I have not told her that I have paid her, and I would have kept that works open with a bucket and a bar for as long as it took, and I would have kept it open longer than nine thousand gallons a day, and I want that said in a room with a minute in it, because in about four years somebody is going to write the works down as a thing a warden did for a factory, and it was not for the factory."

And Ivy Pike, from the back of the room, in a coat she had not taken off:

"Thirty-one against ninety-one. That is the arithmetic and I have the pay book for the ninety-one and Mrs Crane has not got a pay book for the thirty-one, and I am not being hard, I am being the only person here who has watched two works in one winter and one of them close for want of water, and thirty-one women in a laundry on a Friday is thirty-one women until the Friday after that, and a works is a pay book and a gate and a certificate, and you cannot put a value on a job that stops." She put her hat on the chair. "Both of those are true and I am not going to sit down, because if I sit down I shall have to choose and I have spent twenty years choosing."

The chairman looked at the two of them for a while and then said, "The committee notes that the pumping ward's position is that the works was not the object of the diversion," and wrote it down, and Bessie Crane said, "That is the first time anybody has written that down anywhere," and the chairman said, "It is in the minute, Mrs Crane, and it will be in the minute on Wednesday," and Bessie Crane said, "Then it is in a book, and I have been in this district thirty years and I have never once seen a thing I said go into a book that was not about money," and the chairman said, "It is about water, Mrs Crane, and I am not going to argue with you about which of those is worse."

---

Teo Arden came in at half past ten and sat down at the back and did not ask to speak, and got the last word anyway, and it was the best question in the room, and he was the only man in it who left glad.

"Whose water was it?"

"It was the ward's."

"Mine," said Teo Arden, "and I have been sitting here for twenty minutes being angry at you and I have not understood until this second that you did not take it out of us. You took it out of a ward that had nothing left because somebody had spent it, and you spent it, and you are the one standing in the room admitting it, and the district that spent it is not in the room and has not sent anybody and does not know you are here." He stood up. "I cannot be on your side about this. I am not going to say it in a nicer way. You made a decision with nine thousand gallons a day and seventy-one houses and a works on it, and the people who were not in those three were not in it, and I do not know a way of being on your side of that and I am not going to pretend to. But I want it in the book that I said it in a room where the warden gave the district's own men water and did not take anything from the district to do it, and I would like somebody to write that down as well, and if anybody writes it down, Mrs Crane, you will be the one who can spell it."

"I cannot spell maintenance-deferred," said Bessie Crane, "and I have told him so, and he has never once believed me."

---

At about a quarter to eleven the chairman asked the only question the committee had a function to ask, and it was the question about the clause, and she answered it in nineteen seconds.

"The rotation was varied twice. On the eighth of the third month, and on the thirtieth, when the schedule ran its term and the standing rotation resumed. Your note says the diverted volume shall be restored to the ward's reserve within one month of a variation, and that it is reviewed at the next monthly return and thereafter at the discretion of the district works. One month from the thirtieth is the thirtieth of this month. I will restore three hundred and twenty-four thousand gallons between the first and the twentieth of this month, out of the ordinary rotation, and it will be on the ward's return and on mine and on the water board's gauge, and I have told my own clerk, and the clerk has a date in a book, and I do not need this committee to order me to do a thing I have said in this room I am going to do."

"Will the committee note that?"

"Will you mind if I say something first." She had her hands at her sides. "I want it understood that I am saying it in a room and not to a person, and that a person is not present, and that there are ninety thousand people in this district and not one of them has been told that I am restoring the volume, and that I could stand here and promise whatever you like and this room would write it down and it would be the first promise anybody in this district has ever been given in a room, and it would not be worth the paper."

"Noted," said the chairman, and he wrote it down, and it went in the minute as *the pumping warden states that she is without authority to bind the district and that the committee is not an authority in the district.* "It goes in as she says it."

"Fine." Sel Kade nodded. "And I am aware of what I have just said, and I am aware that I have said it to make the promise worth more, and I would like somebody to write that down as well."

"That," said the chairman, "we can take in the minute as a matter of course."

---

Orrin Vale stood in the passage from a quarter past ten and read the minute being written, because a minute is read aloud as it is made if anybody asks, and a man standing in a passage is a member of the public, and nobody in that room had the standing to ask him to go away.

It is four lines and it took about ninety seconds and the clerk read it twice, the way the form requires.

> *That the Water Users' Committee of the Lower Weirs pumping district, having heard the pumping warden of the district, notes that the diversion of reserve from the ward's certified return was made on the second of the third month under the certificate of the works supplying the Carrow Street main, that the works employs ninety-one persons of whom sixty-one are resident in the district, and that the district is of the opinion that the object of the diversion was the canal front and not the works.*
> *That the pumping warden states that she will restore three hundred and twenty-four thousand gallons to the ward's reserve between the first and the twentieth of this month, and states that she has no authority to bind the district, and that the committee has no authority in the district.*
> *That the committee notes that the reserve alarm for the ward is inhibited and is entered against a reconciliation note of the district works accountancy, and that no report has been made.*
> *That the committee has no function in respect of the district's flood performance and has not asked for the gate log.*

Four lines. Nine words in the second one about a woman, and eleven words about ninety-one jobs, and a reservation alarm entered against a note, and the gate's own log, which had been in a room on the second of March and was in an office on the seventh of this month and would be in neither on the thirtieth.

The clause about review was in the works' file and not in the minute, because the minute was about what the committee had heard, and the clause was not something it had heard, it was something it had been shown, and there is a difference, and the difference is the whole of the book.

Sel Kade listened to the whole of it standing at the table with her hands behind her back and did not move while it was read.

Then she said, "There is one thing wrong with it."

"Go on, Mrs Kade."

"Two, and both of them are the same one." She did not go and get anything. "The first line says *the diversion of reserve from the ward's certified return*, and it was not from the certified return. The certified return is a document. The water came out of the ward's reserve and the return is the document that says what the ward's reserve did, and if you write it the way it is in that note then in ten years there will be a record in this district that says a warden moved water out of a piece of paper." She turned to the clerk. "And the second one is the twenty-first. The restoration is between the first and the twentieth of this month, and the return is the thirtieth, and if somebody reads that minute in five years they will read it as the district works having ordered the water back by the twentieth. It did not. I said it. I am the warden and I said it, and nobody in this room can order me, and you have written it in a way that lets the book pretend otherwise."

The clerk looked at the chairman and the chairman looked at the page for about ten seconds and then said, "That is right. Strike the words *from the ward's certified return* and read the second line as *to the twentieth of this month, the warden stating the same and no order having been made.*"

And it was done, in about forty seconds, and the clerk read the whole minute again, and the two phrases were not in it, and the thing that had been in it was in it, and Sel Kade said thank you to the clerk and not to the room, and said it quietly, and it was the only thing she asked for all morning, and she got it.

She went out first and she went out alone and she did not go down the slipway, she went along Weir Lane towards the pump house with her hands in her pockets at a speed that was not a walk and not a run, and Wenna Pruitt watched her go from the window of the back room and said nothing about it, and Teo Arden said, "She has just spent fifty minutes being examined by nine men and she got two sentences of a minute altered and one promise out of them, and it took her twenty-two years to learn that this is the trade."

"She got the promise," said Wenna Pruitt. "Nobody has ever got the promise before. In twenty years of this district, nobody has ever got a warden into a room and got a date out of her, and she got the date and she got it in front of nine men and a factory pay book and a gauge sheet, and on Wednesday it is all going into a book with a date on it, which in this district is the only kind of immortality anybody has ever got."

"She also said the thing in the second line about having no authority, and she said it to make the promise worth more, and she told them she had said it to make the promise worth more, and both of those are true, and I have not decided yet whether I would have had the nerve."

"You would not," said Teo Arden. "That is not a criticism. That is the only thing in that room today I would put in a book, and I am not putting it in one."
