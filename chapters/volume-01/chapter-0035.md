# Chapter 35 — What the Loose Minutes Take Back

The bracket came off the riser on Ferrywright Row at about eleven on the Tuesday night, and the riser was the block's standpipe, and the bracket was the top one, and there was only one in the district that fitted.

That last part is the part that matters and it was not known to anybody who took it. The works' store on the canal road holds about nine hundred pounds of spares for the district's water fittings, most of which are the current pattern, and about forty pounds of the pattern they stopped making in the second year of the war, kept because a building somewhere in four districts is still standing with it on. Ferrywright Row 12 to 20 is a block of twenty-two households built in 1911 with a 1904 riser, and the 1904 riser came with 1904 brackets, and the 1904 brackets are not the current pattern, and there were three of them in the store and there are two.

The man who signed them out of the store on the Thursday afternoon was asked for it in the ordinary way, by name, with a line in the issue book, because a store that is not asked for a name is a store that cannot answer a question, and that had been the argument in a room over a chandler's shop for a month, and the argument was right, and nobody had worked out what it would be worth when the person signing was a man who did not have the right to sign.

Teo Arden signed for two gate wheels, a pinion, a drum, and one 1904 standpipe bracket, in his own name, at about twenty past two on the Thursday of the thirteenth of the fourth month, and he put his address down as a laundry on Bellweather Road, which is not his, and he did that for two reasons and only one of them was the law.

---

The stair light on the Kestrel side went on at about twenty past two in the morning on the Friday, and it was the only thing Teo Arden had wanted in a month, and it worked.

He had taken the collars off the buried line under the towpath on the Wednesday night at two o'clock in the morning — two cast-iron sections, about fourteen inches across, heavy as a chest each, which a man who does not know what they are will call a collar and a man who does will call a section cap, and there is no collar on an hourline and there is no form for either — and he had cut them with a cold chisel and a hacksaw on a stone settee and got them up through a grating with a rope and a man on the other end, and the two of them had been on the towpath at half past one in the morning for two hours and had not spoken.

He had meant the collars for the ninth series. The ninth series is a laundry on Bellweather Road, and it has thirty-one women on a Friday, and its line is on a dead branch and the branch has been dead since the spring, and a laundry with thirty-one women and a dead branch is thirty-one women finding out what time it is by asking each other. He had the caps and he had not the patience, and what he did with them instead was: a stair light on the Kestrel side, where nine women live on three floors above a landlady of sixty-five, one of whom is fifty-eight and a presser and cannot lift her arms above her head on a Tuesday.

The stair had been dark since the second month of the year. It was not a fault. The hall's own labour book has the Kestrel-side boardinghouse stair and landing light entered in the maintenance-deferred class, batch 22/1, one crew, three nights, and a maintenance-deferred item is not a fault and there is nothing for a maintainer to attend to, and a landlord's solicitor had put it in a letter to the landlady in the second month using the word *deferred* and had thought himself helpful.

Teo Arden had read that letter in a kitchen on Weir Lane in the third month and had decided, in the way of a man of twenty who has never once in his life been wrong about a machine, that he would put it right himself on a night, and had found out in the space of about four hours that putting a light right took him eleven days, two section caps he did not need, a set of gate gear out of a works store for a job he abandoned on the towpath at three in the morning, and one 1904 bracket off a standpipe riser in a block of twenty-two households.

He did the work well. That has to be said and it has to be said by somebody who knows, because everything else in this chapter depends on it.

He brought the power back on the dead branch at the boardinghouse without touching a single part of the district's supply, by running a short spur off the landing light's own transformer and putting the feed on the other side of the break, which is a thing that can be done and is not supposed to be done and which takes about four hours if you know which side of the break it is and eleven if you don't. He used the gate wheels and the pinion and the drum to make a bracket for a lamp that had none, out of a pattern he had drawn on the back of a works requisition in a hand that is not his own. He ground the 1904 bracket to the wall, tapped it, painted it with a tin of district grey, and put three screws into a brick that is a facing and not a wall, because there was nothing else in the front room of a boardinghouse at one o'clock in the morning to put them into.

It is sound work. It will hold. It is holding now.

---

The block on Ferrywright Row found out on the Friday morning and Hanna Stroud was there by eleven, because nine of her blocks are on that stretch and she keeps a Sunday and a Monday list and the ceiling of 4B was down on the Monday night.

She did what she has always done. She went to the ceiling herself with a step-ladder and a lamp before she sent for anybody, and she found the bracket missing and the pipe down about a quarter of an inch in the void and the fourth-floor landing wet across its full width, and she found that the block's standpipe was carrying nothing at the top, which on a wet night is not a fault and is not a delay, and she came down and telephoned the works before she telephoned the tenants.

That was on the Friday morning. Ambrose, at the counter on the canal road, had the store's issue book out in about nine minutes because the water in 4B had come down the outside of the riser and the outside of the riser is the store's wall, and a thing that comes down a store's wall gets looked at.

The bracket was out on the Thursday. The two gate wheels and the pinion and the drum were out on the Thursday. The name on all five lines was a name, and the address was a laundry on Bellweather Road, and the laundry's proprietor knew nothing about it and had a till full of a Wednesday's washing to prove it, and the man on the counter wrote the five line numbers in his day book with the date and the hour and the wall, and then he did the other thing, which was the thing that made him the man the works is, and will be on Monday.

He did not report it that morning. He went and looked at the ceiling first, and the light fixture on the fourth-floor landing, and the stair, and he put his thumb on the bracket that was holding the lamp nine hundred yards away — no, he did not. He went to Ferrywright Row and stood on the fourth-floor landing and looked at a wet ceiling and a bracket with three screws in a facing, and he wrote down what he could see and the time he saw it, and then he telephoned the works' inspectorate and said there was a 1904 bracket missing off a riser on that row and that the issue book had five lines on it, and he gave him the five line numbers, and he said, "They are in the book. I have not taken the book out of the drawer and I am not going to, and I would like it minuted that I have not."

He was right to do all of it. It is not an act of loyalty. It is what a man does who keeps a store book: he does not lose a book, and he does not pretend a line is not in it, and he does not go into a stair and take a thing off a wall. The five lines are in a book at the canal road and they are in a minute of an inspection somewhere by the end of the month and they will be read by somebody in a room in a year's time by a man with a form number, and that man will not be hostile. He will be correct.

---

Dilly Amnersley was sixty-six and she lived in 4B and she had wound the clocks on that floor every Monday morning for eleven years, and there were four of them.

There is a hall clock on the ground floor and it is on the district line and it has been out by eleven minutes and forty seconds since the middle of the third month and everybody on that row has learned to live with it. The four upstairs are not on any line. They were put in when the block was built in 1911, on a private spur off the town's own water, and the spur was cut off from the district supply in 1923 and the clocks were left where they were, and they have run ever since on their own weights on their own going barrels, and their rate is the rate they were built to, and the rate they were built to is the rate the district was keeping in 1911, which is not the rate the district keeps now, and which nobody has ever corrected, and which means the four clocks on the fourth floor of 12 to 20 Ferrywright Row are about four minutes out and are out in the same direction and have been for eleven years, and are the only clocks in that building that agree with each other.

Nobody winds them but her. She was given a key in 1911, or in 1961, and there is a record of the key, and the record says the key is not a service, and it has said that since before Hannah Stroud bought the block. The other three floors do not touch them. The man who lets the top room has never once been up the last flight with a lamp.

She had been doing it in the dark since the Friday, because the light on the fourth-floor landing had gone with the riser on the Friday night and she had not been told why, and the whole fourth floor had gone out with it, and she had been doing it with a candle her neighbour on 4C carried up for her on the Thursday and on Friday and had stopped carrying it up on the Sunday because it was the second night and she was not going to be carrying a candle up a wet stair every Monday for a woman who could buy a lamp.

And the four clocks had gone: a hundred and forty seconds a week and no wind and no rate, and on the fourth Monday of the month they would each be about two hours out, and by the fourth they would be doing what a falling weight does, which is to stop.

Orrin Vale went up at about eleven o'clock on the Saturday morning with a case and a tape and the second watch, and Dilly Amnersley let him in and told him the whole of it in about ninety seconds, and did not once ask him to do anything, and watched him do the entire floor in about nine minutes without being asked to hold anything.

He read the four clocks against his own watch and then against each other, and they agreed with each other to within a second and a half, which is remarkable, and all four were four minutes ten seconds out, which is not remarkable at all, and the going barrels were all four in the same state, which is what happens when one woman winds them the same way on the same morning for eleven years.

"There's nothing wrong with them," he said.

"No," said Dilly Amnersley. "There has never been anything wrong with them. I have been winding them since my husband died and he wound them before me and his mother wound them before him, and the only thing wrong with them in eleven years is me, and I have been off for four weeks."

"I know."

"You will not wind them."

"No."

"I thought you would not, and I want to say that I am not disappointed, because you are the fourth man who has come up here and I have told every one of the other three the same thing, and two of them tried anyway." She sat down on the upturned bucket in the roof cupboard, of which there were two, and had evidently been sitting on one of them for a decade. "You are the first one who told me why. Go on. Tell the woman who put the clocks in."

"The reason I will not is a rule and it is a small one and it is mine. I have corrected two clocks by hand in my life. Eleven minutes on a lane in the Weirs, with a bar, on a wet road. Eight on a bracket clock at the Ferrywright corner, in about eleven minutes. Two. Both in the hall's own work book now, under the eighth line of a form, because there is no column anywhere in this city for a hand correction, and it went in the eighth line by accident and I have never minded." He put the watch back in his pocket. "I can wind your four clocks at four o'clock this afternoon and they will be right for about a fortnight and it will take me about twenty minutes, and I will not be able to write it down, and it will be three."

"I do not see why three is worse than two."

"Because I would have to write it, and if I write it I will have to say when I started, and there is a woman in a public file on the Rise with a name beside a line with nothing in the column after it, and she is sixty-one and she went to a library on the afternoon of the eighth of the third month and cannot say how long she was gone, and I have had four weeks of being the man who writes the true thing, and I am not going to start it again at three." He put his hand on the case. "I can do it properly. Four clocks, rebushed and re-rated and certified, and then they will be on the district line like everything else, and then they will be eleven minutes and forty seconds out like everything else, and they will be out every week for as long as the line is wrong, and nobody will be able to tell me that it is not me."

Dilly Amnersley looked at him for a moment.

"You have got somebody else doing your job."

"I have. I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to be asked about it, and that is exactly right, because the moment a person is thanked for that it is a favour and the next one is a favour too and the third one is a debt." He closed the case. "I am going to write down the number of them, and the going barrels, and the fact that they are four minutes ten out and agree with each other, and it will go in my own book in a shop with a lease, and nobody will read it, and it is the only place in this city that those four clocks are written down at all, and I would do it again."

She got up off the bucket. "Then I will wind them on Monday, and I will do it with a lamp in my hand, and if the ceiling comes down on me I shall tell them the lamp was at fault."

---

Teo Arden was on the fourth-floor landing of 12 to 20 Ferrywright Row at half past two on the Friday afternoon, with a bucket under the pipe and a piece of sacking in the gap, and he told the room before anybody asked him.

There was no room. There was a landing with a wet ceiling and four people on it: Teo, Hanna Stroud, Dilly Amnersley, and Orrin Vale, who had come back at two because the man on the counter had telephoned the shop and told him the store had a name in its book and that the name was not anybody's friend.

"It was me. All of it. The collars off the towpath on Wednesday at two in the morning and I have not told you where they came from and I am telling you that too, so ask me. The five things out of the store on Thursday, including the bracket, including the gear I am not going to use because I have used it already and I am sorry about that, and I signed for all five in my own name and I put the laundry's address down because it is the only one in that book that is a building a person can be found at and I will not put a child's."

That last sentence landed on the landing and went nowhere, which is what the reader would expect and not what Orrin expected.

"You put a false address in a store's issue book," he said.

"I put the address of a building with thirty-one women in it and no connection with the man taking the goods, in an issue book that somebody in this city might one day have to look at, and if a clerk comes to that laundry they will be told there is no such man, and they will be right, and the line will have a name on it and a door that does not lead to the man." Teo had his cap in his hands. "The argument in that room over the shop is that a record is a thing with a person in it, and that is the argument I have been living on since the twenty-first of the third month, and I have signed my name on five lines in a store book in my own name knowing exactly what a name in a book is worth, and I did it in the afternoon and not at night, and I did it at the counter, and I would do it again, and if I am caught I would rather be caught than have it be a name that is not mine."

Hanna Stroud said, "You have made a mess of a stair I do not own and a riser I do not own and a woman's ceiling that I have had for four days, and I am going to be very clear with you, because I have been a landlord for nineteen years and I have had two things done to my blocks by people who were right, and both of them were me."

"Go on."

"One. The light on the Kestrel side is lit and nine women have it and you did that in a night for nothing and you did it well, and I have been trying to get that light on for two months with a letter and a solicitor and eleven shillings of a letter, and if you come to me on Monday I will give you a hundred pounds and a list of six buildings and I will not ask you for a receipt for any of it. That is what I think of you. I have thought of that since Friday morning and I told my solicitor on the Saturday and he wrote it down."

"And the other one."

"The other one is the top bracket off a standpipe and the pipe has come down through two ceilings and I have paid four hundred and eleven pounds for the plaster and I will pay again in the spring if it moves, and you did not know there was a fourth floor, and you did not know that bracket was the only one of its kind in four districts, and you did not know that riser was a fire system, and I am not going to let anybody in this building stand there and tell you that you knew, because you did not know, and because a man who knew would have taken the bracket out of a yard and not out of a wall that somebody's water is in."

She had not raised her voice once.

"Two," said Dilly Amnersley. "You put my three clocks out."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Then you are a young man who has put out four clocks that were right on a floor where a woman of sixty-six has carried four up a stair with a candle, and I do not know a single thing about you, and I am going to tell you the thing that matters and then I am going to go and do my Monday, and it is this: you have made a light right, and I have not got a light on my floor, and there is no light on my floor because the same bracket would have gone, and you took the one that was on my riser." She was not angry. "I am not saying you took it off me on purpose. I am saying that is what happened, and that there is one of them in this city and you had need of it, and I have need of it, and the one I have need of was on my own wall."

Nobody said anything for a while and the ceiling dripped into the bucket.

"The hardware is right," Teo said at last. "I want it said in this room, by me, before anybody writes it down anywhere, because I have been in four rooms this month where the hardware was the whole argument and it was not. I took two section caps off a buried line and a set of gate wheels and a drum out of a store, and I did not know what the caps are and I still do not, and I want to be able to say that in twenty years' time without having to remember this afternoon. The light is on. Nine women can see. One of them is fifty-eight and cannot lift her arms over her head on a Tuesday, and there is a stair in Northglass with nine women on it and a landlord of sixty-five who has a fair copy in a drawer that says in the most excellent hand in the city that they have never worked. That is the whole of what I did and it was the right thing to do and I am not going to stand on a wet landing and be told it was the wrong thing."

"And you are wrong about the block," said Orrin Vale.

"I know I am wrong about the block. I have known it since Friday at eleven o'clock this morning and I have been sitting on this landing for three hours working out whether to come up here and say it, and the answer is that I would rather say it on the landing than be asked about it in a room in six months' time by a man with a form number, because a man with a form number will get it out of the book and it will be in the book, and my being first will not be in the book."

---

The report on the Kestrel-side light is in a landlord's letterhead folder and not in any hall, and it is four lines, and Orrin wrote it on the Monday at the bench and took it round himself because it cost nothing and because the alternative was a shilling to a copyist and there is no copyist in that room.

> *Stair and landing light, Kestrel side, Northglass. Fed from a spur taken off the transformer side of the existing branch. Bracket fabricated on site from gate wheels and a drum, unpainted at fitting, subsequently painted. Three fixings into a brick facing and not a structural wall. Light declared and tested. Sound. I estimate two winters, and I would not promise a third, and the estimate is a matter of my opinion and not of a certificate, and there is no certificate in this folder because the work was not done by a licensed man and I am not going to put a licensed man's name under it.*
>
> *The light is lawfully hers. The work was not lawfully done. I was asked by the person who did it not to name him and I have not, and he was entitled to refuse, and I would have been within my rights to say his name and I did not exercise them, and I am putting that in the folder instead of in a book so that it is on paper somewhere.*

He read that back three times and left it as it was, and the address on the folder was Nell Arter's, and the stair had three women on the first landing and three on the second and two on the third and one of the third was Constance Kear, fifty-eight, who could not lift her arms above her head on a Tuesday, and who went down those stairs at ten past six on the Monday morning of the seventeenth with a hand on the rail and no lamp in her hand, for the first time since the second month of the year.

And on Ferrywright Row, Dilly Amnersley carried a lamp up the last flight on the Monday morning of the seventeenth and wound four clocks, and upstairs from her the four of them were four minutes ten seconds out and all four agreed, and the only clock in that block that was on time was the one on the ground floor, and it was wrong by eleven minutes and forty seconds, and there is no form in Morrowfen that any of it goes into.
