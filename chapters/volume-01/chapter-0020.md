# Chapter 20 — What the Ledger Says About Him

He did four days of work in the second week of a new month and every one of the four days was ordinary, and the ordinariness turned out to be the hardest part of it, because there is nothing in an ordinary day for a man to hide behind.

On the Monday he was at the hospital at half past seven in the morning, on the public clock in the porter's hall, because the third floor had started using it as a reference and the pharmacy wanted to know whether it was any good.

He put the gauge on the case and clamped it to the input wheel and read it four times, and it was four times correct to a tenth of a second, and it was the best movement in that district, and he had certified it himself four years ago, and the certificate was in a pigeonhole in a two-room office on the Rise in the hand of a woman who audited a stair.

He wrote the slip. And then he stood in the porter's hall for a moment with the slip in his hand and worked out the thing he had come in to check, which was that the ward's ward clock and the corridor clock were eleven minutes apart and one of them was right, and that the one in the porter's hall agreed with neither, because it is on the main and the other two are both off the sub-line and the sub-line has been correcting out of something for eleven years.

So there were three clocks in that building, and two of them were wrong, and the one that was right was the one nobody used.

He wrote on the reverse of the slip, in his own hand, because there is nowhere else to write a thing like that:

*Green Span hospital, porter's hall. Public clock correct to a tenth, four readings, the best movement in the district and nobody's reference. The two the ward actually uses are eleven minutes apart, and neither agrees with this one. I have certified the one that is right. I have certified the one that is right four times now and it has never once been the one that mattered.*

Halloway Street was next, and the pillar clock there was perfect.

He had not touched it since the Friday in the middle of last month, when a woman from an office with four staff and a written right to be ignored had stood at the foot of it with a piece of chalk and said *this may be a hand correction*. He had gone back and looked at it four times since. It was a pillar clock, cast iron, painted municipal green, and when he had first put a bar to it it had said twelve minutes past ten when the sun was on the horizon in the other direction, and been two minutes slow — five slow before the works came, and they had wound three of the five in a hired cart because the file said three, and so the other two were still in it, and he had wound those himself on a Sunday night a week later, and the extra two had been his own decision and was entered nowhere.

It was right. He checked it with a watch and with the sun and with the tram wires going over it, and it was right, and it was going to stay right, and the two minutes that had come out of the same reserve as the three had gone into a clockmaker with a bar on a wet street in the Clockwork Quarter on a Sunday night, and had never been the property of anybody, and had never been entered in anything.

He stood in the road with his case open and the sun on the back of his neck and made himself do the whole sum again, because it was the sum he had been carrying since the twenty-fourth and he had not made it properly in nineteen days.

*Three minutes. Entered on the Toll Stair in chalk at half past five every morning, in a district's own hand, with the district named. Read by whoever wants to stop on the stairs. Not copied into the Assembly's record in forty-three years, because the Assembly's record is a binder and the binder says CHALK.*

*Five minutes. Found on a street clock by an investigator with a rule about hand corrections, and paid by nobody.*

*Two minutes. Accounted for in no document, no receipt, no entry, no hand's writing, and not knowable from any paper in this city. Taken off the same reserve as the three, in the same minute, on the same afternoon, because a district that gives up three minutes to the machine has three minutes less to give to a man with a bar.*

*Eleven minutes on a lane in the Weirs, corrected by hand with a bar, in about an hour, on a street, with nobody watching and no form on which to enter it. Eight on a bracket at the Ferrywright corner, corrected by hand, in about eleven minutes, same. Two corrections, both of them mine, and neither of them charged to anybody, and a district that has two of them cannot produce a single sheet of paper saying so, because a hand correction is not a fault and there is no column and there has never been a column.*

He wrote it all out in the book and read it back, and there were two hand corrections that he had made himself and that nobody had charged and nobody could prove, and the column is the entire difference between what happened and what the city knows.

Then he shut the book and went to Northglass, where a man had been waiting a week for a regulator to be looked at since before any of this had happened, and the regulator was sound, and he said so, and charged three shillings, and was paid in coin on the counter, and it was the first money that had come into that shop in a month that had not been frightened into anybody's hand.

---

Leda Vale came into the shop on the Tuesday at half past two in the afternoon, which she had never done.

She was off nights. She had two days and she was in a good coat and she stood just inside the door for a moment and looked round the two benches and the vice and the wall of lathes and the sign, and said, "It's smaller than the front of it looks."

"That's the trick of it."

"Right." She took her coat off and hung it on the end of the bench, which nobody does. "I've come to see the room. Not for any reason. I've been on nights since August and I have been eating standing up in that kitchen since August and I got to thinking about it and I thought, I have never once been in the place where my brother keeps the thing that is going to get him killed, or a job, or a wife, or a small boy, or whatever it turns out to be. So I came to look at it."

"It's a school exercise book," said Orrin. "Grey cover. Nine coppers."

"Right." She did not sit down. "Show me."

---

He got it down from the shelf behind the bench, where it had lived for six years in the dark behind the brass weight, and he did not open it, and she took it out of his hands and opened it, and stood at the bench with it in both hands, and read.

She read for a long time. There was not much in it. It is a small book, and it is six years old, and Orrin Vale is not a man who writes at length.

The first four years are all machines. Dates, machines, faults, parts, and what they actually did, in the order they did it, with no conclusions, two thousand-odd pages of a man being extremely good at a trade, and then a fifth year of it, and then, in the sixth year, four hundred-odd pages that are not like the other five.

She turned the pages faster where it was ordinary and slower where it was not, and she got to the seventeenth of the month before, without saying anything, and then she turned back and read that again, and read the two accounts of the missing minute side by side, and the line in the middle of the page in the hand he did not like: *I do not know whether I read that stage or invented it. Everything after the ladder is reconstruction.*

And then she went on, and she read the twenty-first, and the twenty-fourth, and the Saturday, and the four districts, and the ninety seconds in a yard, and the eleven minutes in a corridor on the third floor of her own hospital, and the item about a widow's wall clock, and the item about the rope on the school bell.

And then she got to the end of the month and turned back and counted.

"A hundred and nine pages in four weeks," she said.

"A hundred and something."

"A hundred and nine, and I have read every one of them, so you will take it that I know." She put the book down on the bench, open, and put her hand flat on the page, which was the gesture he made and she had never once made in her life. "Out of a hundred and nine pages, and I am not going to do all of them: three are about a clock with a bad click, one is about a bracket, one is about a regulator a man in Northglass has been waiting a week for, nine are about a pillar clock on Halloway Street and the two minutes nobody can account for, eleven are about a pump house and a board, six are about a dead pump in a yard, and thirty-eight of them are about other people's hours and not about machines at all, which is more than a third, and I do not think you have noticed that, and I do not think anybody else has either. The other forty are weather, and work, and the small stupid repairs, and the four hundred-odd pages of last year are a different matter and I have read those too."

He had not thought of it like that and neither had she, and they both stood and looked at the book for a moment.

"You have stopped writing about machines," said Leda Vale.

"Yes."

"When did that happen."

"About the eighteenth of last month," said Orrin, "at about a quarter to eight in the morning, at a bench, with a pencil, and I did not notice for a fortnight, and I would not have noticed at all if I had not been made to look at it by somebody who does not read for a living."

Leda laughed at that — one dry syllable, with no humour in it at all, the way a woman in a good coat had laughed once in a corridor a month ago with a number on her clipboard, and the sound went round the two benches and off the lathes.

"Right," she said. "Now I'm going to do the thing I have come to do, and I am going to do it without any dignity whatever, and I am warning you in advance so that you can put your face into your hands first if you want to." She turned the book round and put her finger on a page near the end. "There is one page in here that is a fault and it is not anybody else's."

He looked. It was the file-room page, the second of the month, the first thing he had written after the seventeenth, and he had written the item about the file on Cathedral Rise, and about batch 21/4, and about a woman's signature, and about not telling her.

"Read it out," said Leda.

He did not want to and he read it out.

> *File, Continuity Office, pumping district, 2nd month, day 2, read in a room with no fire in it. Batch 21/4 in the volume, forty-one entries, countersigned S. Kade, deputy pumping warden, eighteen years ago, aged twenty-six. She was not the warden of anything. Nobody had told her. I had it in my hands in a room with the investigator in it and I did not take it round to her, and I went out of the building and I did not tell her, and I told myself the reason on the stair and the reason on the stair was that she has a tenant review coming and a man who is not me would ask her about it in a room where she is not represented.*

> *That is not the reason. The reason is that it is a piece of paper with her name on it and it is the only one I have ever had, and I did not want to be the one who handed it to her.*

And underneath, when he got there, the second line he had added on the Monday, in a hand that had got smaller:

> *The rule I wrote at the top of this book is about using a result. It is not about keeping one. Those are the same fault and I have only noticed it now, and I noticed it because I read this back and my own handwriting is not a person who loves me and has not got anything invested in my standing.*

Leda closed the book.

"You are not going to tell her either, are you," she said. "You have still got it."

"I have got it. It's on the second page of the new writing, near the back of a book that is not in any office in this city and never has been."

"Yes," said Leda Vale. "That's what I came to find out. I didn't need to look at the whole book for that. I have been doing that since you were twenty-four and I have never once been kind about it and I am not going to start, and I would like it noted that I have not been kind about it because it works and because the alternative is worse, and not because I get anything out of it. There is nothing in it for me. That is the point. I am not invested in your standing, you are. If I were, I'd have stopped telling you in a corridor in August and started helping you write the certificates that make it true."

She picked up her coat off the bench.

"So write the third rule," she said. "And write it properly, in the same hand, at the top, and do not put it at the back where you will find it in a bad temper in six weeks."

---

He did not write it for five days, which was longer than he had expected, because the first version was wrong and the second version was worse, and it was only on the Sunday morning with the shop shut and the lathes cold that he found the one that would do.

The first one had been *tell them everything*. The second had been *keep nothing back from the people it is about*. Both of those were rules for a man who had one fact and one book, and both of them were wrong because both of them said nothing about what to do on a Tuesday night in a hospital corridor when there is a pump and a woman on nights and no room anywhere with a form in it.

He wrote:

*No result of mine goes out of this room without a person named who can refuse it.*

Then he sat and looked at it and found, as Leda had told him he would, that it was worse than the first one, and he could not for about an hour work out why, and then he did.

The first rule had only ever bound what he did with a result. He could keep a thing forever. He could hold a page of paper in a shop with a lease for the rest of his life and nobody could call him on it, and the keeping was free.

The third rule bound the room as well. It meant that if there was no person in the room who could refuse, then he was not permitted to speak. It meant that on a Tuesday night in a hospital with a pump going, and a sister on the ward who was not a party to anything, and an engineer who could not certify a toll, he would have had to say nothing at all — and he would have had to stand in that corridor with the whole of it in his hand and go home, and there would have been no certificate with a note on the back of it, and no four lines in a notebook in a woman's hand, and no copper wedge and no four minutes and no morning.

It was a rule that could stop him. It was the first rule he had ever written that had the power to make him do nothing, and a man who needs to be stopped is a man who will start looking for a reason the stopping is wrong.

He read it twice. He copied it out at the top of a clean page, because a rule that lives at the back of a book is a note.

Then he sat in the cold shop and did the sum, because that was the thing he did instead of feeling, and the sum was short and it was not a sum of minutes.

*Perry Vance went down a ladder at about ten on the seventeenth of last month, and it is thirty-two days ago, and nothing anybody has done since has cost anybody a minute in a place where anybody could read it.*

*Three minutes is on the Toll Stair. That is still the only number this city has published in a month, and it is the correct number, and it is the price of a bridge, and it is about a fifth of what Perry was paid for a night's work over twenty years, and no clerk has ever put those two figures on the same sheet of paper.*

*Eleven copies of the hall's labour book are in nine buildings. Thirty-one households have not had a rise they were told they were getting. A woman in my trade has lost nineteen years for copying a document nobody is allowed to stop being copied. A warden moved a reserve in March for seventy-one people and has not been reviewed by anybody and does not need to be until somebody has the time.*

*A ward lost four minutes in the fourth week of the month and it is written down in a ward book in a building, and the word on the cover of the ward book is not the word anybody would use.*

*A regulator in a cloth on a shelf behind me has been there for a month and eighteen years, in no book anywhere in this city, with a bar's jaw on it, and nobody in Morrowfen has asked me one question about it, and nobody will until somebody has a reason to want it not to be there.*

*And the Archive of Unlisted is exactly as shut as it was on the afternoon a man wrote two words in chalk on a valve spindle in daylight and told the truth about a thing the clock said was shut, and the door has not moved an inch, and there is a man on the ground floor of a building on Cathedral Rise with a patched cushion on his chair who would show it to anybody who asked him in the correct way, and there is nobody on this earth who can ask him in the correct way, because the correct way is a form, and the form is in a room that is not open to petition, and the man is the only person in Morrowfen who has ever been kind to me about it and he cannot help me and he says so first.*

He put the pencil down and found that his hand was quite steady, and that he had not written anything for six years that had cost him a person, and that the entire cost of a month of the best work of his life was four minutes on a ward, thirty-eight minutes out of a man, and one rule on a clean page, and a thing he had written down about a woman he had not told.

---

He did the school bell on the Monday, in the morning, after the rule was written and after she had gone, and it was the rope again, because the rope was nine years old and had been shortened twice and the striker was at the top of a boy's reach and the boy had got taller.

"You could get a man up there," said the boy, looking up.

"I could get a man up there for four shillings and he would not do it as well as I am going to do it, and you will not fall off, because I have done this about four hundred times and I have never once let anybody fall off, and I am going to let you hold the light this time because I want somebody to be holding the light who is not me."

He let the boy hold the light. When he came down, Orrin asked him whether it would break the bell, because he had been asked it once before and it had deserved the same answer.

"It'll break the rope," he said. "Not the bell. Bells are harder than rope. That's most things."

"That's what you said last time."

"It's what I said last time and it is still true," said Orrin, "and you are going to be about thirty before you find a case where it isn't, and when you do, write it down and tell me, because I shall want to know."

---

Teo Arden came in at four o'clock on the Monday afternoon without knocking, which he had done once before and would do for the next three months, and he did not come to the bench, he came to the front of it, and he had a folded sheet in his hand that was a copy, and he was not sorry about anything, and he was not happy either, and he looked about eleven years old and about forty.

"Two things," he said. "And the first one is not a favour and don't get used to it."

"Go on."

"Mrs Stroud's block got the rise put back." He put the sheet on the counter. "Not by her. She went to the insurer on the Friday with a copy of our minutes off the demand — the six returns, printed in full, with her own twelve years of rents in a column beside them, which is a thing she has had in a drawer since she started and has never once needed — and the insurer has put the block back on the present schedule and written her a letter about it, and the letter is in the same envelope as the premium. So the block gets the rise in January, and so do the other eight, and the letter is four lines long and it says the district returns are insufficient."

"And the second thing?"

"The second thing is a boardinghouse in Northglass." He unfolded the sheet. It was a page of the copy, the middle of it, and beside a line reading *Kestrel side, boardinghouse, stair and landing light, maintenance-deferred, batch 22/1, nine entries, six years* he had written, in a small hard hand, a woman's name and a street and a date of birth, and a second name below it.

"That's not in the book," said Orrin.

"No. That's from the woman, who is the landlady, and she came to me on the Thursday because she thought — " Teo stopped and started again, and for the first time since Orrin had known him he looked as though the sentence was going to come out wrong whichever way he said it. "Because she thought we might be able to help her get the hours back off the record. Not get it back off. Get it *into* the record. There's nine women on that stair, all of them have worked in that house since before any of us were born, and one of them is fifty-eight and she is going to lose her hours when she can't stand up, and the book says she is not a person who has ever worked there."

He pushed the sheet across the counter.

"That's the question," said Teo Arden. "Not for you. Not today. I don't know what it is yet and I'm not going to pretend I've got a plan, and I'm not asking you for anything, and if you want to say no you can say no and I won't come back." He looked at the grey ledger on the shelf behind Orrin's shoulder, visible in the gap, and then away from it. "But a name in a book in a room is a thing that happened to a person, and a person who can say no, and I have got no idea what to do with that, and you are the only man I know who has ever had one, and I've come to ask whether there's a way of it that isn't mine."

Orrin Vale stood at the counter of a two-bench shop in Verge Lane in the Clockwork Quarter with a rule he had written the day before that would have stopped him saying one word of this, and worked out, in the flat procedural way he worked out everything, that a rule only means anything if it is obeyed in the room where it costs you something.

"I don't know," he said. "But it's not going to be mine."

"Right," said Teo Arden. "Thursday, then. I'll have the other two. And I'll tell them it's yours, because it isn't, and because you were in the room when the minutes got printed and that's the only claim you've got."

He went out. Orrin stood at the counter and listened to him not say goodbye, and then he took the sheet and squared it, and put it on the bench, and took the grey book down, and turned to a clean page at the top, where a man keeps the things he is going to do, and wrote the date and the name of the woman on the Kestrel side of Northglass and the nine on the stair and the years, and did not put a rule at the top, because the rule was already there, and because on Thursday there would be a person in the room who could refuse him, and that was the whole of the difference between this book and a drawer, and it took a month and one sentence from a woman standing in a cold corridor at seven in the morning to learn.
