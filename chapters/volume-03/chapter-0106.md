# Chapter 106 — The Nurse's Kitchen

Monday the thirtieth of the first month was cold enough that the ink skinned in the well if he left the lid off, and Orrin worked with the stove lit and the door shut and the letter still face down on the shelf.

Nella Rook came at eleven. She was twenty-eight, a hospital clock nurse in Green Span and Leda's colleague, Imani Rook's niece by blood, which was canon and was said once in the ward and never used as a reason. A hospital clock nurse keeps medicine, staff and public clocks reconciled against each other; it is a nursing specialty and it exists because the hospitals discovered what a Reading costs.

She had a carpet bag and a token in a twist of paper and no appointment, and she put all three on the counter.

"Miss Rook," Orrin said.

"Mr Vale. Leda said you'd tell me true whether I'm buying hope or brass."

He unwrapped the twist. G, a hundred, year and die on the rim. Real to the eye, weight right in the hand, but the eye was not an assay and he said so.

"What do you want it for?"

"My ward," she said. "Medicine times. We run on the free master and write the line clock beside it, and the line is three hours out and stepping fifty seconds a night, and the ward's slack is forty minutes on the best night of the year. A hundred minutes is two and a half times the whole of the slack. Two tokens is five times. I want to buy a hundred minutes for the ward's bracket clock, in the kitchen, where the night staff can see it done and argue with it."

"Whose kitchen?"

She smiled for the first time. "Yours if you'll have us. No — ours. Fourth floor, 9 Bellweather? No. Green Span nurses' kitchen, off the ward, gas ring, table, list on a nail, the same size as every other kitchen in this city. Four of us, off shift, no matron, no Directorate, no guild. Leda will be there and disagree with me and neither of us will be asked to choose."

On Tuesday the thirty-first — no, the first month has thirty days, so Tuesday the thirty-first was Wednesday the first of the second month. Orrin corrected himself in the book and was glad the book made him. On Wednesday the first of the second month he went to Green Span at seven in the evening with his tool roll and the narrow book and no brass of his own.

The nurses' kitchen was nine by eleven like all the others, a gas ring, a table, a list on a nail for tea money. Four women off shift: Nella, Leda, a night sister of nineteen who was paid at the ward rate and had asked twice to be named, and a student. No Assembly man, no Directorate man, no guild man. The bracket clock from the ward was on the table, eleven inches high, its back off.

Nella put the token on the table. "Four shillings and sixpence," she said. "A hundred and twenty nights. My money, not the ward's. The ward has no fund. I am buying it the way Miriam Coss bought hers, and for the same reason: official tolls took time and no instrument will give it back lawfully."

Leda leaned against the sink. "And I disagree," she said, evenly. "Not with the arithmetic. With the spending. A token spent in a ward clock that is not the clock it was cut from takes the minutes out of the Quarter's margin and puts them into the network, and the network gives them to the next clock behind, which is not this ward but the Weirs branch that is hungrier. You will have bought four months for a clock and paid for them with somebody else's margin, and the somebody else has no name, which is the whole of the last two volumes."

"Then what?" Nella said. "Leave the medicine times to drift?"

"Say the number out loud in front of the people whose hours they are before you spend it," Leda said. "That is my rule and it has won an interval and no hours at all. Say that a hundred minutes is two and a half times forty, that two tokens cost nine shillings, that the ward has no fund, that the branch has been stepping two hundred and seventy-three nights and two tokens last two hundred and forty. Say it where families can read it. Then spend if you still want to, and I will wind with you."

The night sister of nineteen spoke up. "Will you put my name on the card if we do it? I have asked twice."

"We will," Nella said. "Anybody who wants her name off it can come and take it off and we will write down who and when. Nobody took it off last time."

Orrin checked the bracket clock's beat, cleaned the crutch, set it true, and refused to spend the token, for want of standing, in the same words he had used in Bellweather. He wrote the refusal in the grey ledger at the table, with the date, and both nurses watched him do it.

"I will not put it in," he said. "I have no licence. But I will write down that you asked, and that Leda disagreed, and that neither of you was asked to choose, and that no person was thanked in this kitchen."

Nella wrapped the token back in its twist. "Then I am still holding brass," she said. "And brass is honest. It weighs what it weighs. What it is for is the hole."

On Thursday the second of the second month Orrin wrote the kitchen at length in the shop, because a working woman buying a hundred minutes in a kitchen and she was a working woman and not a symbol, and the reason she gave was her ward and its medicine times.

*Green Span nurses' kitchen, 1st second, evening. N. Rook, 28, hospital clock nurse, buys G100 for ward, kitchen, four women, no officials. L. Vale disagrees, neither chooses. Refused to spend for want of standing. Token held, not spent. Nobody thanked.*

He put the pencil down. The letter was still on the shelf. He had not answered it, and the unanswered letter was now eleven days old, and the city was buying back its own time at four shillings and sixpence while he swept his step.
