# Chapter 93 — Twenty-Two Doorsteps

On Saturday the tenth of the twelfth, at about ten in the morning, in daylight, in a dry cold December with the sun on a brick wall off Weir Lane, four people from two streets walked down a row of twenty-two houses and told each household in it, in person, in the words of a notice, that the only safe route through this district's water runs through a brick cistern under their own row and costs three hundred minutes of their water; and the street said a great deal back; and the column on the wall in a pumping ward, which has a heading and no names in it, has twelve names and twelve clocks in it after that morning, in thirteen houses, because two of the thirteen are one clock in a court, and nine households on that row that have no clock in them at all, whose water goes off for five hours, are in no column of any kind and were not expected to be, and nobody thought of that until a woman of twenty-four said it on a step at number six.

---

**Friday the ninth, in the evening, in a room over a chandler's shop in Bellweather, four people and a sheet of foolscap and no meeting.**

There is a stove in that room and there is a chair and there is a bell that has not worked since 1949 and there is a shelf of minute books in which the most recent volume is 1948, and there is a clerk's pencil and no clerk, and nothing that has been decided in that room this year has been decided by anybody but a show of hands, and nothing that is decided in it binds anybody outside it.

There were four of them and they were not a committee and there was no chair, because **a chair is a thing you sit in once and then people begin to look at whoever is in it, and on Saturday morning at about ten o'clock on a street of twenty-two houses the man they have never met is going to have to be able to be looked at directly by everybody and answer for himself, and a chair in a room is how you stop that.**

"Read it again," said Wenna Pruitt, thirty-eight, who has been the tenant council's organiser for nine years and who did not take her coat off.

He read it. It is four lines long and it took him about a minute and a half, and it is in the words that a notice is in, because on the Friday night in a room over a shop four people worked out that **a notice in this city says four things and does not say a fifth, and a fifth thing on a doorstep is a thing a person is being asked instead of told, and nobody is going to be asked anything.**

> *1. What is proposed. On Saturday the seventeenth of the twelfth, at about seven in the morning until about noon, the cistern under this row is to be drawn down and refilled, and while it is down the clocks on this row which are on this cistern will not be driven, and the notice this was copied from says **eleven** and there are **twelve**, and the difference is the whole of what the fourth day is for, and the two standpipes on this row will be dry, and there will be no water in this row for about five hours.*
> *2. When it takes effect. Seven in the morning. In daylight. On a Saturday, so that anybody who is at home is at home.*
> *3. What it costs. Three hundred minutes off the clocks of the Lower Weirs pumping district, which is a district of a hundred and forty thousand people four streets from here, and not one of them lives on this row and not one of them has any of this row's water. **That district cannot be told. It is not able to be consulted and it is not able to object and there is no form in this city on which it could be asked anything.**
> *4. Who pays. Nobody knows yet and the sheet says so, and the sheet is the second of these four lines and the sheet is on a board in a pumping ward four miles away and on a board at a washing-yard and on a board at a works' gate, and a board is read by about eleven people a day, and the four of us are here because a board was not good enough and we are not good enough either and we are doing it anyway.*

"That is a notice," said Wenna Pruitt, "and it is in the notice's four words, and it is the first notice anybody in this district has ever read out loud on anybody's step, and I have been trying to make one of these for four months and I could not get it under nine pages, **and you have got it onto a half-sheet in four lines and the reason is that you are not trying to persuade anybody of anything, which is what I have been doing all year like an idiot.**"

"And the fifth thing?"

"The fifth thing is *do you want to wind a clock on Saturday morning at seven*, and **that is a question, and there is no form in this city on which a question may be put to anybody about their own house, and we are going to ask it anyway on a doorstep, one at a time, twenty-two times, and the reason we are going to ask it anyway is that the notice is four lines and the fifth line is the only part of this that is anybody's.**"

---

**And two of the four had voted against it in November, and one of them had not said why, and neither of them lives on that row.**

**Hanna Stroud is fifty-four and has nine blocks and about two hundred and eleven households and she lives in 4B at 12 to 20 Ferrywright Row, which is a mile from Sluice Row and is not off it, and she has a key ring with about two hundred and eleven keys on it and has never used it to ask anybody anything.** **Nell Sayer is twenty-four and is a wire-drawer at the galvanising and wire works and lives at number nine, Weir Lane, and has lived there six years, and Weir Lane is the street Sluice Row comes off and number nine is not on Sluice Row.**

"There is one thing to say on every step and it is going to be said on twenty-two steps and it is going to sound like a formality and it is not," said Hanna Stroud on the Friday night.

"Say it."

"**That none of the twenty-two households on this row is one of the twenty-six addresses on the list in a kitchen in Bellweather, and that I have checked the four lines and checked them against a thing I have not seen and did not ask to see, and that we are not going to tell you what is on that list and we are not going to anybody's door on it and no person in this room will ever do that, and I am telling you the list exists because you are about to be told a price by a stranger on a step and I would rather you had the whole of it from us than found out in a month that there was a part of it we kept.**"

"That is the sentence of the morning," said Wenna Pruitt. "And I have been trying to write it since the fourteenth of November and I could not get it under a page, and Hanna has got it in forty-four words at ten past eleven on a Friday night, **and the difference between us is that she has two hundred and eleven doors in her year and I have got a room.**"

---

**Saturday the tenth. Half past eight at the Bellweather Road stand, and the bus at ten past eight, and the walk from Weir Lane, and the four of them on the row at nine forty.**

It was a dry cold morning with about four days of frost gone and the sun on a brick wall. The row is about four hundred yards and it is not straight: it goes down about twenty feet, and there is a lamp on a bracket at the bottom, and the cistern's vault is a brick arch at the north end with an iron plate on it and a handwheel at street level, and the iron plate has not been lifted in anybody's memory.

Nell Sayer had said on a Friday night that she would not promise a number to a street, and a man of thirty-two had said three hours if nobody opens a door to anybody and two if they do, **and it was four hours and fifty minutes from the Bellweather Road stand at half past eight to the bottom of the row at twenty past one, which is two hours longer than the estimate two people made in a room with a bulb against the window, and the reason is that the second of every door takes about four minutes and there are twenty-two of them and nobody had counted the fours.** The longest part of it was the four doors where nobody was at home. The shortest part of it was a man at number eight who shut the door in about nine seconds.

Nobody was on that row who was not one of the four. There was no table, no chair, no paper except a sheet each and a pencil, no minute, no tally of who had said what, and **nobody wrote a single line down anywhere on that row about anything that was said to them, which is the first time in a year of this that a set of twenty-two conversations has left no record at all, and it is not an oversight and it is not a rule anybody made, and Wenna Pruitt said at the top of the row, before they started, that if anybody put anything in writing about what any household said to them she would take it off them in the street, and nobody argued with her and nobody has argued with her since.**

And at the first door, before he said the four lines, he said the fifth thing, and he said it at twenty-two doors and the words did not vary and Hanna Stroud had written them out in her own hand and gave the sheet round and he read it off it.

> *"Before I say anything else, three things. One: none of you is on a list of twenty-six addresses in this district and we are not going to tell you who is and we are not going to anybody's door about it and the twenty-six households are not going to be told anything. Two: I am not from this district and I do not live in it and I have never drawn a bucket on this row in my life. Three: there is nobody in this city I can be asked to give this to, and I am the man who cannot be refused, and I have come to your step on a Saturday morning because there is no other instrument, and I would rather be looked at than be sent a letter, and if you want me to go away I will go away and I will not come back and I will not write to you."*

Nell Sayer said, on nine of the steps and not on the other thirteen, the sentence she had been carrying since the third of the December: **"A number on a table is a thing a person can be angry at, and I have been the loudest person in about four rooms a year for six years and I know what I sound like from the other side of it, and I am not the loudest person on this row today, and the man is."**

---

**Number three. A woman of about sixty-two who has been in the house since 1938 and winds two clocks herself, and one of them is in a passage.**

"You've been up this street twice and you haven't knocked."

"Twice. In the middle of November and on Monday. And I did not knock either time and I want to say why before I say anything else, because it is the worst thing anybody has done in this district this year and I did it twice." He had it written out and it came out of him in the flat voice of a man reading a notice. "**Because there is a price on a wall four miles away and I did not want to come to your door with the price in my hand. And that is a real reason and it is the reason a man gives when he is ashamed of it, and the whole of a year of this is that asking is the only instrument there is and I had a number on a wall and I did not ask. I am asking now.**"

"You'd be the clockmaker."

"Twenty-two years and a bench on a lease to Michaelmas and no licence, and I may repair a clock and measure one and be paid for it, and I may not certify anything, and that is the whole of what I am."

Then he said the four lines, and she listened to all of them, and then she asked the only question anybody asked him all morning.

"**How long?**"

"Five hours. Seven until about noon. Three hundred minutes."

"Right." She did not say yes and she did not say no. "**Then I'll wind it. And the passage one's mine and nobody knows that, and it's a hundred and four year old face with the numbers off, and it's not on your list, and if you go up that passage in March the plaster'll come down on you — you go at the quarter hour when the light's on the wall.**"

"Nobody knocked at the second court in November and I have written that down twice."

"You counted eleven from the middle of that street. I watched you do it. You stood in the middle of the road and looked left and right like a man counting sheep." She did not smile. "**A street is not a survey and a pair of eyes is a street, and I have been at the bottom of that passage for forty-eight years and there is a clock on that wall the whole time, and nobody has ever been asked about it because nobody has ever been in the passage.**"

She gave a name for her own clock and she would not give a name for number seven's and she said why: *"Number seven is not here on a Saturday and you are going to go back to number seven, and I am not telling you a thing about number seven that number seven has not told you with his own mouth, because the first time anybody in this city says a name on a step it is going to be because he stood on it and said it, and I am not going to be the first."*

---

**Number eight. A man of about forty-four who shut the door in about nine seconds and then opened it again, and said no twice.**

He was a fitter. He had been in the district nineteen years and worked north of the canal and had a boy of five and a girl of eleven and a shift at six.

"No."

"May I say the four lines?"

"**No.**" He had his hand on the edge of the door and he did not take it off. "You are going to tell me a number and I am going to be angry at the number and then I am going to be angry at you for making me angry, and that is how a thing like this gets done to a man instead of by him. That's not me being clever. **I've been the man on the step in a gate in a yard. You want to know the number? Three hundred. And I don't care, and I'll tell you why I don't care, and then you can go.**

"My washing's on that standpipe. That's a Saturday job, that is. That's four trips with a basket and my mother-in-law's sheets in it and the whole street knows it's my job, and it's the one thing on a Saturday I've got that isn't the shop, and you're asking me to give it up for a cistern you've never seen for a reason you can't prove to me.

"And my boy goes to school at eight and I've never missed it."

And Orrin Vale said the only sentence he had for it, and he had it because a woman of twenty-four had said the other half of it to him six days earlier and he had not understood it until that step.

"**Your washing is not in the column. That is the thing I got wrong and you have found it in nine seconds and it took me a month.** Nine households on this row have no clock in them and their water is going off for five hours, and there is no column in this city for them and there is no form on which to make one, and if four people had told me that on Monday I would have found you in a fortnight with an answer. **You have just made the instrument, at a door, for nothing, and I cannot use it and I am not going to pretend you have not made it.**"

The man looked at him for about four seconds.

"Right," he said. "**Then ask me again on a Friday. Friday I wash on a Sunday. And if you knock on a Saturday again I will not open it and you will have wasted a morning of your own and that will be your own business.**"

The door went. Hanna Stroud wrote **8** on the back of her hand in grease pencil and did not write a name, and said, to nobody: *He is not on the column and he is the first one of the day who has understood what is being asked of him and he has said no, and I am not going to argue with a man on a step, and if anybody in this district argues with a man on a step then they have stopped asking and started collecting and I have watched a woman collect for eleven years and it is how you end up with a list of people instead of a street.*

---

**Number fourteen wanted to do all of it himself, and had to be told no, and took it well.**

He was about thirty, a printer's compositor, and he had the whole thing worked out by the time Orrin was halfway through the second line: eleven clocks, four hours, one man, a ladder.

"**I'll do the lot. You don't need eleven people, you need one person and a stepladder.**"

"You do. And I am not allowed to say yes, and the reason is not mine and I am going to give it to you exactly as I have had it given to me, and it is eighteen words long: **the person who can refuse the result has to be somebody who can refuse.**"

"I can refuse it."

"You can refuse it on Saturday morning. **On the seventeenth there is no form anywhere in this city on which anybody may be heard saying no.** The result of Saturday is three figures in three hands and nothing else — no seal, no form, no filing, nothing admissible — and that is the only reason it is safe to do at all, and it is safe because nobody can be made to act on it. And if one man winds eleven clocks then the figure on the wall is one man's figure, and the twelve households it was taken from have had nothing to do with it, and the whole thing becomes a thing a man did."

"Then what is the column for?"

"**The column is the whole of the instrument. There is no other part of it.** The rehearsal certifies nothing. The number came off a counter sheet. The only thing in this that belongs to anybody is a list of names, and if the list is one name long then the list is a signature, and a signature is what a hall has been telling us for eighteen years is worth what a man expects." He said it and then heard himself and stopped, because it was Pell's sentence in a December voice on a doorstep, and Pell had not been in the room.

The compositor thought about it for about a minute.

"Right," he said. "**Then I'll wind mine and I'll do it because I'm asked, and not one of my neighbours' clocks, and if you need somebody to carry a lamp for you I am that somebody and that is a different job and I would like it in writing that it is a different job.**"

There is a line in the grey book on the Saturday night that says *fourteen offered the whole of it and was refused and asked for the lesser thing in writing, and it was given to him in writing, and it is the only undertaking anybody on that row asked for.*

---

**Number nineteen's woman had four shillings in an envelope and could not give them away.**

"I don't know what to do with them. That's the honest position. I've four shillings and I've a gas bill and you'd be doing me a kindness and I can't think how."

"**Nobody can take them.** Not me, not her, not the council. There is no fund for this and no rate can be struck for it and no body in this district could be invoiced for it, because a body is a thing you can ask to pay and there is not one, and a rate is struck on a return and our return has said nothing about us since the storm month." He said it as it was. "It is not modesty. If I took four shillings off you I would have to enter it somewhere and there is nowhere, and a gift that cannot be entered is a thing I would be doing to you and not to you."

"Right." She put the envelope back in her apron and then, after a moment, put it on the sill inside the door, where it stayed until the Tuesday, when she gave it to a man buying a weekly for a woman in number six, which is her own business and is not in this.

"**You are the first person in about eleven years to tell me plainly that you can't take my money**," she said. "Don't come back on your own. If you come back you knock."

---

**And at number eleven a woman opened the door and said "Is this a committee thing?", and Hanna Stroud answered her and Nell Sayer did not.**

"Is this a committee thing? Because I've had these round twice this year and I'm not having them round a third."

"No," said Hanna Stroud. "**There is no committee. There are four of us and two of us are the people who voted against it in November and I am one and she is the other, and there is no minute, and there is no vote, and there is no form, and you are the eleventh house and I have been to ten and I am going to say the same four things on your step that I have said on every other step and then I am going to go away and you are not going to be asked a single question about anything except whether you will wind a clock on a Saturday morning.**"

The woman looked at all four of them and then at the woman at the end of the row, who was twenty-four and had not said anything at eleven doors.

"Are you one of them?"

"**No**," said Nell Sayer. "And if anybody writes the word committee on anything about this I will stop, and I have not stopped, and you can ask anybody on this row what time we started and what time we finished and nobody will have written it down."

Then she read the four lines, and at the end of them she said: "Right, I've a kitchen clock on the wall and my mother had one and I'll wind both." And that was a household that said yes and it was said in a kitchen, and the fourth of the morning.

---

**And at number twenty-one nobody came to the door.**

They knocked once, which is the rule they made at the top of the row, and there was a sound of feet in a passage and then a gate, and a boy of about nine came round the side of the house in a coat that was not on and said, "She's at work, she's back at six."

"She's not needed at six. We're not doing it today. We're doing it on Saturday the seventeenth, at about seven in the morning. Will you tell her that somebody came and it wasn't a bill."

The boy considered this.

"Are you the water?"

"No."

"Then I'll tell her you've been." And he went back in and shut the gate, and nobody knocked a second time, because **a second knock on a street where nobody has knocked in nineteen years is a different instrument and they all four knew it and none of them said so and one of them wrote it down.**

**And it was done on the Sunday the eleventh, at about four in the afternoon, in the same four words, and there is no form in this city on which a Sunday may be used for anything, and a Sunday is the one day on which nobody in this district can be asked to sit down about anything, and a step is not a sitting.**

---

**The column, at about twenty past one, at the bottom of the row, in the sun, with four people and a sheet of foolscap and nobody's chair.**

Thirteen households on the row have a clock on the cistern in it and there are **twelve clocks**, because two of the thirteen are two houses in the second court with one clock on the wall between them, and a clock in a court is the only clock in this district that two houses share. **Twelve said yes. One said no at a door in about nine seconds and gave a reason and asked to be asked again on a Friday.**

Nine households on that row have no clock in them at all, and their two standpipes go dry from seven until noon, and they are in no column of any kind, and the instrument four people spent a month building has no line for them, and that was found at a step at number six by a woman of twenty-four who said the whole of it in one sentence and then said she had said it.

"**You have spent five weeks on eleven clocks. There are nine houses on this street with no clock in them and they are going to have no water from seven until twelve, and every one of those nine houses is going to find out about it from the person next door, which is how the twenty-six are going to find out about themselves.**"

And Hanna Stroud said, and it is the last thing she said on that row and she said it once: "**Then the column is the wrong column and the column is a column of clocks, and what has to be asked on Monday is not a second column. A column of clocks is a thing a machine wants. A column of households is a thing a person wants, and there are twenty-two of them and they are all on this sheet, and the eleven of them who wind a clock are in one place on the sheet and the nine of them who have no clock are in another, and no instrument in this city can tell the difference and I can, because I have charged rent in this district for twenty-two years and I have never once had a clock on a rent book.**"

> *Ninth to the twelfth of the twelfth. Twenty-two houses. **Twelve clocks on the cistern, in thirteen houses, because two of the thirteen are one clock in a court, and twelve names, one refusal, and one door done on the Sunday because a boy of nine came round the side of the house in a coat that was not on.** The notice on four boards says eleven and the column says twelve, and **a notice cannot be corrected, it is taken down and put up again, and it comes down and goes up on the twenty-second of the twelfth with a timetable on it that has twelve lines where the notice had eleven.** Nine households with no clock and no column, and the column was found to be the wrong column at a step at number six at about half past eleven in the morning by a woman of twenty-four who has joined nothing and has not stopped.*
> *It was four hours and fifty minutes, from half past eight at the Bellweather Road stand to twenty past one at the bottom of the row, against an estimate of three hours and a half of somebody's, **and the estimate was short by an hour and a half and nobody has worked out yet whether the arithmetic was wrong or the fours were not counted, and a woman of twenty-four says the fours were not counted, and says it without being asked, and has not been thanked.** A fitter at number eight shut the door on the first line. A compositor at number fourteen had to be talked out of doing the whole of it. A woman at number three had a clock in a passage that the district's own counters had walked past twice.*
> *Nobody wrote a word down. **A street is not a room and it is not a meeting and it had no chair and no minute and no quorum and no vote, and it is the first set of twenty-two conversations in this district that has left no record whatever, and it was Wenna Pruitt's rule and she made it on the pavement at nine forty and nobody has argued with it since.**
> *The four lines are a notice and they were read at twenty-two doors in the words a notice is in, and the fifth thing — will you wind a clock — was the only thing anybody was asked, and it was asked twenty-two times and there is no form in this city on which a question may be put to a person about their own house, and we asked it anyway, one at a time, on foot, in daylight, in December, and the reason is that the four lines are the whole of what a notice can carry and the fifth line is the only part of it that is anybody's.*
> *One man said no, at a door, in about nine seconds, and gave a reason about a washing stand and a son of five going to school at eight, and asked to be asked again on a Friday, which is the seventh day and is the sixteenth, and Hanna Stroud wrote his number on the back of her hand and did not write his name, and I am not going to write his name here.*
> *And the first, the second, the third, the fourth and the fifth of the seven days' notice have not been spent. **The sixth day is the thirteenth, the seventh day is the sixteenth, and the seventh day is not mine.***
