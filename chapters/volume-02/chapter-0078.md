# Chapter 78 — Six Hundred Brackets, and the Asking

On Friday the thirtieth of the ninth month a man of twenty asked a question in a works' yard about six hundred standpipe brackets, and the answer took nine minutes, and the price of the thing he had asked for was one thousand eight hundred and ninety pounds against a brass pointer at eleven shillings and fourpence, and nobody in that yard had ever divided one by the other, and the not dividing was the last honest thing in the whole business.

---

**The yard, Friday the thirtieth, about half past ten in the morning, with the gate open and about seventy men in it, and it being the fifth week.**

**Hollis Nunn is the district works' engineer.** He is forty-four, nineteen years in the post, and he is not a villain and he is not a saint and he is the only man in this district whose name is on a published schedule, and he is the only one who has found out what an interval costs.

He had said it once already, in a yard in the fifth month, in front of about thirty people, with a casting in his hands, and he had said that he did not think it would be agreed, **and Orrin Vale had been in that yard and had been given his name on the day and had never once written it down, because a thing a works' engineer says in a yard is not a record and has never been one, and the only reason the name is on this page is that the man who said it is the man who is refusing.**

"You said in May you did not think it would be agreed."

"**I said I did not think it would be agreed and I was wrong about the reason and right about the answer, and it has taken me four months to find out that I was wrong about the reason, and I have not liked any of the four months.**"

"Then say the reason and say the answer and I will go and do something else."

Nunn put down a spanner.

"**The answer is no. The reason is that I cannot write an interval for a standpipe bracket, and there is a clause in the published schedule that says every item in the schedule is inspected at the interval I state and not exceeding twelve months, and the first inspection falls in programme year one, and the clause is a standing obligation and there is not one first inspection in it, and programme year one ends in the fourth month of next year.**"

"That is the answer to something else."

"It is the answer to everything and I will come to your six hundred in about four minutes." He wiped his hands. "**I have been stating intervals on this branch for nineteen years. The trestle brackets are a four-year interval and the last survey was two years and one month ago and they are not due for a year and eleven months and that is a finding and I have been living on it. The crossing brackets are a twelve-month interval and there is a first inspection in year one and I did it in June in a yard in daylight. Every one of them has a number against it and a date and a survey in a file, and the interval and the results are published in the district's own sheet and not filed only, and that clause was carried in June by a man in a good coat who is right about the money and wrong about the district.**"

"And the standpipes."

"**The six hundred standpipe brackets are on my sheet. They are on my sheet at one thousand eight hundred and ninety pounds and they have been on it since before I came, and that figure is a replacement rate. It is what it costs to put a new one in, one at a time, as a contractor's price, and it is a rate and not a programme, and there has never been one single instance in the history of this works of a standpipe bracket being replaced, and that is not because they do not move. It is because nobody has ever looked.**"

---

**And here is the whole of it, and Nunn said it in about ninety seconds, and it is the reason of the batch and nobody in the yard had a word for it.**

"**A standpipe bracket is not my asset.** That is the whole thing and it has taken me nineteen years and a question from a twenty-year-old to be able to say it in a sentence.

"A trestle bracket carries a conduit across a canal on a structure the works built. It is on my schedule. It has an interval because I own it and I know what it is doing and what would hurt it.

"A standpipe bracket carries a standpipe. **The standpipe is on a service. The service is on a property. The property is in a return.** And a service is not a works' asset and neither is a fitting on it, and I cannot inspect a thing I do not own, and I cannot state an interval for a thing I do not own, and the clause in that schedule says every item in the schedule, and **the six hundred are not an item in the schedule, they are a rate on it, and a rate is not an item.**"

"So you cannot put them in."

"I could put them in. **I could put six hundred standpipe brackets in the schedule as items tomorrow morning and it would take me four minutes and it would be entirely lawful, and every one of them would then be an asset of the district works, and the district works would own six hundred standpipes belonging to about four hundred ratepayers, and the ratepayers would own nothing, and there is a rate notice in October.**"

Nobody said anything on the yard for about half a minute, in which a lad of seventeen walked past with a barrow.

"That is the answer," said Hollis Nunn. "**It is not that it is expensive. It is that a schedule line is a thing somebody owns, and the six hundred of them are owned by nobody, because the thing that would own them is a list of properties, and the list of properties in this district is the hole in the middle of everything we have been doing since the ninth of June, and you cannot fill a hole in a schedule by writing in it.**"

---

**And the price, which the twenty-year-old had not expected to be the whole of it, and which he had got out of a book in June and had been carrying since.**

"The tell-tale," said Teo Arden. "**The brass pointer on the trestle bracket. Eleven shillings and fourpence. Six hundred of them is thirty-four pounds.**"

"It is thirty-four pounds," said Nunn.

"**And the six hundred brackets on your sheet are one thousand eight hundred and ninety pounds.**"

"It is."

"So the survey costs thirty-four pounds of pointers and the thing the pointers are for is to find out whether any of them has moved, and if one has moved you put a new one in, and that is the one thousand eight hundred and ninety."

"**That is the first arithmetic anybody has done on that figure in the history of the works and I have wanted somebody to do it for about four years.**" Nunn said it flatly. "And now I am going to tell you why it does not get done, and it is not the thirty-four and it is not the one thousand eight hundred and ninety.

"**It is that the first inspection of the interval clause falls in programme year one and there is not one in it, and programme year one ends in the fourth month of next year, and it is the end of the ninth month of it.** The first inspection for the crossing brackets is in year one and I did it in June, on a Saturday, in a yard, in daylight, with about forty people, and it cost five pounds eighteen shillings and sixpence against a published estimate of two pounds fourteen shillings and sixpence, and the difference was eight hours of a man's time, and the man was the works' fitter and I paid for the eight hours out of my own pocket and told nobody until afterwards.

"**And there is no money in that schedule for the second year of a thing nobody did in the first year.** There is a clause and the clause was carried in June and it is four months old, **and I can put your six hundred brackets into a schedule in four minutes, and they go in on the next adoption, and the next adoption is the fourth month of next year, and the first inspection of them is a year after that, in the fourth month of the year after next, which is about eighteen months off, and eighteen months is not a thing anybody in this district can argue with, because arguing with it means arguing with a clause in a published schedule, and nobody in this district has ever yet been in a position to argue with a clause in a published schedule.**"

"So it goes in the twelfth month."

"It goes in whenever the schedule is next adopted, and the schedule is adopted for three years, and it was adopted in the fourth month of this year and it runs to the fourth month of the year after next, and the next adoption is the fourth month of next year, and the notice of it goes up in the third month of next year, and a new item goes in on adoption and not before." He put the spanner down. "**And that is the answer to your question and it is a scheduling answer and it is the correct one and I have given it to about nine people in nineteen years who did not like it.**"

---

**And a man of eighty-one with a lamp was asked a question on the Sunday the second of the tenth, in his own front room at the top of four flights in number fourteen, Ferrywright Row, and there is no light in that stair's fanlight and has not been since about 1948.**

The arrangement was made by Teo Arden and not by anybody else, and the reason it was made by him is that he went and asked first and was refused twice by people who had better reasons, and then he went to the top of four flights and knocked on a door.

"You have got about two minutes," said Alden Fenwick. "I have got a lamp to fill and the wick wants doing and nobody in this building has done it since my wife."

"Six hundred standpipe brackets," said Teo Arden.

"I know what a standpipe bracket is. **I have had one over my cellar since 1922 and it is on the wall of my cellar and I put my hand on it about four times a day going down with a lamp.**" He put the lamp down. "You have come up four flights to ask me whether I want a brass thing put on it."

"Yes."

"**I have wanted somebody to ask me that since 1951.** I wrote to the landlord and he wrote back that it was a matter for the landlord, and I have never been angry with him and I have never been able to put the two together since. And you are twenty." He looked at him. "You are the first person anybody has sent."

"Will you have it?"

"No." He said it immediately and he was not being dramatic. "**Because you are going to write it down and put it in a schedule, and the schedule will be adopted in the fourth month, and I came into this building in 1941 and the last time this building was inspected was 1949 and the thing they looked at was the roof.**"

"It would be a line in a schedule for the fourth month of the year after next."

"**Then I will be eighty-two when it is inspected, if I am alive, and I have got a lamp.**" He picked the lamp up. "And the other thing, and this is the one you should write down and not the one about the lamp.

"**You have come up here because nobody would say yes to you. I can tell, because you have not asked me a single question about the cellar and you have asked me a question about a bracket, and a man who wants a cellar inspected asks about the cellar.** I had four feet of water in that cellar in May and I told nobody, and nobody has asked me about it since, and the man who wrote it on a bit of folded paper is a lad of about twenty who has never been in my cellar in his life, and he is carrying it about in his coat. **You are the third person in three months who has carried something of mine about in a coat and not one of them has come up the stairs.**"

Teo Arden stood in a small front room at the top of four dark flights and did not say anything for about four seconds.

"Would you say yes," he said, "if somebody came up the stairs and asked you about the cellar?"

"Yes," said Alden Fenwick. "**And there is no form, and there has not been one since 1951, and I have been told so twice, and I am eighty-one and I have stopped writing.**"

---

**And on the Monday the third of the tenth it was put in front of a woman of fifty-four with nine blocks, and she did not refuse it, and that turned out to be harder.**

Hanna Stroud had it on her table on the Monday morning because he had asked her on the Friday and she had said come on Monday.

"Right," she said. "**Before anything else: I am not going to be the woman who says this is a good idea, because you have not shown me what it is, you have shown me a price.** One thousand eight hundred and ninety pounds is a great deal of money in this district and it is nothing at all in a works' order book, and both of those are true, and which one it is depends entirely on whether a person is holding the pen, and the pen in this case is the district works' schedule, and the district works' schedule is eleven thousand pages of things that are somebody else's fault.

"So. What do you actually want."

"I want the six hundred read."

"Read by whom."

"**By anybody. By a man with a lamp. By a fitter on a Tuesday for an hour a day for a month, which is about twenty-five days and it is not a survey and it is not an inspection and there is no form for it and there is no interval and it is not in anybody's programme, and it would find out.**"

She looked at him for a while.

"**I have nine blocks and about two hundred and eleven households and eleven of those blocks have a standpipe on them and I have never once seen one taken out, and I have had four burst mains in twenty-two years and every one of them was somebody else's water and every one of them came out of my tenants' rents and I have never been told why a pipe burst.**" She squared a stack of papers she did not need to square. "I would like that done more than I have wanted anything in about four years and I am not going to say so in this room, because if I say it in this room it is a landlord asking and a landlord asking is a landlord."

"Then what do I do with it."

"**Nothing. Put it down.** Put it on your wall. Put the figure on it — one thousand eight hundred and ninety, and thirty-four pounds of pointers, and the two of them in the same line because one of them means the other one. **And then stop, and do not come back to me in a fortnight with a different version of it, and if you come back I will know that somebody taught you to, and I will not help you.**"

"That is a strange thing to hand a man."

"It is the only thing I have got and it is worth about four months of arguing in a yard. **A thing that is written down and not acted on is worth about nine hundred times a thing that is said in a room, and I have been in eleven rooms this year and I have got nothing on paper except a certificate in a glass case and six figures in chalk, and I would not have either of them without a man who could be asked to explain them.**"

---

**And on the Tuesday the fourth of the tenth at about four in the afternoon it went on the wall of the outer room of the pumping ward, in chalk, in the middle height, and Bevin Sarr wrote it and did not write the line under it that everybody in the room wanted her to write.**

> *Six hundred standpipe brackets on the district works' own sheet, at £1,890, as a replacement rate. Nobody has ever looked to see whether one of them has moved.*
> *A tell-tale is 11s 4d. Six hundred of them is £34.*
> *Asked, Friday 30th September, by T. Arden, in the district works' yard. Refused in nine minutes by H. Nunn, engineer.*
> *Reason given: a standpipe bracket is a fitting on a service and belongs to nobody, and a schedule line is a thing somebody owns, and the thing that would own six hundred of them is a list of properties which does not exist in this district in any form that can be acted on.*

And underneath it, in smaller letters, and she wrote that herself and nobody in the room asked her to:

> *The first inspection falls in programme year one. There is no first inspection in it and programme year one ends in the fourth month of next year. **The asking is the thing. It has been asked. It is on this wall with the price on it and the reason on it and nobody has to be told about it again.***
