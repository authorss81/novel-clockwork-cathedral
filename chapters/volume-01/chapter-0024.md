# Chapter 24 — The Twenty-Eighth

The room was the second door and it was warm, and there was tea, and the cup was the chipped porcelain with the rim gone white, and it had been in that office since before Orrin was apprenticed, and it was the last day of the month.

He had not slept. That was the fault of the twenty-seventh: he had got on a tram at half past four with the ledger shut on his knees and had gone eleven minutes without opening it and had then been up until two, and the ear had gone first — the right one, a hum up somewhere behind the eardrum that he had learned in September how to work around by turning his head further than he needed and leaning in rather than sitting back — and then the jaw, which he had been flexing under the counter for four days and which would not close properly on a bad night and had been fine on a good one since the seventh of the month.

He had a form in a bag and the fee in a coat pocket in coin, four shillings and sixpence, counted out at half past eight, because he did not trust notes for that and never had.

---

Hask had the lever escapement out on his own bench under a glass and had been reading it with a loupe through one eye, and he put the loupe down when Orrin came in, which he had not done the other two times.

"You look tired," he said. "Sit down. It's a bad morning for it, and the tea's no better than it was, and I've put it out anyway, because there's no sense in a man drinking water in a warm room."

"It is the twenty-eighth."

"It is the twenty-eighth," said Hask, "and I've had my own since eight, and I'll tell you why, because it's the sort of thing that's better said than implied. I do this at ten on the twenty-eighth, every year, with the holders I have asked to come, and it's the whole of the reason I asked you. It's not a queue. It's one at a time, and it's in my own book, and I do it out of my own pocket in the sense that the master pays for his own tea."

He turned the cup a quarter turn on the saucer.

"So. How's the year gone?"

---

There were two answers available, and Orrin had known that on the Friday, and he had spent the weekend finding out which one he was going to give, and the weekend had not helped because both of them were true and the question was designed so that they would both be true.

"Bad," he said.

"That's most years." Hask did not write anything down, which Orrin had been expecting him to do and did not entirely like not doing. "Bad how? Bad as in you haven't made the rent, or bad as in you can't sleep, or bad as in somebody's died and you'd rather not say so in a warm room in front of your licensing authority."

"The second one. And the third, sort of, but not somebody."

"Mm," said Hask, and did not turn the cup. "I'll tell you what I do with that, since you'll want to know and I'd rather you didn't spend the night on it. I do nothing with it. I write in the book *year: bad, holder's own word* and I put the date against it, and that goes in a file that is not published, and it has been in a file that is not published for twenty-two years, and I could not tell you this morning what I wrote in nine of them. It's not a kindness. It's a filing system. There is a great deal in this trade that looks like a kindness and is a filing system, and the ones that are only a filing system are the ones you can rely on."

He picked up his tea.

"But I am not going to let you sit there being thirty-two and tired and tell me a man's name into a room. If you want to say it, say it. If you don't, it goes in the book under a line and I shall ask you again in a year and you will say *worse*, and we shall both know what we are doing."

Orrin took the tea. It was as bad as it had been a month ago and about one degree warmer, and there was a skin on it.

"My father," he said. "Eighteen years ago, in the storm month."

Hask put the cup down.

"Aye," he said. "Well. That's not news to me and it isn't news to you, and I'm not going to do the thing where a man of my age gets his face ready. I have buried two of my own and one of them in a hall, which nobody believes." He did not say anything else about it. He let about four seconds go by, and then he said, "Right," in a voice that closed the subject, and moved on, and it was so cleanly done that Orrin understood that he had been given a thing and had not known he was being given it until it was over.

---

"You're working," Hask said. "I can see it. You've had a hand on a bench for four years and I have watched you in this room a hundred times and I can tell what a fortnight off a bench does to a man who has never had a fortnight off a bench in his life. What is it?"

"Nothing I could put in a form."

"That's the answer I expected and it's the wrong one." Hask said it lightly. "Try the other one."

"There were four days in the first month where I was not on the board," said Orrin, "and one of them was a Sunday morning, and nine men helped, and I told you about the Sunday morning and you said you knew about the trestle and half the Quarter knew about the trestle. I have not had a conversation about it since and I am not going to have one today, and I have said yes to the other part of that, which is that I would tell you if there was work, and I am telling you, and it was a hospital pump in Green Span and a dead cistern in a yard in Northglass and a timer on a wall."

"Did you certify anything."

"No."

"Nothing on those that wanted a certificate."

"The timer wanted one and I did it. It's a pharmacy interval timer on the same line as the ward and the corridor, and the ward is right, and I certified the timer and the certificate says nothing about the ward and it took twenty minutes." He turned the cup he had not been given a saucer for, which put it near the edge of the desk. "Which is a sentence I have been turning over since the Sunday and I have not got to the bottom of it yet."

"That's a better sentence than most of what I hear in here and I have heard four years of them." Hask got up, went to a drawer, and took out a sheet of the guild's own printed stock, folded once, and put it on the table between them. "Second sheet of the W-4. That's what I wanted to give you, and I have been waiting for a quarter of an hour to find a way in."

Orrin looked at it. It was the same sheet he had taken off the licensing counter on the Monday afternoon for nothing, and it was folded the same way, and it was the *other* half of it — the hall's return copy, with a line at the foot for the office use of the licensing floor and a rule at the head requiring it to be returned with the book.

"The card asks for four things and none of them is this," he said.

"No. Nobody asks for this. It is not on the card and it is not on notice five, and if you do not hand it in, they will hand you a fresh one at the counter on the nineteenth and the fresh one will have this year on it and your book will have last year's on it, and then a man in this building in about two years will have a document in front of him that says a maintainer's book was not returned with the set." Hask sat down again. "Housekeeping. That is all that is. Four shillings and sixpence, one signature, and that sheet, and it is the whole of the transaction and there is no fourth thing."

Hask had not mentioned the fee. Orrin had it in his coat. Hask had not mentioned the nineteenth. Hask had, on the Monday, been asked by nobody about anything and had answered nothing, and the notice with the two-hour window on it was on a wall two rooms away and had been on that wall for years.

"There is a new standing order on the licensing floor," said Orrin.

"There is a standing order on the licensing floor," said Hask, "and I did not write it, and I have not changed it, and I am not going to ask you what it says, because if I asked you what it says I would be a master asking a man about a notice, and there is a difference between that and a man telling a man where the counter is. The counter is open on Sundays for licensing, by the way. There is no law saying it is not and there is no law saying it is, and it has been open on Sundays for as long as I have been in this room, and there is usually a porter at the door and a clerk behind the counter and nobody else in the building, and you will not be troubled and you will not be hurried and I am telling you that now, on the twenty-eighth, on purpose, so that you spend the nineteen days between now and then not dreading a Sunday."

That was a kindness. It was exactly a kindness, and it had been arranged with a precision that Orrin could feel in his teeth, and the two facts sat side by side and neither of them cancelled the other, and that was the entire machinery of the thing, and Hask had not threatened him once in forty minutes.

---

"You've gone quiet," said Hask. "That's the regulator."

Orrin turned round.

It was on the bench under the glass, a lever escapement about a hundred and forty years old in a case with a moon dial, and it was running, and it had been running when he came in.

"Give me your honest answer," said Hask. "Not your professional one. Does it want oiling."

It was a kind question, asked genuinely, by a man who knew what he was asking. Orrin stood there and listened to a hundred and forty years of going train and escape wheel, which was a sound he had been hearing since he was fourteen in kitchens and shops and his father's back room, and it was the best sound in the world and it told him nothing.

"No," he said. "No, I can't tell."

"Mm," said Hask, and did not look up, and did not ask him a second question about it, and that was so careful that it took Orrin a moment to work out what it was.

"You can tell by the top of the click," Hask said, "and by the way the drop sits on the escape wheel, and you always could. You've been listening for forty years." He lifted the glass off and put his thumbnail against the cock. "It's dry, by the way. About a fortnight dry. Not badly, and it's not hurting anything, and it'll run for another two months and then the click will start to go and you'll hear *that* if the room's quiet." He put the glass back. "I only asked because a man came in here in January who hadn't been in a room with a good escapement in twenty years and wanted to talk about it, and I found out more about him in ten minutes than I've found out about him in nine years, and it wasn't what I was after. That's the whole trade, and there's no lesson in it, and you'd think there was."

Orrin put two fingers on the edge of the table and left them there.

There was no room in that building for the sentence he needed to say, and there was not going to be, and the reason there was not going to be was that Hask had been kind to him about it, deliberately, in front of the only witness, and had then told him the answer.

*That is what it costs to want to do something so much that your body puts the whole of it on your account before you have decided.* He had written that on the eighth of the month in a book nobody could read, and here was a hundred-and-forty-year-old governor in a glass answering a question about whether it wanted oiling, and a man with a loupe giving it away for nothing.

---

"Right," said Hask. "I have got a quarter of an hour and there is nothing else on the list, and I have not been going to have a list for you, and I want to say the last thing and then I shall let you go because you have a train of your own to think about and I can see it on you."

He turned the cup round one last time and did not turn it back.

"You asked me in the corridor on the twenty-fifth of the first month — the day the card came off your bench — whether I had moved your date." He said it back exactly, which was a thing Hask did with documents. "I did not. I have not. It is the nineteenth of the third month and it will be the nineteenth of the third month, and I have not touched the fee, and it is four shillings and sixpence, and you can count it in the shop before you come, and I would rather you counted it in the shop than argued with me about it in here.

"And there is a letter I am not writing, and I want to tell you the shape of it so that you can stop waiting for it, because a man who is waiting for a letter cannot hear anything. If I ever write you a letter it will be about a machine. It will be a specific machine, at a specific address, on a specific date, and it will say what is wrong with it and what I want done about it, and it will be true, and you will be able to act on it, and there will not be a second paragraph. There is no version of that letter that is about anything else, and you have got the nineteen days between now and then in which to be convinced of that, and that is the only thing this room has ever been for."

He squared the paperwork on the desk between his two hands and did not look at it.

"You've been in this building six times since the card came and you have not been afraid once, and I have been waiting to see whether you would be, and you have not, and I have found that interesting, and I have not written anything down about it." He got up and opened the door, which is a thing a master does at the end of a conversation and which ended it. "The twenty-eighth of next year, if you want to come and tell me how the year went. Same room. Same cup. Optional. Nothing follows from it."

---

Orrin sat for a moment in a chair that had not been offered to him, in a warm room, on the last day of the month, and asked the question he had come in with, because he had not come in with it, and because it was the only one left.

"Has the hall ever been asked for its labour ledger?"

Hask stopped in the doorway with his hand on the frame.

"Yes," he said.

"By a district?"

"I could not tell you."

"You've been in this room twenty-two years."

"I have been in this room twenty-two years," said Hask, "and I could not tell you, and I have never once wanted to, and I would like you to understand that those two clauses are the same clause. When I took this hall the steward showed me the returns and the certifications and the fees and the price books, and there is a shelf in that room of things that are answered once a year, and a ledger is not on that shelf, because nothing has ever come up that has wanted one." He came back three steps into the room, which he had not done in the whole forty minutes. "I will tell you what I have instead. I have a card. It has a form number and it is in a drawer and it goes out about four times a year, and it goes out because somebody asks a question a free question, in a corridor, and the answer is that there is no such thing, and the answer is on the card, and I do not have any say in it and I have never been asked whether I would like to change it, and I would not, because it is true, and being right about it is not the interesting part of my morning."

"Did you know it came out three times on Monday?" said Orrin. "Because it came out three times on Monday, and I put one of them on the table of a woman with four staff, and the drawer has four in it now, three of them older than this month, and she is going to write a paper about the fact that it is not a refusal, because it is not, and I have not got an answer to that either."

Hask looked at him for about three seconds longer than the conversation had required.

"No," he said. "I did not know. Thank you for that; it is a small thing and it is the kind of small thing that keeps a hall honest, and I am not going to be a hall that is not." He put his hand back on the frame. "Do not thank me for anything else, because there has not been anything else. Good tea."

---

He was nearly at the bottom of the worn stair when he stopped, and he stopped because he had done the arithmetic in the room without meaning to and could not get past it.

On the twenty-sixth of the first month Hask had written a date on a card and told him: *the twenty-eighth of next month, at ten, in this room.* A month out, and three weeks before the renewal, and the two figures are the same figure counted two ways, because the writer of them had done the sum before he spoke, and had said the second one in the round because a man who gives you an exact number of days is telling you that the days are the point. And Hask had said he had put it in the middle on purpose, so that a man who was going to have a conversation about his year would have it before he decided what he thought of it, and a man who was not would have it while there was still time to write.

Nineteen days, and not three weeks. That was the whole of the cost, and it had been spent in forty minutes, in a warm room, over a bad cup, with a man who had never once raised his voice or once been anything but exactly what he was.

He stood on the cold stair and worked it out properly, the way he worked everything out.

*He has not moved the date. He has not moved the fee. He has told me what the counter does on a Sunday so that I will not be afraid of it, and he has given me a second sheet nobody asks for, and he has told me the regulator wants oiling, and he has answered a question I did not have the right to ask by telling me the price of it, kindly, in front of the only witness in the building.*

*And I have paid nineteen days for it, and I could not have got them back, and there is no document, and the document that would prove any of it is a file that is not published.*

*In nineteen days' time he will be at that counter at ten, and he will sign a piece of paper, and there will be nothing on it about this hour, and there never is.*

He went out into the lane, and the rain had stopped, and the right ear was going and the jaw would not close, and both of those were a bad night and a bad month and not a price anybody had charged him, and he was aware as he stood in the doorway of his own shop with the key in his hand that the last nineteen days had cost him nothing at all, that they had cost him a sleep, and that the sleep had been given to him free by a man he had lied to five weeks ago about a Saturday night.
