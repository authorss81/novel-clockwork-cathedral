## The batch in one paragraph

The clocks stopped stepping and stayed out, the Ward's own book said a line error that nobody will put on a form, and the Water Users' Committee — **nine men with a property qualification, who were told on the second of the third month that a class-three maintainer wanted the night hours and could not be asked by a district that had no way to be asked** — did the correct, lawful, terrible thing and re-voted the fourth draw on the seventeenth, so the front is protected and the line starts stepping again and the whole city gets further from the truth it already could not see. Meanwhile a two-pound-fourteen-shilling-sixpence bracket that has been carried forward twice sits at line seven of a published schedule in programme year three, a nurse has a card in a board saying *no fault; line error; reported, no report form exists*, and a warden has restored three hundred and twenty-four thousand gallons of a ward's water to the same seventy-one houses it was taken from and been thanked by nobody. Then it storms. A storm puts the Green Span hospital trestle's eleven-year failing load into a hospital ward whose whole slack is forty minutes, and the only reserve in this city that can carry the repair is the pumping district's, and the front needs it too, and **there is no Reading anybody may hold to settle it, because settling it privately and in silence is precisely the thing that killed Perry Vance.** So the man who has spent fifty chapters learning that a correct answer spends its cost on whoever cannot pay it does the one thing he has sworn not to do: he does it in daylight, in public, with the arithmetic printed where anybody can check it, he names the outage, he posts the hours, and he puts his own name under a number he has no standing to certify — and the guild suspends him for it, and the volume's last ten chapters are the bill.

## Batch goal, shape, and turns

- **The opening is a job.** A paying customer, an invoice, a named person who can refuse the work, four clocks on a private spur and a woman of sixty-six who did not ask for anything. **This is the volume's rule three finally working** — *no result of mine goes out of this room without a person named who can refuse it* — satisfied by a piece of paper instead of a favour. The stair lights. The bracket does not get fixed, because the bracket is in year three.
- **The middle is the measurement.** A craftsman gets a tape, a level and a straight edge onto a canal trestle in daylight and writes down a number anybody can check, and the number is that a span has dropped three-eighths of an inch and dropped further since somebody last wrote it down, and that the load on it is the hospital's clock pressure, and that the pressure comes out of one reserve that belongs to a district four miles away.
- **The cost is the storm and the arithmetic of one reserve.** The choice is not between right and wrong. It is between **doing it at night so that nobody can object to it** — which is what Chapter 9 nearly was, and which is what killed Vance — **and doing it in daylight with the hours posted**, which is slower, more expensive, and impossible to forgive.
- **The end is a bill read out in rooms.** A suspension that is not a punishment for wrongdoing. A pump with a name on it. A ledger with two new columns and one blank. A woman who keeps her post and gets a process she did not have. A councilor who files what she blocked. And a stranger on a stair, asking a question the book has spent fifty chapters refusing, and a name to give him, and nothing at all to put in the column next to it.

## Style

Rain-lit, tactile, grounded in repair work and in rooms where paper is read out loud. **This batch's measure is still a tape measure, a level and a stopwatch, and in the last four chapters a printed sheet with a number on it.** The storm is weather and it is not a villain; the trestle is iron and it does not mean anything. **The climax's emotion comes from the posting, not from the weather**: the moment is a man giving a district the information it needs to be angry with him, in writing, early, with his own name at the bottom.

Orrin's competence must be visible in the doing. **Chapter 41 is the chapter where the reader gets to enjoy him**, and it must be written with real craft pleasure: a clock let down, a barrel cleaned, a beat set, weights on a line, the sound of a movement running again after eleven years. He is not a scholar of politics and can misread a room; in this batch he is in rooms he is not a member of more often than he is in his own, and the reader should feel the difference every time. His interiority is procedural — he counts, he measures, he works out what a thing will do — and it should sound like a craftsman thinking. Show information through work, conversation and consequence; do not deliver lore. Let the important moments breathe. Keep paragraphs to two to six sentences, with a one-line paragraph only as a deliberate beat. Vary rhythm; do not lean on *held, steady, plain, counted, breathed, wrote, nodded*. The System is the Consequence Engine: one sparse physical notice in plain declarative language, at most one per chapter, and **this batch should not need one at all.**

**Chapters 47, 48 and 50 are the three to get right.** The reader has watched a correct answer save a bridge and leave out a crew, and a category swallow a district, and a published schedule swallow a scandal, and a kept promise cost the same people the diversion helped. **If the climax is dramatised as heroism the volume has no argument; if it is dramatised as punishment the book has no warmth.** It is a competent man doing a competent thing in public, on a piece of paper, with his name on a number he is not allowed to sign, while the people he is costing stand in the room and are entirely right to be furious. The guild will suspend him lawfully for exactly that, in four true lines, and it will be the correct use of a well-run system. **And Chapter 50 must be quiet.** A stair, some chalk, a stranger's question, a name, a blank, and nine.
