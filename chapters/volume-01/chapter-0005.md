# Chapter 5 — The Pump That Should Have Stopped

The afternoon was the strangest of his life, and he could not have said why at the time.

He had got in at ten past five with a clean certificate and a fee receipt in his coat, and had not taken his coat off since the Hill, and the bench was there, and the vice was there, and the list was on the board, and every single thing on the list was a machine that a person had paid him to fix. Market scale, done. School bell, done. Ropeworks work clock, done on the day before. Four clocks in the Saturday row, two tap repairs, a regulator to look at for a man in Northglass who had been waiting a week.

He stood in the middle of his own shop with his coat still on and found that he did not want to do any of it, and could not have told a client.

So he did what he always did, which was to put his hands on something. He cleaned the vice. He put the grey ledger on the bench and opened it to a fresh page, and put in the date, and then set down *What the Loom knew at 2.10 this afternoon*, and made a list.

*Water: Sable head, Morrow Sound, tail of the tide, Northglass basin level. Weir Two forebay, Weirs slipway, Weirs culverts. Gates: Weir Two, Ferrywright sluice, four tide doors. Load: Ferrywright Bridge deck, hand rails, tram wire tension on the span. Clocks: eleven named. Schedules: six named, of which two are the pumping ward's day and night, and the night one is a drum, not a document.*

Then he sat and looked at it, and it was a good list, and it was a complete list, and every single line on it was in a book somewhere that a clerk could produce on a Tuesday.

Not one line of it was a person.

He tried the other way, which was what he actually wanted. He wanted the blind margin to have a shape, the way a missing tooth has a shape you can put a finger into. *Unregistered night work is not represented.* He copied the phrase out twice. Then he set down the night shift at Weir Two, which was four men by the works' book and five by the board, with the hours Vance had told him and the hours Vance had not, and then the question he needed answered — *whose hours?* — and he looked at it, and understood that he had no instrument in the world that could answer it, and that the machine upstairs had answered it in a footer half the size of a signature, in a type designed to be read by nobody, and that the footer had told the truth, and that a true statement of what is not represented is not a complaint.

He sat with the grey book open on his knees for forty minutes and listened.

That was the stupid part, and he would think about it for years, and it would be the stupidest thing he ever did and the only thing he ever did that was genuinely new. He put his fingers flat on the bench, on the frame, on the vice, the way he did when he wanted to know a machine, and he waited for the city to tell him what it had spent.

And the city told him. It was astonishing how much it told him, within reach. He could hear the four clocks on the Saturday row, three of them good and the fourth a half-beat behind, which was the loose pin he had been meaning to look at since Tuesday. He could hear the bench clock on his own wall, which was on the district line, and which was the one thing in the shop that had changed, and it had changed by three minutes, and he found that instantly, without effort, the way you find a missing tooth with your tongue. It was a small cold place, no bigger than a farthing, and it was the most satisfying thing he had felt all day, and it was his own clock on his own wall, and it would be even again by Friday.

And there was nothing in the note at all about the night shift at Weir Two. Nothing. Not an absence he could feel the shape of. No gap, no silence where a person should have been; the sound simply did not go there, and had never gone there, and he understood with a completeness that made him put both hands flat on the bench and hold on that the machine he had just spent his district's morning on had not lied to him, had not hidden anything, had simply never been told, and that nothing in Morrowfen, from the crown of his head to the sole of his boot, was going to be able to hear the shape of a man who was not on any instrument.

He was a man with a very good ear, listening in exactly the wrong place, and he had three quarters of an hour to work on it, and it told him nothing.

---

The Weirs at dusk on a rising tide is a place where everybody is looking at the water.

Orrin came down the slipway at half past six with his case and a lamp he did not need, because the lamp was how you carried yourself through a building that had water in it, and there were already nine men and a woman on the pavement outside the Weir Two pump house and none of them were there for the water. They were there because the work clock was fast and the head was up and the night crew came on at seven, and if the stage had to run they wanted to be stood somewhere.

The watchman was a young man in a coat three sizes too large, sitting on an upturned bucket with a stove and a whistle lanyard.

"Vance," he said, before Orrin had got to the door. "He said if the clock man came back after four he'd be inside. He said you'd be stupid enough to come at dusk."

"He's inside?"

"He's on the board." The watchman nodded at the door. "I'm not letting anybody in that isn't on the works' book, and you're not on the works' book, and I'd have turned you away at four o'clock as well, and I'd have been right to."

"I know you would," said Orrin. "I'm not asking you to let me in. I'm asking you to let me stand in the doorway for ten minutes with a lamp, because that stage is going to run past midnight and I want to hear it when it decides whether to stop."

The watchman considered this, and Orrin could see the exact moment he understood that it was harmless, which was the whole of his authority and all it had ever needed to be.

"Ten minutes," he said. "And you touch nothing, and if it goes, you go down the slipway and not in the river, and I'll not have to write anything."

---

The second stage was on its bedplate and the forebay was over the fourth mark and the lamp made the room the colour of weak tea.

It was the biggest thing he had ever put his hand on, and the frame was cold and oily and it had a heartbeat in it, and the heartbeat was not regular, and he knew before he had his palm flat why.

He put it flat.

He asked it in the plainest words he could find, because the words mattered more than he had understood before this moment: *if the second stage is asked to stop tonight, what fails first?*

And the answer came up through the bedplate into the bones of his hand and up his arm, and it was not a sound this time, and it was not a beat. It was a weight. It was the sensation of a thing leaning, steadily, in one direction, with no intention behind it and no opinion, the way a rope leans when whatever is on the far end of it is heavier than the rope.

He felt the stage run to its count, and then run past its count, and then keep running; he felt the discharge close against a path that was not where the logic said it was; he felt water that had been shut in coming back up a line, hard, all the way, and a man going down a ladder into the middle of it because a man would, and he felt the whole of that night standing there with its collar turned up, and it took about four seconds, and it was not a vision, and there was no light in it, and there was no face in it, and he was not told a single word.

Then his knees went and he caught the rail and he was sick over the floor of Weir Two pump house, four gallons of bile and cold tea, and the room swam and rang and had three edges to it, and the joint of his right hand, the one he had used, throbbed from the wrist to the second knuckle as though he had been struck there.

He sat on the step of the machine with his forehead on the rail for a long time.

There was no word for what he had just done. He had done it before, in the way you have done things before without knowing they had a name, but he had never done it about something that had not happened yet, and he had never done it about a man.

*There is going to be a night,* his hand had said, *and it is not one anybody has to be told about, and it starts when this stage fails to stop.*

He stood up. His ears were full of a high thin whistle on the right side and everything on that side sounded as if it were happening in the next room.

He went out to the watchman.

"Right," he said. "That is going to run past midnight and it is not going to stop, and the discharge is going to push back up the bypass when the tide comes through, and if the hand valve is open when that happens it is going to be driven open further and stay there."

"That is the valve," said the watchman, patiently. "It is open. It is open because they open it in June."

"Then it is going to be open in an hour and a half, and when it is, that stage will hammer itself to pieces against a tide that has not crested yet, and somebody will go down there to shut it by hand, and the pump that is supposed to stop them will not stop, because the logic in that box is working from a clock that says the valve was shut when they started."

The watchman took the whistle off around his neck and turned it over in his hands.

"Say the part where I open the gate for you," he said.

"I have no way to open the gate. I have a class-three licence and it lets me service and certify one civic machine. I can't file a district proposal. I can't witness anything. I have no shift time, I have no proposal, I have no number, and I have not got a single name to give you of anybody who is on that crew, because the crew is four men in a book and five men on a board and I met them this afternoon and the man who leads them has no licence and no hours and no paper of any kind in any office in this city." He heard how his own voice sounded and hated it. "I am asking you to let me send a message up the hill and get somebody with a licence to make a decision before that tide comes through. Not to make a decision. To send a message."

The watchman looked at the slipway, and the water, and the sky, and then at Orrin's hands, which were shaking, which he had not known were shaking.

"Send it yourself," he said. "I will open the door for a man who is sending one. I will not open the door for a man with a feeling."

Orrin went back up the slipway on his legs and found a boy in a kiosk near the tram stop and put nine coppers in his hand and said, *Cathedral Rise, Question Hall, a question about Weir Two, and tell them the night watchman will corroborate that the second stage will not stop, and say it now, and say it twice, and if anybody asks you for money for it, say it louder.*

The boy said, "It'll be five o'clock before anyone's off the Hill."

"Then it will be five o'clock," said Orrin, "and it will be too late, and that will not be your fault, and it will be mine."

He stood at the stop in the rain and watched the boy get on the tram, and then he went home, because going home was the correct thing to do, and it was the worst thing he had ever done in his life, and he did not sleep, and at about half past nine the bells of the district went out and a boy with nine coppers came back down the slipway and told him that the Hall had closed at four and the watchman had said what he was, and there was no question to send, because a question needs a proposal and a proposal needs a licence and he had told them both about his licence, and anyway there was nobody at the counter to argue with, and anyhow the crest was tomorrow afternoon and everybody on the Hill was at home in their chairs.

---

The crew came on at seven because the crew always came on at seven, whether or not there was a book. There were four of them, because the works' book says four, and Vance was four minutes early as usual and Bevin Sarr was on time and Amos and the Fenn boy came in with their coats still buttoned. Nobody said one word to any of them about the head, the stage, the valve, or the afternoon.

That was the thing, afterward, that Orrin could not get past. The Directorate had not concealed anything from them. There was nothing to conceal. A correct answer had been delivered, in the correct form, to the correct office, and a copy had gone down a pneumatic tube to the pumping ward, and the pumping ward had put it in a file, because a district does not interrupt a night crew with a document, and because the model said the valve was shut.

The night tide came in at about twenty past nine and it came in hard, the way a tide does when there is a river running at it, and by ten the second stage was hammering, and the banging came up through the pump house floor and out into the street where the loose tuners had their bench, and Bevin Sarr came out and stood on the step in her coat with the board-hook in her hand and listened to it the way you listen to a man shouting in the next street.

Perry Vance took his lamp off the nail and said, "That's the valve."

He said it the way he had said it in the afternoon — flat, unsurprised, the way a man names a thing that has already been named.

He got the bar. Bevin said, "It's a tide, Perry, the crest's not till tomorrow." And Vance said, "It won't be tomorrow when that comes up the bypass, and the Fenn boy is sat on the stage with his back to it and I'm not having the stage go with him on it," and he went down the ladder in his own boots with the bar under his arm, and the water was over the third step.

Orrin was two hundred yards up the slipway at the time, in the rain, in the dark, standing in the road with nine people in it, because he had come back at nine without deciding to and had not been able to make himself go home a second time. He heard the hammer stop. He heard something else, once, that he did not have a name for then and would spend a year of his life not having a name for.

By midnight the tide had made. At half past three they got the man out of the well.

---

Dawn, and a wet grey light, and the street outside the pump house full of people who had all been told and none of whom had any work to do.

Bevin Sarr was sitting on the upturned bucket with the board-hook across her knees and the board-hook was not for anything. She told Orrin, in a low flat voice with her hands still, exactly what the night had been: the valve had been found open when the head passed through, wide open, driven up hard by the water coming the wrong way, and the stage had been running against it for an hour and a half, and the stop that should have come at the changeover had not come, and when Perry went down to shut it he was doing the job the machine thought had already been done, and the pump he was standing next to did not stop, and the discharge came back over the ladder.

"Two more in hospital," she said. "Amos and the Fenn boy. Amos will keep the arm. The Fenn boy's fourteen and he's got water in the chest and they're drawing it tonight, and he was sat on the stage with his back to the ladder when it came back." She looked up. "They've put the body in the shed behind, and they're waiting for his people, and the warden's clerk is up the hill, and there's a man in a wet coat asking everybody in the street if they saw a stranger in the pump house last night."

"He came to me," Orrin said. "I was at the door. Ask him."

"He has. He says you told him the stage would not stop, and he says the watchman told him you offered to send word to the Cathedral, and he says nobody came." She was not looking at him with anything. "Perry shut that valve. Twenty years, twice a night some nights, and tonight he shut it because somebody wrote on a slate that it was shut and it wasn't, and he didn't know anybody had written anything, he just knew it was open."

"I know," Orrin said.

"Then you can tell me one thing for the board." She got the chalk and put it on her knee. "Who was in the building. There's four names on the works' book for last night and there's five on mine, and one of the five is the Arden boy, and the Arden boy was two streets over all night and hadn't stood a shift here in a month, and nobody has said one word to me about that, because he's not a man, he's a fifth name in chalk." She set the five names down in chalk and put the chalk away. "That's what made four look like a miscount instead of a removal. Twenty years I've been saying they took a crew off the model and nobody would have it, because every time somebody looked at the book it said four, and four is a number, and four is a building."

Orrin looked at the five names on the board and thought, with a clarity that was almost restful, that the district had been defending itself for twenty years against a document that agreed with it.

"You'd better," said Bevin Sarr, and got up, and went back inside, because there was a body in the shed and a board to chalk on and no argument anywhere in the district that she had time for.

Orrin stood in the road and put his hand on the pump house wall, and there was nothing in the wall at all, and that was the answer to the only question he had left.

He had not killed Perry Vance. He went through it in his own head, standing in the wet, once, and made himself do it properly, because he knew from twenty years of being asked to explain faults that the first account you give of a death is the one that will be true of you for the rest of your life.

He had not opened the gate. He had asked the only honest question he knew how to ask, and the machine had answered it, and the answer had been right, and the bridge was standing this morning. He had known at half past eleven that the stage would not stop, and he had stood at the top of a ladder and watched a man go down into the water on his own initiative because it was his valve, and he had said the thing that was true and useless, which was that it would run past midnight. He had not written a note, because a man with reasonable grounds had explained to him, in a warm office, over tea, at half past eight that morning, that a note would cost the Quarter and achieve nothing, and he had believed him, and he had gone on believing him for six years of his life about nine hundred other things. He had had no licence, no proposal, no shift time, no name, and no number, and there was no instrument in Morrowfen that could take those five absences and do anything at all with them, and there was one instrument that could, and it was eleven miles up the hill, and it needed three witnesses, and it needed a fee, and it was shut at four o'clock, and it needed somebody to stand at a rail and put their wrist in a bond and say *I am here and I agree to remember this.* Perry Vance had not had any of those things either. Perry Vance had a chalk mark and a bar and twenty years, and that had been the whole of his apparatus, and it had been enough to keep a district out of water for twenty years, and it was not enough last night.

Being right had been the injury. He understood that, in the road, in the rain, with the bells going and the tram wires hissing overhead and the whole of the Lower Weirs standing in the street in the clothes they had slept in, and he understood it the way you understand a new tool by the cut it has already made in you: it was going to be true of him for a long time, and there was not one thing he could do with it, and the machine that had given it to him had closed at four.

A clerk came down the slipway at eight with a printed sheet under his arm and a hood full of rain.

He put it on the door of the pump house, weighted it with a stone, and went round the street telling anybody who would listen that the Cathedral of Consequence confirmed that Ferrywright Bridge stood through the first tide of the head, that the question had been answered and the gate opened in good time, that the toll had been three district minutes from the Clockwork Quarter, and that the city had lost nothing that morning that it would not get back. Then he asked if anybody had seen a stranger.

Orrin read the notice on the door from three feet away.

Every word of it was true.

When he had read it he found that he had one piece of information that nobody else in that street possessed, and that it was the most useless piece of information in the city, and that it was going to stay that way for as long as he lived.

The model had said the valve was shut.

Nobody else in Morrowfen had any idea that that had ever been a question.

---

They came at about ten with a handcart to take the body up to the cold room on the Rise, and the street cleared the way a street clears.

The loose tuners were still there — there were nine or ten of them, in threes and fours, and Orrin had not registered a single one of them in three days — and they had come down the slipway in ones and twos all night, from the lower lanes, because that is what you did, and they were standing about in the rain with their belts and their lamps and the particular stillness of men who are waiting to find out whether they are going to be blamed.

One of them was standing apart from the others with his hands in his pockets, in a working coat that had been let out at the elbows twice, and he was twenty years old, and he had been awake for two nights, and he was not looking at the cart or the crowd or the notice on the door.

He was looking at the door of the pump house, and at the board inside it, and he did not turn round when the cart came out.

Then he turned round, and he looked straight at Orrin, and he did not ask him anything, and he was not a stranger to the dead man at all.
