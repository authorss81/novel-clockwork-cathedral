# Chapter 115 — What a Ward Costs

On the Monday the sixth of the third month a woman of forty came into the nurses' kitchen at Green Span hospital at ten in the morning with a blank sheet of paper in her hand and asked to count something, and Leda Vale told her that there was no form on which anybody could be asked to do it and that she should go and see the hospital's returns, and the woman said that she had, and that the returns did not have it in them, and that she was not asking for a return.

Imani Rook chaired the Assembly's committee on public health. She was forty, she was the Green Span delegate, she had been on that committee for six years and had run two hearings that nobody in the committee thought were memorable, and she came into the kitchen without a hat and without a secretary and with a coat over her arm, and the reason for the coat was that it was still cold in the first week of the third month, which is a smaller thing than it looks and which she would have said if anybody had asked her.

"Sit down," said Leda, "not there, there is a bundle of clean linen in that chair, sit there. Do you want tea?"

"No," said Imani Rook. "I want to be somewhere with a clock in it. I have spent six years in rooms where the only time anybody quoted was the time limit on a motion."

"Right," said Leda. "Then you can have the landing."

She said it exactly the way she said everything, which was as a fact with a decision inside it, and the woman at the table put the blank sheet down on it and did not sit, and said, "Before I ask. My niece buys a hundred minutes for this ward in a kitchen in Bellweather Road. I have not asked her about it and I am not going to, and I am not in this room because of it, and if anybody in this building thinks the two facts are connected they should leave now and I will not hold it against them."

Nobody left, and the reason nobody left is that in a hospital the number of people who leave a room when a person of forty says a true thing about a relative is not a number that improves anybody.

"That is the correct statement to make and it is the wrong order," said Leda. "Say the thing you want first, in case you run out of time, and then say the other thing."

"I want the hours," said Imani Rook. "I want to know, across this hospital, how many hours a week are spent by the staff of this hospital on clock work. Not medicine. Clock work. The writing, the reading, the comparing, the reconciling, the going round with a card. I want it in a year and I want it in a ward and I want it by grade, and I want it for the wards in all eleven of the hospitals in this city and I cannot get it for one of them."

"Why not?"

"Because there is no form in this city on which a hospital is required to return it," she said, "and I know that, and I have known it for six years, and I have asked for it four times in writing and three of those times I was answered with a letter about the district's return, which is a different return, and the fourth time I was answered with a letter about the year before last, which is the same letter. And I want it for all eleven of the hospitals in this city and I have had it for three wards of one of them, and those are not the same ambition and I am not going to pretend they are."

"What do you want it for?"

"To put in front of the Assembly in the autumn," said Imani Rook, "with a working, in a document that a member can take out of the room and show to somebody in a lobby. Not a resolution. A figure with a working on it. And when it is on a table with a working on it, the answer to the question 'what would it cost to fix the clocks in this city' stops being a sum about brass and becomes a sum about people, and the second sum is about a hundred times bigger, and I have never in six years been able to make that happen with a sentence."

"That is the best reason anybody has had for a count in this building in my time," said Leda, "and I am not going to do it."

"Why not?"

"Because you cannot count it by asking," said Leda. "You have no return and I have no return and neither has the matron and neither has the hospital engineer, and if you send a form round eleven hospitals asking for the hours, the answer you will get back is a number a matron has estimated in her office in four minutes from memory, and it will be wrong by a factor of two and it will be wrong in the direction that makes the hospitals look better, because that is the direction every estimate in the world goes when the person making it has a budget on the other side of it. If you want the number it has to be timed, and it has to be timed by the people who are doing it, on the job, with a watch, and that is my three sisters' labour, and I am telling you what it costs before you ask, because you are the first person in six years who has asked me for a figure instead of a feeling and you are not getting one that costs me nothing without being told."

"I know what it costs," said Imani Rook. "That is why I am asking now and not in the autumn."

---

The count took two days and it was done in a room with a board in it, and the room was the board room off the third landing, which has a table and eight chairs and a clock over the door that is nine minutes slow and is not the free master and has never been the free master.

The working was this, and it is checkable, and it is on the board in a hand that is not Orrin's and not the clerk's and is Leda's.

Three wards of one hospital: the two medical wards on the second landing and the surgical ward at the end of the third. Three handovers a day in each: seven, three, and eleven at night. At each handover the sister on the ward reads the free master, reads the line clock, reads the two watches where a ward is on the two-watch method, and writes the interval for every patient on the card, and reconciles what the card says against the drug chart, and does it standing up in a corridor with the noise of the ward behind it.

At the seven o'clock handover it takes six minutes. At the three o'clock handover, three minutes, because the ward is quieter and the round is shorter. At the eleven o'clock handover, three minutes.

That is twelve minutes a ward, a day, on the mean. Three wards is thirty-six minutes a day. A hospital year is three hundred and sixty-five days and thirty-six minutes is thirteen thousand one hundred and forty minutes, which is two hundred and nineteen hours, which is nine days and three hours of one person's working year, spent in three wards of one building in a city, by people who are paid to be nurses, on a column with no heading.

Alaric Tope, twenty-four, committee clerk, timed it with a stopwatch on the Tuesday and wrote it down in a table with a column for each ward and a column for the grade of the person doing it, and he was frightened the entire two days and it showed in the way he kept asking whether the figure would be checked, and it should have been and it was.

The eleven o'clock handover was done on the Tuesday night by E. Cobb, who was nineteen, and who was the only person in the building who could do it and who had to be asked to do it and who had already been thanked for nothing in her life, and who said yes.

"You have to time me all the way through," she said, "and I am going to ask you to start again if you do it kindly."

He did it three times and wrote the three figures and the mean, and the three were two minutes fifty, three minutes, and three minutes twenty, and Cobb looked at the spread and said that a ward's handover was not a thing that had a figure, it was a thing that had a floor and a ceiling, and the ceiling was on a bad night, and that if he wrote the ceiling down instead of the mean then in a year somebody could compare it, and if he wrote the mean down then in a year nobody could.

"I am not going to put a ceiling in a column called hours," said Tope.

"Then call the column what it is."

He called the column what it was. There are two columns in that table now, *best observed* and *worst observed*, and the mean of the three is thirty-six minutes a day, and the worst is thirty-seven, and the report prints the worst, and the worst is two hundred and twenty-five hours and five minutes, which is nine days and nine hours and five minutes of one person's working year, and the report says in a footnote that it is the worst rather than the mean, and the footnote was Imani Rook's and it is the reason the figure is worth anything at all, and the number that will be quoted at her by people who have read the first page and not the footnote is two hundred and nineteen, and she would like that on the record as the first thing that will go wrong with it.

Imani Rook did not check it. She took the figure and the working and the two days and she did not send one letter to anybody, and the reason she gave, on the Wednesday, to Orrin, who had come in at ten with a lamp he had not needed, is the sentence that the whole of the third month is standing on.

"You have got a figure with a working on it," she said, "and if I send it anywhere with a letter on it, then in about four years somebody will ask how the number was got, and the answer will be that a committee of the Assembly asked the hospitals, and the next question will be how the committee got it, and the answer will be that the committee asked the people who were doing the work, and by that time there will be a form. And the form will have a box in it that says *estimated by* instead of *timed by*, and the box will get used, and in nine years a hospital will be able to tell you a number about its own clock work that it has never once measured, and it will be a good number, and it will be wrong, and the people who will be harmed by it will be the people doing the work, because they are always the people who are harmed by a good number."

"Then what are you going to do with it."

"Take it into a room myself," said Imani Rook, "in September, with the working, and let it be argued with, and publish whatever comes out of the argument, including the parts that are wrong, because the parts that are wrong will be found by somebody who wants it to be wrong and that is the only way a figure with a working on it ever becomes a fact anybody can lean on. It costs me the standing to have done it myself, which is a thing I have and value, and I am spending it, and I would like somebody to say thank you, and nobody is going to, and I have not come here for it."

Nella Rook was in the room on the Wednesday afternoon and was not asked about anything, and did the three o'clock handover's three minutes, and had her figure written in the column, and her aunt watched her do it and did not help her and did not speak to her about it, and afterwards, on the stairs, said one sentence to her in about four words.

"That was honest, that."

"Yes," said Nella.

They were both right about it and they have not agreed about it since and nobody has asked them to.

Leda's own contribution to the day was the reason the figure exists, and she put it the way she puts everything, at the end of a conversation, flat, to a person handing over a drug chart.

"You will want to know what the answer is going to be used for," she said. "So here it is, and I am saying it to you and not to your committee. In a hospital the only clock that matters is the interval between a person deciding to give a drug and the drug arriving. A district's drift is a municipal fact. It is a municipal fact until it is a dose, and after that it is a death, and there is no form in this city that knows the difference between the two, and that is not a defect in the forms, that is a defect in the idea that there ought to be one. So when you put two hundred and nineteen hours in a room in September with the working on it, half the men in that room are going to hear *two hundred and nineteen hours* and think *paperwork*, and you are going to have to make them hear *two hundred and nineteen hours of a person standing in a corridor at seven in the morning writing down a time they did not choose to be late by*, and I do not know how you do that, and I know that I cannot do it and that I have watched you do it twice."

"I have watched you do it three times."

"You have," Leda said, "and one of them was in a room of forty people, and I have not thanked you for any of them and I am not going to start."

On Thursday the ninth of the third month the working went up on the board outside the board room on the third landing, in chalk, at the height of a person who is not tall, with the date and the two days and the ten words *nothing here is a certification and nothing here stops anything*, which were the ten words on the card Orrin Vale had left on a kitchen table in Bellweather Road on the first of the third month, and which he had said out loud in a room were the only part of a form that anybody in a bad situation can use, and Imani Rook had heard him say it and had written it on the back of her own sheet and had not told him she had done so.

Two hundred and nineteen hours, in chalk, where any person in that building who could read could read it, and about nine people read it before lunch, and one of them was a ward sister of about fifty who said, out loud, to nobody, that she had known it was three years and had not known it was two hundred and nineteen hours, and went back to her work.

Nobody thanked the two women who had timed the handovers and the young man who had written the table and the clockmaker who had brought a lamp he did not need. Nobody thanked the Assembly member, either, and she said on the Thursday that she was not going to be, and said the reason, which was that the day a figure with a working on it is thanked before it is argued with is the day somebody puts a better number under it and lets the thanks stand in front of the worse one.
