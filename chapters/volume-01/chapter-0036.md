# Chapter 36 — The Ledger, Wider

The copy of the guild's labour book had been in nine buildings on the twenty-first of the third month, and by the morning of the seventeenth of the fourth it was in twenty-two, and nobody had sent any of them, and nobody had been in charge of any of it, and the reason was that a printing shop will set any type it is paid for and a weekly paper sells its front page in blocks whether or not anybody is angry.

Eleven small trades on the Kestrel side and Bellweather Road — a laundry, a draper, a lockfitter, a chandler, a fishmonger, a cobbler, a chemist, a tobacconist, a coffee house, a watchmaker's widow, and a builder who had never set foot in the district — had between them eleven pounds forty of advertising and a block of front page going spare in a bad month, and they had between them also a man who set type for a living and had done a favour for two of them and did not ask what the notice was about.

The notice was eleven lines long and it was in the guild's own form and it was a true extract of the hall's own series, and it read like this.

> *From the labour book of the Guild of Horologists, Clockwork Quarter, series 3, site and class as entered:*
> *Lower Weirs, Weir Two floodgate and second-stage pumping station — maintenance-deferred class — batch 21/4 — 1 crew — 3 nights.*
> *Northglass, Kestrel side, boardinghouse stair and landing light — maintenance-deferred class — batch 22/1 — 1 crew — 3 nights.*
> *Sloughs, pumping station, No. 2 — maintenance-deferred class — batch 20/2 — 2 crew — 3 nights.*
> *Bellweather Road, laundry — maintenance-deferred class — batch 23/3 — 1 crew — 3 nights.*
> *and six further series entered in the same class, the total head count of the series being twenty-two.*

Every word of it was true. It was a true copy. It was in an excellent hand, because the hand was Hester Lomas's, and she was working that week for a firm of printers in Bellweather at a rate she has not moved off in nineteen years, and she set the type and did the imposition and was paid at the page rate and a signature, for the same firm in Bellweather that has kept her on its books since the sixth of the second month, and she was not thanked by anybody and did not expect to be, and she has not been thanked in thirty-one years and says so as a measurement and not a complaint.

It went on the front page of the Advertiser on the Friday of the fourteenth of the fourth month, under a line about drapers, between an advertisement for boots and an advertisement for a burial society, at a type size nobody had to squint at, and it was read in this city by about eleven hundred people who are not the people it is about, and it is the only line of type in the volume that has ever been set in type that somebody paid for out of a different budget from the one it concerns.

---

It went up on the Wednesday night of the twelfth, which was the night before a paper came out on the Friday, in two sittings, and the second sitting was the one that nearly stopped.

The press is a two-revolution flatbed in a shop off Church Street that has been in the same family since the first year of the war, and it can do about nine hundred sheets an hour if two men feed it and a third does the ink, and the run was four thousand, and the paper was cheap and heavy and it was raining and the shop had a skylight that leaked onto the stack.

The first sitting did about eleven hundred and stopped at midnight because the ink wanted thickening and the man who had come in off the day had to get back to a house in the Sloughs, and the second sitting started at eleven and had the press running by half past, and at about twenty past one in the morning a man came to the side door with a lantern and a run sheet.

He was the paper's own counter and delivery man and he was fifty-one and he had counted that press at one in the morning for nineteen years and had never once been told what he was counting, and he had a piece of chalk and a run sheet and a key to the type case, and he stood in the doorway for about four seconds.

"That is not our ink," he said.

"No," said Teo Arden.

"That is not our paper either. That is the cheap stuff off Canal Wharf and it will go on the front at eight and the front will be unreadable by two o'clock and the editor will say it is set bad and he will be right."

"That is exactly what we are going to print it on."

The man looked at the run sheet and then at the press and then at the two of them, and nobody asked him to do anything, and there is no form in Morrowfen on which a man in that doorway can be asked to do anything at one in the morning, and that is the whole of the reason he eventually came in.

"I am not going to help you set it," he said.

"Nobody asked you to set it."

"I know nobody asked me. I have been at this press since I was thirty-two and I have never once been asked to do anything I did not do, and I am not going to start, and I would like that on whatever it is you are doing." He put the chalk down on the bench. "I am going to count it, because there are four thousand coming off that stone and if I do not count them then the number in the book is a lie by me and not by you, and I have kept that book nineteen years and it is the only book in this city I have never had to apologise for."

They printed until about a quarter to four. He counted them twice, in fives and then in twenties, and the total was four thousand and sixty because six sheets were spoiled at the makeready and he spoiled them himself and would not let anybody else do it, and he wrote the figure on the run sheet and signed it and put the time on it, and he did not read the notice and was asked nothing about it and said nothing about it and has never in his life said anything about it to anybody, and he is not a member of anything and was not asked to be.

That was the first sitting's whole method in one man in a doorway. Nobody had a right to make him. He made himself. And what he made was a figure in a run book in a printing shop, with a date on it, that says that on the night of the twelfth of the fourth month four thousand and sixty copies of a true extract of the guild's labour book came off that press, and a man counted them, and the man does not know what they are.

---

Hanna Stroud read it on the Friday morning at the chandler's shop, in a page of a weekly, standing up, and got through about eight lines before she put her hand flat on the table.

"There are four hundred and nineteen addresses in that district that I could put a name to, and about nine hundred that I could not, and I have let nine blocks in it for nineteen years and I could not tell you the tenant of a single flat in 12 to 20 Ferrywright Row before this week." She put the paper down. "That is a list of shops, Mr Vale. That is a list of *shops*. Eleven trades on the front of a paper are not a thing anybody made up. They are a thing somebody paid for. And you cannot print a list of shops in this city without somebody, somewhere, being willing to say what the address is and who is behind it, and there are eleven of them and they are not in this district's book and they have not read the binding of this district's book."

Renn Corby put the paper down and did not pick it up again.

"Nowhere near enough," he said.

"Hanna—"

"Listen to me for a minute, because you have been the only person in this district for four weeks who could make that page do something and I have watched you make it do nothing, and I have never said so, and I am saying it now." He was about fifty and he had a rigger's hands and he had been holding one of the nine copies for four months because he was asked and because he had a strong hand. "A list of addresses is not a list of people. That is the whole thing and I have been waiting a month to say it and I have not said it because I thought saying it would stop the printing, and I would rather it went on for another month with nobody saying it, and I have now said it and the printing is not going to stop, so I have cost myself a month for nothing."

"What do you mean."

"The notice names a site. A site is a street, and a street has a number on it, and a number goes to a door, and a door is a route." He turned the paper round without touching the print. "You do not need a name to get to a man. You have needed that since the first month and you have not used it because the method you have built says you will not. Good. Right. That is the method and it is the only reason none of us has been arrested. And the moment the copy in twenty-two buildings is a list of routes instead of a list of facts, then anybody with a grudge, anybody with a landlord's lawyer, anybody in this city who wants to know where a person sleeps, has twenty-two buildings' worth of a starting place and no name in it at all, and the man who could refuse is not a woman in a kitchen with a book, he is a woman in a kitchen with a *book of initials*, and there is not one person in this district who could tell you what any of those initials mean except about four of us."

"Which is why we have not written it down," said Wenna Pruitt.

"Yes," said Renn Corby. "And I want it minuted, in whatever this is, that it is because you did not write it down, and that it is not an accident and it is not squeamishness, and that in about two years somebody is going to come and ask her to write it down, and if she writes it down then the twenty-two buildings become a list of names and the twenty-two buildings become a list of people, and on that day this stops being a district with a counter-record and starts being a thing with a roll."

---

The book of initials is a school exercise book with a marbled cover and it is in Wenna Pruitt's kitchen, on the table under the eleven years, with three other ledgers on the same table, and it has been going since the second month and it has forty-one entries in it and about twenty different hands.

Each entry is a set of initials and an address. The address is a building. The initials are a person, and about four people in the district can read the initials, and none of them has ever written down the key, and there is an entry in the shop book at Vale's Bench that says why, and it was written on the twenty-first of the second month, which is the day the method was agreed, and it says:

> *Because a list of names is a list of names, and a list of names can be produced by a man with a warrant or by a man with a grudge or by an office with a box on the second floor, and a list of addresses is a list of addresses, and an address is a building. Forty-one initials, one key, and the key is in four heads and in nobody's book. When the four of us are dead this stops. It is not permanent. Nothing here is permanent. It is the longest thing anybody in this district has ever built and it is going to last about nine years and that is nine years of refusals, which is nine years more than anybody in this city has ever got out of a piece of paper.*

Nobody has asked her about it since. There is no form on which it could be asked about.

---

A copy of the district's own extract of the series went to the Office of Public Reckoning on the Monday morning of the tenth, in an envelope with a covering note, four days before anybody had thought of a front page, and it was not sent by the tenant council, because the tenant council has four pounds ten less than it had a fortnight ago and is paying a copyist at fourpence a page.

It was sent by a woman in a laundry on Bellweather Road who had a telephone and a good hand and a fortnight of the district's front page and an idea.

Bessie Crane telephoned the Office of Public Reckoning at about eleven on the Monday morning of the tenth, spoke to a clerk for about four minutes, was put through to nobody, and was told — by a clerk, courteously, with a form number — that the office received correspondence, that a copy of a printed page could not be received because it was not a return, and that if the district wished to place anything before the office it should be done on the office's own form, and that the form carried no fee and would be sent out on application.

She applied for the form on the Monday and it came out on the Wednesday of the twelfth and she filled it in in about eleven minutes at the laundry and sent it back the same afternoon, and it was received at that office on the twelfth, and she paid nothing, because the Office's own form carries no fee, which is a fact about the Office that nobody in Morrowfen knows and that Mara Venn has said out loud in two rooms and that four people in the Weirs have now discovered by accident.

What came back took six days, which is the office's own precedent, and it is in the second-floor drawer of the Office of Public Reckoning, where there were eleven of the hall's four-line cards on the seventeenth of the third month, and where there are twenty-two now.

> *Received and noted. The document submitted is a printed page containing an extract of a labour book of the Guild of Horologists, series 3, together with a list of sites in the maintenance-deferred class. This office has no standing in the Lower Weirs pumping district and no authority in respect of the labour records of any guild. The document is received. It is not a return and it is not a toll receipt and it is not a complaint, and this office has no function in respect of it.*
>
> *Received at this office, in one copy, on the twelfth inst.*

That last line is not on the form.

Mara Venn read it standing up in her own office on the Tuesday morning of the eighteenth of the fourth month, with the drawer open on the desk, and she read it twice, and then she sat down and read it a third time, and then she telephoned the laundry on Bellweather Road and asked for Mrs Crane.

"That is not on the form," said Mara Venn.

"No, ma'am," said Bessie Crane, on the other end of a line, having been warned by a man with a bar about nothing in particular. "I asked for it. I have been sitting on that sentence for a fortnight and I will tell you what I said to the clerk and I will say it to you because you have got a form number and I have not. I said: the document exists, and I want the office to write down that it exists, and I do not want you to do anything about it, and I want the date on it."

"That is not what this office is for."

"I know exactly what that office is for," said Bessie Crane. "I have been in this district thirty years and I have had four forms refused and one form honoured and I have never once been asked what I wanted the form for, and I have wanted a piece of paper with a date on it for about eleven years, and the only office in this city that will put a date on a thing without doing anything about the thing is the one with four staff and a written right to be ignored."

There was a pause on the line of about four seconds.

"Who told you that?"

"Nobody. Mrs Crane, I have been an investigator for nine years and I have sent out four hundred and eleven notes exactly like that one and I have never once had anybody ring me up and say *thank you for the note* and I have got one in a week, twice, from a laundry, and I am not going to tell you how it feels, because I have a file open and you are on a telephone."

"Six days," said Bessie Crane. "My cousin got a refusal in four hours. Does that mean anything?"

"It means they read it," said Mara Venn, "and it means it went up somebody and was sent back down, and it means that in about four years somebody in that building will be asked to explain why, and there will be a file, and the file will be about the date on a note and not about the note." She put the pen down. "I want to tell you the correct thing, which is that this is a very cheap way of making a city look at a piece of paper and it is the only way available to you, and that I have used it in the other direction for nine years, and that I have never once thought of it being used at me."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It is a piece of information and I am an office of public information." A pause. "Send me another one when you have one. I will put the date on it. I cannot promise you anything else and there is nobody here who can, and I have written that sentence nine hundred times and it has never once been true before."

---

The hospital line came into it in the second week of the month and not through the Advertiser.

Leda Vale telephoned the shop on the Monday morning of the seventeenth of the fourth month, which is the first time in six weeks she has telephoned the shop, and she asked him one question, which was whether the error on the hospital branch was the same error as the error on the pumping main, and Orrin said it was, and she said *right* and put the phone down, and then telephoned him back four minutes later and said, "I have asked the ward sister to bring the three clocks into the office at seven tomorrow morning and I am going to say a number out loud in front of eleven people and three of them are going to hate it, and I am doing it because you would not ask me to do anything, and I have not told you in advance, and I am telling you now because I have worked out that this is the arrangement and I am not going to pretend I have not worked it out."

The three clocks of the Green Span hospital are the public clock in the porter's hall, which is correct to a tenth of a second on four readings and which nobody uses, and the ward clock and the corridor clock, which are eleven minutes apart and are on the sub-line, and which have not agreed with each other since the fourth of last month. The pharmacy timer loses four and a half minutes a round and has done since the first month and is a good instrument with its click set a quarter of an inch too deep.

On the Tuesday morning of the eighteenth, in the office on the third floor, Leda Vale said out loud, to a room of eleven people including two ward sisters, a porter, a pharmacy clerk, a girl of nineteen who does the ward clock's work by hand on nights and who is called Elsie Cobb and is paid for it, and a ward's clerk with a clipboard:

"The error on this line is eleven minutes and forty seconds, and it has been since the fifteenth of last month, and it is not our line, and it is not a fault, and it is going to be there in nine weeks' time and it is going to be there in nine years' time unless somebody with a form says otherwise.

"That is the whole number. It is not our fault and it is not anybody's fault and I want it said in this room before anybody decides what we are going to do about it, because if we do something about it first and somebody in this building then tells me the number was different, I will have spent a ward's fortnight on the wrong figure.

"Now. The three of us have been comparing by hand every four hours since the eighth of last month. Elsie has been doing the ward clock on nights by hand and being paid for it. I am not going to take that off her and I am not going to ask for anybody else to do it. I am going to write the number on a card, every week, with the day on it, and I am going to put the card on the board in the nurses' room where the families read it, unsigned, and I am going to put the same number on the back of the ward roster with the four districts on it, and I am going to say that number out loud at the change of shift every week for as long as I am on this floor, and in front of the people whose hours it is.

"And I am telling you now, all of you, what that is worth. It is worth a record. It is not worth an hour. Nobody in this building has gained a single minute by my having said the number, and nobody is going to, and every one of you is going to have to do the same comparison in four hours' time as you did this morning, and I am sorry for that, and I am going to keep saying the number out loud anyway, because the day I stop is the day it becomes ours."

Elsie Cobb said, "Will it be on the card in my name or not?"

"What do you mean?"

"Whether the card says a girl of nineteen is doing it by hand. Because if it doesn't, then in a year somebody will say the ward was on time, and it will not have been me doing it." She was nineteen and she had water in her chest and she said it without any self-pity at all, as a question about a form. "Because there is no column for me. That is the thing. There is no column for me anywhere and there has not been for a year."

"The card will say a girl of nineteen on nights is doing the ward clock by hand and is paid at the ward rate," said Leda Vale, "in those words, and if anybody in this building wants to take the name off it they can come and take it off it, and I will write down who did and when."

Nobody took it off.

And on the Monday morning of the twenty-fourth of the fourth month the card went up in the nurses' room of the third floor of the Green Span hospital with the day on it and the number on it and a line at the bottom that said *no fault; line error; reported, no report form exists*, and eleven minutes and forty seconds of it, every Monday, until somebody finds a form or somebody admits there is not going to be one.
