# Chapter 117 — Nobody to Carry It To

On Monday the thirteenth of the third month Corvin Hale wrote to the Weirs tenant council for the second time, and the second letter was better than the first, and everybody in the room said so, and the first letter had been refused on the tenth of the second month in the council's own words and published with the minutes, and thirty-one days is a long time for a man to think about a door.

*To the Weirs tenant council, 13th third. I was refused on the 10th of the second month, in your words, published, and I have read it about ninety times and I am not going to dispute a word of it. I ask a different question and I would be obliged if you would answer it.*

*1. May I put a question to Bellweather Road, and not to Sluice Row, from a room I rent at my own expense in Bellweather Road, about the drift of the clocks in that street?*

*2. If I may, who may refuse me?*

*3. And if there is no answer to 2, to whom would I have to answer?*

*No form is enclosed. There is no form in this city on which I could enclose one and I have been looking for one for a fortnight. C. Hale, No. 14 Pindar Row.*

Wenna Pruitt read it standing at the table and then sat down, which she did not do.

"That is a well-made letter," she said. "He has been thinking about a procedure for four weeks and three days and he has not found one and he has said so on the paper, in his own hand, to us, which is a thing I would not have believed of a broker in the first year of the arcades and which I have not got a way of being unfair about."

"Three questions," said Ivy Pike from the window. "One of them is a request and two of them are about people. In four weeks and three days."

"He has worked out that the first refusal was about a street and not about him," said Sel Kade. "That is the whole of what he has worked out, and it took him four weeks and three days, and he has not worked out the next thing, and the next thing is that there is no answer to the second question and he has already guessed it or he would not have asked it in that order."

"What is the next thing?"

"That the refusal of the tenth binds him and nobody else," said Sel Kade, "and that a refusal binds the person who asked and nobody else is not a shield, it is a knife with the handle pointing at the person holding it, and he has asked us to hand him the other end."

There were four entitled and three present through Monday and Tuesday and Wednesday, and the fourth was the man who had not come, and the three were Ivy Pike, Sel Kade and Wenna Pruitt, and the organiser was not a member and had never been one, because if she was a member she was inside the decision.

Orrin was in and out of the rooms over the chandler's four times that week on business that manufactured itself, and on the Tuesday evening Wenna put a cup of tea in front of him and said, "You are not a member and you are not a member's friend either, and I am going to ask you a question in front of two witnesses and you may refuse it, and if you refuse it I will write down that you refused and not what you said before you refused."

"Ask."

"You posted a letter to the Cathedral on the nineteenth of the second month," said Wenna Pruitt. "Wenna, no — I know. *You* posted a letter. It is a question dressed as a letter and you told me so on the Monday and I have not asked about it since because it is a door and not a business of ours. But it is the same question as the second one on that paper in front of us, and I have been sitting here for two days trying to get a handle on why, and I cannot, so I am asking you."

"The letter says it," Orrin said. "I asked the Director of the Cathedral who may refuse me if I go down into a room under her building. That is question two of Hale's letter with a building on it. She answered it. There is a woman of fifty-eight in that building who has been the registrar for nineteen years and she may refuse me and she was told she may and she was not asked whether she is willing, and she said that not asking her would be a form."

"Was that the answer?"

"That was the answer to the first one," said Orrin. "To the second she said no. She will not write on the door, in her own hand, with a date under it, that nobody may refuse me. She said it would be true, and that in four years a person would read it as a permission, and that she had spent four years learning the difference between those two and I had not."

Ivy Pike sat down for the first time in an hour and said, "She is right and it is not much of an answer, and there is nothing in it that could be written down and put in a drawer and used in four years. That is what a door is. A person, in a room, at the time."

"That is the answer to Hale's second question," said Wenna Pruitt, writing. "It is a person in a room at the time. And the answer to the third is that there is nowhere to carry it, and that is what the January minute says in clause four, and Hale has asked us to confirm it in writing and we are not going to, because a written confirmation of *there is nowhere* is a schedule of nowhere, and a schedule is a thing this city has been very good at since 1919."

On Wednesday the fifteenth a woman came to the rooms at about four in the afternoon, on her own, with nobody, and knocked, and said her name and her street and did not come in until Wenna came out to her in the yard.

She was thirty-four and worked Thursdays to Saturdays at a laundry in the Clockwork Quarter and stood on the step of a tobacconist's and said, "Nobody sent me. There is no list with me on it and I am not one of twenty-six anybody. I live at number eleven and I came because I have read the minute about the man in the arcade and I would like to put a line in your book."

"Put it in your own book," said Wenna Pruitt.

"That is where it is going," said the woman, "and I have not got a book, and I have had a month to work out whether I am allowed to be in a room and I have decided I am not, and I would like it minuted that I asked."

They stood in the yard for about ten minutes. Wenna Pruitt asked her three questions, which were the three questions she asked everybody, and they were the same three: what are you called, where do you live, and what is it you want said.

"Dilys Fenn," she said. "Number eleven, Sluice Row. And I want it said that the man in the arcade may not ask me anything about my hours, and I do not want him to, and I want my refusal to be in the same book as his and to be as easy to find as his, and I am not asking the council to do anything at all, because a council that does something is a council that can be asked to do something else."

"Two things," said Wenna Pruitt. "One: that refusal binds you and nobody else, so nothing you write here protects your neighbour and does not restrain you either. Two: your name and your door number go in a book in a room, and that book has a shelf, and the shelf is not locked, and anybody may sit down and read it, and the reason we publish is not that it is noble and it is that a refusal nobody can be shown is not a refusal, it is a preference."

"I have thought about that for a month," said Dilys Fenn. "I would like my door number in it more than I would like it out of it. If the only way anybody finds out that I said no is by opening a book, then put it in the book."

The council sat on Thursday the sixteenth in daylight with the stove lit and four entitled and three present, of whom one was not a member, and it is worth saying that this is now the third occasion in eighteen weeks and that nobody on that body has suggested a different day.

They read the fourth clause aloud, slowly, and Ivy Pike read it and Sel Kade read it, because a clause that has been read aloud twice in public in four months should be read aloud a third time in a room where a decision is being taken, and it is the fourth and not the first or the second or the third, and everybody in the room knew that without saying it, and one of the three said it out loud.

*"The council may not put a question to the row either. The row gets the same seven days' notice a schedule gets. The row may refuse the council in the same words and in the same book. And there is no office, court, committee, form or appeal in this city to which either refusal may be carried, and both of them stand."*

"Answer the first," said Ivy Pike. "May he put a question to Bellweather Road from a room he rents in Bellweather Road, about the drift of the clocks in that street."

"He may," said Sel Kade. "The refusal of the tenth named Sluice Row, and the row's water, and any hour belonging to a person of the row. His new question is about a different street and about a room, and the council's right is over what may be asked of Sluice Row, and it says nothing whatever about Bellweather Road, and if the council extended it to every street in the district it would be a body that could put a question to the whole of the Weirs, and that is the opposite of the fifth clause."

"Answer the second."

"Answer the second properly," said Wenna Pruitt, "and the second is the one that has been sitting on this table for three days. There is no procedure. There is not a procedure for *him* and there is not a procedure for *her* and there is not a procedure for anybody, and there is not a procedure for a man with a room in Bellweather Road because we have never had to refuse a man with a room, and the reason we have no procedure is not that nobody has made one. It is that a procedure is a form, and a form is a way of asking without the rule, and we spent January finding that out and it took four hours."

"Write it," said Ivy Pike.

"Not in those words."

"Write it in the words of the clause and add a ground. *No procedure exists in this city by which the question in his second paragraph may be put, or refused, or carried. The council has no such procedure and will not minute one, for the reason given in the fifth clause at its making, which is that a procedure is a form.*"

"That is four lines and it is true and it is four lines that will be read by every man in Bellweather Road within nine days and understood by about six of them," said Wenna Pruitt, "and that is the correct number, and I will write it."

"Answer the third."

"Third," said Sel Kade, "is to whom he would have to answer, and the answer is to whoever stands in front of him, and that is a person and it is not a body, and it changes every day, and there is no way of writing down a rule about a thing that changes every day except to say that whoever it is may refuse, and that is clause four, and he has had it since January and has not used it."

And then Dilys Fenn had her line, and the room found that it was not a problem at all, and that the finding about it was the finding, and it was put in the minute in five lines and read back twice.

*"11th Sluice Row. D. Fenn, 34, attending at her own request, no form, nobody sent. She requests that the refusal of C. Hale of the 10th second, in its terms, be held to bind the row and any person of it, so that no person need answer it twice. The council records that it has no power to extend the refusal, and will not pretend to, and records that a refusal binds the asker and nobody else and does not bind any other person, and that the only way to obtain such a refusal is to write to a body in the same words, and that the council will receive such a writing and minute it and hold it on the same shelf as the others. Grounds: clause four. No person thanked."*

"That," said Sel Kade, "is the whole of what this body is for, and it took a woman from a laundry to say it, and it does not stop one thing."

"It does not stop one thing," said Ivy Pike.

"And nobody can be shown the line a refusal stops at," said Wenna Pruitt, "and that is the answer to Hale's third question and he will not like it, and it is true, and it is the only true answer anybody in this room has."

Nobody in that room thanked anybody, which is a thing that happens in rooms like that one, and Orrin could not have said why and did not try, and the minute went into the cloth-bound book on the shelf and the copy went on the Saturday notice run, and two hundred and forty households in four streets got a printed sheet about a man in an arcade and a woman in a laundry and the reasons a body has no power to extend a refusal, and not one of the three people who wrote it was thanked for it, in the sheet or out of it.

And the answer to Hale's third question went into it too, in three lines, and the three lines were: *there is no person to whom either refusal may be carried, because there is no office, court, committee or form in this city; the only person who may refuse a question put to them is the person who is standing in front of whoever asks it, and that person is not the same person on any two days; and this council will not minute a rule about it, for the reason given in the fifth clause, which is that a rule is a form and a form is a way of asking without the rule.*

"It is a bad answer," said Sel Kade. "It is a true answer and it is a bad answer and both of those are the same answer."

"It is the first one anybody in this city has written down," said Wenna Pruitt, "and it will be read by about forty people in nine days and understood by four, and the four will be the ones who are stopped by it, and that is the correct number and it is not going up."

At about six she put the book on the shelf next to the 1948 book that is not a minute book and next to the row book from the pumping ward that somebody had brought in a fortnight ago and had taken back again, and neither book cites the other and the two offices have never heard of each other, and the only thing the two of them have in common is a shape, and a shape is the only part of a rule a person in a bad situation can copy, and she did not say that out loud because four people in the room already knew it and one of them was not a member and had no standing.
