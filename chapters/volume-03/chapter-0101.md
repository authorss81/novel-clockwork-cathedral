# Chapter 101 — A Hundred Minutes of Brass

On Friday the thirteenth of the first month Orrin Vale opened the shop at eight and swept the step before he touched anything, because the step was the one part of Vale's Bench he could put right without asking anybody, and the bench inside was already set for a week's paid work and a letter he had not answered.

The shop was four paces wide, door to bench, on the ground floor of a building with a bathhouse over it and eleven rooms over that. He had taken the lease for the landing light and a stair nobody else wanted. The test stand stood by the window, where the Quarter's public clock showed through the glass every hour of the day and cost him nothing to set a rate against. Two drawers of wheels, sorted by size, in brown paper. A tin of twenty-two millimetre offcuts that he bought by the pound from a clockmaker in Northglass who never asked what he wanted them for.

The letter was on the shelf behind the bench, under the narrow book. Four lines on the Cathedral's own paper, dated the eleventh, signed Anja Voss. He had read it on the twelfth in the pumping ward's outer room with Bevin Sarr in the room and had not read it since. He knew the shape of it by heart and that was worse than not knowing.

*A door is not a form.* That was the sentence. She had put it third, after the date and after his name, and after it she had asked him to come on a Tuesday, and the Tuesday was the eighteenth, a week off when he read it, and he had read the letter on the twelfth and said nothing, and it was the thirteenth.

He had spent a working life learning the difference between a thing that could be refused and a thing that could not. A notice could be refused. A form could be refused. A door was a door, and a person standing in one asking to be let through had already done the only thing a door knows how to do, which was to be shut. Now a woman who ran the building that had taught him had written the difference down in one sentence and asked him for a Tuesday.

He put the kettle on and left the letter where it was.

The grey ledger was open at the week. He had written the week's money in on the Thursday night in pencil before ink, the way he did everything now since the forty-one seconds. Eighteen shillings of bench work booked, a school clock and two watches, and three weeks of electricity owing, and the hall's out-of-shop rate of four shillings and sixpence an hour written in the margin because that was the rate everything else in the city was about to be measured against and he wanted it where he could see it.

The kettle boiled, and he drank it standing, and while he drank it he read Thursday's entry twice. On Thursday he had done a whole movement correctly and backwards — pinion out before the plate bridge, and the plate bridge held by the pinion — and the clock had run, and the invoice had said four hours.

He wrote that in the margin with a rule under it. *Thursday: order wrong, clock ran, invoice said 4h. Bill the next one for the time it takes, not the time it is worth.*

The bell over the door went at half past nine.

The customer was a printer's devil of about seventeen, with ink under his nails and a parcel wrapped in proof paper, and he put the parcel on the counter like a man delivering something he had been told not to drop.

"Mr Vale? Mr Coss sent it. Said you'd know what it is and not to let his men fiddle with it."

Orrin unwrapped it. Inside was a bracket clock of about eleven inches, japanned case, white dial, the kind of office clock that gets knocked off a shelf once and never tells the time again. And beside the clock in the paper, loose, a small brass disc with a hole in it.

He picked the disc up before he touched the clock. It was about twenty-two millimetres across and three thick, with a raised rim, stamped on the head with a Q and the figure 100, and on the rim with a year and a die number so small he had to hold it to the window. It was cut clean. It weighed in the hand like a half-crown and sat warm from his own fingers in about four seconds.

"What did Coss say about this?" he said.

"Said it was in the clock. Rattled. One of the men found it when they moved the office. Said it's money."

"Did he say how much?"

"No. He said you'd say."

"It isn't money," Orrin said. "It's a token."

The boy waited. Orrin set the disc down on a piece of white paper and did not pick up a tool yet, because a customer who thinks a thing is money will watch what you do with it, and a customer who has been told not to let men fiddle will count your movements.

"A token is cut from the surplus plate of a public clock that held more surplus than it needed at a return," he said. "A holder brings the movement in, the surplus goes on the plate, and if there is more brass there than the next repair wants, the hall cuts a piece off it and stamps it. Not off a new clock. Off one that has been paid for twice."

"So the clock gave it up."

"The clock gave up what it had spare. A token is a promise that the clock will be held steady for a stated time if it stops, and the whole of the promise is the hole in it. You spend it by putting it in the clock it was cut from, and the escapement is held off its dead angle for a hundred minutes, and a clock that would otherwise be a paperweight keeps being a clock."

The boy frowned. "So what does a hundred mean?"

"A hundred minutes."

"That's four shillings and sixpence worth?"

Orrin looked up. "Who told you that?"

"Man in the arcade. Said he'd give four and six for any with a Q on."

Pindar Row was off Weigh Lane, forty-one pitches under a glass roof, and Orrin knew it the way a Quarter man knows every place that sells what he mends. He had walked through it on Tuesdays for eleven years without stopping at the six pitches that had begun trading in tokens. He had never held one over his own counter before.

He turned the disc over a second time. Q meant the Quarter's die. A hundred meant a hundred minutes. Four shillings and sixpence was not a fact about the token. It was a fact about what a man in an arcade had decided a token was worth this week, and a man's willingness to pay four and six for a hundred minutes of the Quarter's margin said one thing and one thing only, which was that he had four and six and no lawful way to spend an evening.

"All right," he said. "Now the clock."

The back came off with two screws. Inside, the movement was sound and dirty, the crutch bent about a sixteenth where it had taken the knock, the pendulum suspension kinked. Ordinary work. He could see the whole order of it and then, halfway through seeing it, lost the thread of which screw came out first, and stood with the screwdriver in his hand and no sequence in his head.

He put the driver down, took the narrow book from the shelf, and wrote in pencil before he began anything: *back, two screws in tin; crutch out; suspension off; clean; bush centre; straighten crutch; hang; beat.*

The boy watched. "You always write it down?"

"Since September," Orrin said. "Forty-one seconds. I lose a sequence and I don't know I've lost it. The book is the sequence now."

"That'd be a bad thing to have. At the shop we set type by memory and you can see a page that's got a word in the wrong place from across the room."

"Yes," Orrin said. "You can. That's the whole of it."

He did the work in the order he had written. The crutch came out, the suspension came off, he cleaned the plates, bushed the centre wheel, straightened the crutch against the bench anvil and hung the movement on the test stand. It beat clean in four minutes. He set it to the Quarter's public clock through the window and wrote the rate on a beat-card.

That was the moment the third correction offered itself, the way it had five times since the summer. The dial feet were loose, and the quick way was to lift the dial on its pins with a bar and ease it true by eye, eleven minutes of hand work saved, no receipt and no form. He had done it twice in his life and both were in the hall's W-4 now. He could feel his hands wanting the bar.

He did not take it. Not because a clock made right stops being a public instrument a whole street can compare itself by — that had been December's reason, on Sluice Row, and December was done. This was a different reason and he said it out loud because the boy was there and somebody ought to hear it said once in a shop.

"If I lift that dial by hand I will have mended the clock and left no way for Coss to know whether I mended it or moved it," he said. "The book behind that counter says who pays for the record. This shop's book says who did the work. A hand correction is a record with nobody in it, and I have got two of those for my life and I am not having a third in a week with a letter on my shelf I haven't answered."

The boy thought about that. "Would he know?"

"No."

"Then how's it a record?"

"Because I would know," Orrin said, "and a shop is a thing that other people are allowed to ask questions of. If the only person who can read a mend is the man who did it, it isn't a record, it's a habit."

He pinned the dial properly, screwed it down, cased the movement and wrote the invoice. Three hours at four shillings and sixpence is thirteen shillings and sixpence, materials one shilling, total fourteen shillings and sixpence. The addition was exact and he wrote it twice.

"And the brass?" the boy said.

Orrin wrapped the token back in the proof paper separately from the clock. "It goes back to Coss in paper, not in the movement. A token inside a clock he does not own is a hundred minutes he cannot spend without putting them into somebody else's margin, and I am not going to be the one who does that on a Friday morning with his office boy in the shop."

He wrote what he was going to say on the back of the proof paper, in ink, so the boy could not get it wrong on the walk back. A hundred minutes is a hundred and twenty nights at fifty seconds a night, which is four months. A man in an arcade was offering four and six for the brass, and the brass was worth four and six and the minutes were somebody's, and those were two different facts and the boy was to keep them apart.

"There's your paper," he said. "Read that to him word for word, don't improve it, and if he asks what four and six is, it's a hundred and twenty nights and not a hundred and twenty minutes."

The boy put the parcel under his arm. He did not say thank you and Orrin did not look for it. Nobody had been thanked in that shop since the autumn, and it was not discourtesy. It was method: a man who is thanked for carrying a thing stops writing down that he carried it.

After the boy had gone Orrin stood at the bench with the narrow book open and added one line under the order of operations, in ink this time.

*Token weighed by eye only. Not assayed. Returned loose. Count of my own hand corrections is two.*

He closed the book. The letter was still on the shelf. He had not answered it, and not answering was also a thing with nobody in it, and he wrote that down too, because the sixth time in a year the correct thing had been to write a thing down in a book in a shop and put nothing at the bottom of it, and this was the seventh.

On Saturday the fourteenth he beat the clock again in the morning, found it holding two seconds a day slow against the Quarter's public clock through the window, and sent word to the printer that it was ready and would be collected. The token stayed in its paper on the counter all day where anybody coming in could see it, and a woman with a shopping basket stopped in the doorway and asked whether brass was what she thought it was.

"Clock part," Orrin said. "It's a customer's. It's going back today."

"That's the loose stuff off the plates, is it."

"Some of it is."

"Cost a fortune, do they."

"Somebody paid four and six for it this week," Orrin said, and did not say by whom or for what, and the woman went out deciding that clocks were a rip-off, and he let her decide it, because she had not asked and a shop that answers what it was not asked is a shop that talks.

That evening Mara Venn came in for four minutes with a printed sheet in her hand and put it on the counter without ceremony. It was the ward's rate notice for the pumping district, struck on the Office's figure with the working on it, three shillings in the pound on six thousand nine hundred and sixty, one thousand and forty-four pounds a year, and her name was on the schedule and not the Office's.

"I am the name on it," she said. "I wanted you to see it before somebody reads it to you wrong."

He read it twice and gave it back. "Is it right?"

"The six thousand nine hundred and sixty is the ward. The fifty-one thousand four hundred and eighteen is not the ward and I want you to hear me say that, because anybody who puts the two numbers side by side will make a total out of them in about a minute and the total will be wrong. Fifty-one thousand four hundred and eighteen is the sum of four hundred and eleven printed head counts, added in three sittings, the two foot totals agreeing, and it is the city's figure and not this district's. It is on the return as a schedule before the rate was struck, and the rate is struck on the six thousand nine hundred and sixty, and there is a line in between them that nobody has ever printed. Whether the number under it is right is a different building."

"Whose building?"

"The one that has to answer a question about the water and cannot." She folded the sheet back into her coat. "The Office can print a number. That is all the Office can do. It cannot walk out to Bellweather Road and knock, and if it could it would not be the Office any more, it would be a man with a clipboard, and men with clipboards are what the district has been getting for a year."

She looked at the token in its paper on the counter. "Has it started?"

"It was on my counter this morning," he said. "A boy said a man in the arcade would give four and six for any with a Q on. I sent it back loose."

"Good," she said. "Don't tell me what it was. I want to be able to say I don't know."

Then she asked him, because she asked everybody and because it was a Friday and the shop was empty, whether a district could refuse a number as well as a question. He said he did not know, and that a refusal and an absence were different animals and the difference was a person in a room willing to say no, and she said that was the answer she expected from him and she looked disappointed in a way that suggested she had expected it.

She went out into Verge Lane with the rate notice in her coat, and neither of them had thanked the other for anything, and the shop was quiet.

He locked up, wound the test movement down, and wrote the day in the grey ledger. At the foot of the page he wrote the figure the whole of this month would be measured against, the way Leda wrote a number on a card whether or not anybody wanted it.

*A night of drift is fifty seconds. A hundred minutes is six thousand seconds, which is a hundred and twenty nights, which is four months. A token costs four shillings and sixpence.*
