# Character State

State at the end of Volume 01, batch 0003 (Chapters 21–30). Read with `state/chapter-summaries.md` and `state/continuity.md`. A volume is roughly a year; ages are canon and must not be rounded.

## Orrin Vale, 32 — municipal clockmaker, line horologist

- **Wants, in the order he would say them if asked:** to find out what his father did in the storm eighteen years ago and whether the street photograph ever becomes a name; to keep a district's night hours on a page that an office is obliged to receive; and, since Chapter 27, to understand why the correct thing and the useful thing are two different documents. He no longer wants to prove a district can produce its own hours — the district proved that in the first month and it cost them in the third.
- **Has, at the end of batch 0003:** a **class-three licence renewed on Sunday the 19th of the third month**, on the expiry date itself, in a two-hour window, endorsed by Hask in four seconds, **valid for a year from the date of lodgement**, and **not suspended**; a W-4 lodged with forty lines out of two thousand pages, one of which has put eleven years of the Weirs' night work into the hall's own form of his own motion; the copybook's contents; the priced deferral; the copper wedge; eleven copies in nine buildings; a **fair copy of the Weir Two line in the hall, perfect, signed, and missing the only evidence there was**; a district that will not be a single thing; a grey ledger; and a footnote on page nine of a printer's return that names him in one clause about as long as a sentence.
- **His leash, paid and still slack.** It never had to be pulled. A two-hour Sunday window on a notice that is not on his card, a form with three columns and no fourth, a printed card the hall sends out four times a year, a gift of a second sheet nobody asks for, and a conversation that cost **nineteen days** of margin and told him nothing. **He has understood, and it is written in the book, that the whole of the slack the notice removed cost him nothing because the constraint was never the days: the constraint was the book.** He is not angry about it. He is a man who paid a fee rather than a price, which is the difference between a building charging you to keep what is yours and a building charging you to be allowed to do it, and he says so on the last page of Chapter 27.
- **Power:** Stage 2, Aftershock, **practised with a price he can quote before he pays.** One use in this batch, in the Question Hall, with a true answer about a governor that will hold and is the only part of the business nobody will ask about, costing a hundred and forty seconds entered as a *reading cost* with the reason written under it. **He knows the price now and the knowledge is worth nothing, because the problem is not a machine.** No Choice Trace, no Loom access, no new filed Reading, no certification of a toll, no licence offence, no suspension. **A private sense is still not a Reading, and Chapter 25 is the chapter that proves it, because his answer was right and there was no form under it.**
- **The ninth beat is not a stage and not part of this line.** Two hearings, in Chapters 1 and 3, and **in Chapter 15, on a tram at ten past five, he notices he was listening for it in the middle of the Quarter's ordinary perfect hour and it does not come.** It is a sound with a count in it that leans on a place he cannot name or locate: no picture, no word, no cost, no prophecy. He must never treat it as a free Aftershock. It is unexplained at the end of Chapter 20 and stays unexplained through Chapter 50.
- **Cost of the readings, in the order the page fixes them, and it escalates.** First, Chapter 5: the minute, with clean edges, and two incompatible accounts. Second, Chapter 9: two days of fine motor control in the right hand and the permanent ring. **Third, Chapter 19: thirty-eight minutes, and the permanent loss of the ability to judge a movement by ear** — he can still hear a watch at thirty feet in a still room and has tested it twice since the Tuesday; what he cannot any longer do is tell by the sound alone whether a movement is running true, which is the whole of a clockmaker's trade, and nothing had been strained, so it is not coming back — because he wanted to act and did not. **This is a precision loss and deliberately not a range loss, recorded so that Volume 08's long-range injury and Volume 14's clean single-machine hearing are both still available.**
- **His three rules, all on the page and all at the top of the grey ledger, and all three now tested.** First, Chapter 10: *nothing of mine is used again without its blind margin and its toll named in public, in a room, to somebody who can object.* Kept in Chapter 19; **did nothing.** Second, Chapter 17: *a toll is what happens when you ask something you cannot pay for. Ask one thing, about a named load, and know before you ask what it will cost.* **Working: in Chapter 25 he quotes the price before he pays it.** Third, Chapter 20: *no result of mine goes out of this room without a person named who can refuse it* — **the only one that has ever done anything, and it has twice done the opposite of what he wanted.** In Chapter 25 it stopped him with a true answer and no gain. In Chapter 27 he found its hole, on the page: a form is not a room, and a fault observed on a civic machine is a job, so filling in the W-4 put eleven years of the district's night work into an institution with nobody able to refuse it. In Chapter 30 he offered it to the district for nothing and was told the district had outgrown it, because nine people who can refuse is a room and a room has no address.
- **Flaw, in action rather than in summary:** he said *keep it* about the one book he could not replace; he did not tell his sister about the lamp; he let Hask's question about work off the board pass with two words unsaid; in Chapter 16 he decided not to put a document in front of the person it was about and gave himself a good reason on a stair; and in Chapter 24 he sat in a warm room and did not tell a master that he has been at the governor again, because he could not construct the sentence in which a man refuses a master and then adds *by the way*.
- **What the batch costs him that is not a number:** he handed eleven years of the Weirs' night work to a guild in the guild's own form with nobody able to refuse it, and he knows exactly what he did and why he did it, and the reason was that the alternative was a book in a shop with a lease. He fills a form of forty lines out of two thousand pages and the selection is a judgement nobody in the building will ever audit. And he is named in a footnote and is glad, and works out that this is the only place in the city where he has ever been useful, and that is not modesty.

## Mara Venn, 33 — investigator, Office of Public Reckoning

- **Wants:** the same, and now with a method she has admitted out loud to a corridor and is ashamed of — three years of a committee being bored by a bridge, and then a clerk asking what the form number on it is — **and has now said out loud in a room of a hundred and thirty people at the Cathedral, which is a bigger thing than she intended.** She chose the one ground in that room where a form is the thing being read, and she lost, and she said in advance that she would not be able to use it twice: *you cannot get a city to look at a man; you can get it to read a form, once, and then you have used the form up.*
- **Has:** a copybook's contents, a price schedule, a form number, a four-shilling debt to a woman who is never thanked, a requisition for the guild labour book in a tray for about fourteen months, **eleven identical four-line cards in a drawer**, a seven-day note with *no further correspondence on this matter* in the corner in small type, and one thing nobody has said out loud in a room yet: **a name on the GH-9 countersignature line**, whose first entry would be a woman of twenty-six in the storm month eighteen years ago.
- **Does not have:** any authority over any other office, and any safe way to read a machine. Her mother's name is still not spoken and must not be until a volume the outline names. She said *Mara* to Rook once without thinking and did not take it back.
- **State with Orrin:** professionally allied and privately uninformative, and in Chapter 16 they agree about a bridge bracket and disagree about a whole case and do not resolve it in a corridor. She gave him, in Chapter 19, a page with his own decision on it, witnessed, because she knew he would need it in four months when he decided a rule was a thing that was true in a pump house.
- **Her stake in the argument, unchanged and now sharper:** a name-and-duration column cannot be drawn beside a figure nobody has counted, and her office is ignored three times in a fortnight by people who are not being cruel.

## Teo Arden, 20 — unlicensed tuner, organizer, the Weirs

- **Alive, and no longer only a suspicion.** He sent eleven copies of the hall's labour book into nine buildings and it cost a tenant block of thirty-one households a Michaelmas rent rise, and he has not apologised to Orrin because Orrin did not send it. That is the working relationship: a disagreement about method with consequences on both sides.
- **Wants:** still a boardinghouse on the Kestrel side of Northglass with nine women on a stair a hall's form says were never there — **and the batch's answer is that the method does not travel, and that a new batch number would make the nine a new standing withdrawal at two shillings the head for a year.** He has not accepted that and he is not going to be told it twice.
- **State at the end of the batch:** the Loose Minutes have a method and no leader and a name, and they are not a syndicate. **He does the one thing nobody else in the district is able to do: in Chapter 25 he refuses Orrin permission to say a true thing in a room, on the correct ground, and he is right, and Orrin is grateful and does not say so.** He wins a fair copy of the boardinghouse line at a penny and finds it worse than nothing. He moves a motion in Chapter 30, is outvoted seven to three to one, says in advance that he will not be bound by it, and does not go behind Wenna Pruitt's back.
- **The disclosure argument is open and must stay open into batch 0004.** He says the Advertiser is the only door in the city that is not shut; she says it is the only door not shut *at you*. They agreed in Chapter 22 not to settle it and said they would not be the same arithmetic in a year.
- **Not yet:** a member of any committee, a man with a plan he trusts, or a person Orrin can rely on. He is not grateful and he is not recruited.

## Leda Vale, 27 — emergency nurse, Green Span

- **From method to consequence, and she is the one who says it.** In Chapter 15 she prices his rule. In Chapter 19 she tells him in a corridor that the rule failed afterwards, in a file, the next day. **In Chapter 26 she is the one who reads the blind margin aloud in a room that can write it down** — the Water Users' Committee of the pumping district, Monday the thirteenth, a member of the public, granted without objection, entered in the minute — because she works in a building where that sentence is the whole of her trade, and she tells the room what it means: *we are the cost, and the cost is not wrong, and the cost is not a lie, it is simply not in.* The committee does not re-open the schedule.
- **And then, in the rain, the hardest thing in the batch:** her ward is on the wrong side of the Chapter 25 bill for a decision taken in a room she was not in, the water is worth more than the bridge was and she has been arguing against that for four hours and has nothing, and **there is no answer.** She has looked. She is the one who has to say so, and she says it to a man with a bar in the street, and she tells him not to write it down as a lesson, and she says that what she wants is to have been in the street when it happened, which is the whole of what she has ever wanted from him.
- **What she has put on the page:** the four districts on the back of a ward roster, unsigned and undated, on the duty board in the nurses' room where families read. She is the one who gets asked about it and she has a shift pattern that lets her say *I am not on until Friday*.
- **The lamp, named at last:** she was nine, in the chapel school on Weir Lane, about sixty people, the bells stopped, she counted, her aunt came at four with a basket, their mother three years dead. **She does not know what their father did and has never once pretended.** She has told Orrin that his not-telling is not a kindness, that she would have come, and that she cannot hold a lamp for something she does not know is happening. This is the reason his third rule exists.
- **Not a reward, and she leaves no hospital.** Her ward still has a morning in which two medicines arrive four minutes apart, and a girl of nineteen on nights is doing the ward clock's work by hand and being paid for it.

## Perry Vance, 41 — dead, the end of Chapter 5, the night of the 17th

- Unchanged. Still not Teo, still not a martyr, still not explained. In batch 0002 he is named three times (Chapters 12, 16 and 20) and appears nowhere, which is the point: the batch's whole argument is that a man who is not in a document does not exist, and he does not, and Orrin carries him in a grey book that is not in any office.
- **His record, as it now stands:** a line in a copybook, eleven times, with no name on it; a batch of forty-one entries; four names on a works' return and five on a board, the fifth being a boy who cannot be entered on a form; a hole in a ward's forty minutes with his name not in it; and a rule of Leda's about wards.

## Sel Kade, 44 — pump warden, Lower Weirs

- **Still not told.** Her eighteen-year-old countersignature on batch 21/4 is in the Continuity Office file and in Orrin's hands and he has not put it in front of her. Mara named the reason as a good reason with a door in it. The tenant review of the March diversion is owed by about Chapter 46.
- **In the room twice in this batch and not on anybody's side.** In Chapter 22 she says a pump warden's business is the water and that everybody else is talking about money, and that a person who starts pretending to know is how you get a district hurt, and she is resented for it. In Chapter 25 she is not in the hall; her post is the reason the pumping ward has no reserve, and nobody in that room said so. In Chapter 30 she gives the hydraulic case for not taking a position — *a schedule is a machine, and a claimant is a thing a machine can be run without* — and then puts her own unreviewed March diversion on the table in her own words and refuses to be questioned about it in a room, and says that this is a dodge and is also correct.
- **Still public and still unreviewed:** the reserve moved in March to Carrow Street, seventy-one people, said out loud on a public street, and now said a second time by her, on the record, by her own motion. **The documented diversion with a schedule and a named beneficiary is batch 0004's work and it now has a woman on the record who has pre-empted it.** She is not recruited and she is not converted.

## Gerren Hask, 55 — master of the Clockwork Quarter guild hall

- **His crime is legible to the reader and invisible to every character, and batch 0002 did not change that by a word.** He asked for the work book, explained that the hall cannot take it and would resign before signing that, explained the other half more plainly, offered an optional interview that cannot be refused without giving a reason, and said the words *it is not a threat* in effect without ever using them.
- **What is new is the mechanism, not the crime:** from the renewal card onward, the only record of what a maintainer has found in any office in the city is one the hall writes, on form W-4, with three columns and none for *I am not sure*. The licence **expired on Sunday the 19th of the third month and was renewed in Chapter 27 without incident**, in a two-hour window he never mentions, endorsed in four seconds, with no letter, no threat and no inquiry. **The interview on Tuesday the 28th was forty minutes and cost four weeks of margin and produced no information at all.** He gave Orrin the second sheet nobody asks for, told him the counter is open on Sundays, and told him the escapement on his own bench wants oiling — kindly, in front of the only witness, to a man who can no longer hear it. He committed no impropriety: the notice that compressed the renewal is set by a forms printer, the hall's four-line card has been in a drawer since before Orrin was apprenticed, and a copyist made the Weir Two enclosure because she did not know what a station was. Hask is owed losing the right to certify silent shifts and publishing the labour ledger with a liability annex naming the night of the Long Nine as the night the hours stopped. **He is not destroyed; he becomes a repairer under public oversight.**
- **Never asked about the Long Nine on the page.** Della Marsh names the liability annex in Chapter 14 and refuses the figure, which is not Hask and does not touch him.

## Secondary people established on the page in batch 0002

- **Tobias Rill, 44,** Continuity Office inspector of hourlines. On the page, in person, and the most reasonable man in the book. He certifies that a line agrees with its own record and explains, without unkindness, why a district's own chalk board cannot be entered on a form, and why the fifth name cannot be entered at all. He signed the trestle's £2 14s 6d deferral two years ago and is not a villain for it. **In Chapter 26 he certifies the Ferrywright gate interlock within tolerance and adds four true lines to a public file — that a forty-one-minute release delay has not been reviewed by any office since the mechanism was installed, that the window is now shorter by design, and that the Office has no function in a pumping schedule — and Orrin does not know until the next day that it is there.** It is the only item in the Chapter 26 bill that anybody put in writing in a form belonging to an office. He reads the Outer Sluice minutes in Volume 06 and his inspection history is public record in Volume 14.
- **Della Marsh, 61,** guild hall secretary, thirty-one years. The person a form number comes out of, for four shillings in coin and no receipt, on a rule of her own, a week after the asking. Frightened of an inquiry, not corrupt, loyal to nobody, and never once thanked for anything. **On the page twice more in batch 0003, on the licensing floor: she tells him what the fifth standing notice actually does to him, that W-4 is two pages and the issue changes without notice because there is nowhere to put a letter, and that a man can walk in any day and ask the counter for a blank and the hall cannot write a rule to stop it; and then she explains that the Weir Two enclosure was not requested by anybody, that nobody in that hall has ever heard of the Lower Weirs pumping district, and that a copyist did what she was taught.** **She is never thanked on the page. In Chapter 28 the not-thanking is the exchange: he starts a sentence, and she says *Mr Vale*, and he says it is a wet afternoon, and she says it is, and that is the whole of thirty-one years.**
- **Hester Lomas, 44,** hall copyist, nineteen years in the back room, who signed a true copy of the Weir Two line eleven times without knowing what a standby was, made the eleven copies over three nights, lost her place, and put her own name to the paper because one copy went to the man who sells the Advertiser's front page. She does not recur after this volume.
- **Ellen Reeve, 44,** hospital engineer at Green Span, twenty-two years. Packed the failing gland wrong in August, said so before letting Orrin touch the pump, and packed it properly afterwards with the spindle drawn and reseated, which is what she asked to have on the record and which buys a fortnight. She does not appear again after Chapter 19. Competent, and unable to certify a district toll either.
- **Hanna Stroud, 54,** landlord of nine Weirs blocks. Pay her own renters on the first, has never been two months late, took a rise off one block on the strength of the guild's own labour book, and got it back with her own twelve years of rent book and the district returns printed in the minutes. The insurer's letter says the returns are insufficient. Reasonable, and still reasonable.
- **Ivo Pell, 36,** veteran operator. On the page twice and contemptuous both times, and right both times: a bench man's ear is not an instrument, a man who practises on a dead pump is behind his trade, and a man who practises on a live one is a public hazard. He offers no lesson, takes no interest, and says *don't* on his way out. He is not a mentor here and Volume 02 must not find one retroactively.
- **Councilor Imani Rook, 40,** chair of the public-health committee. Filer of her own demand on arithmetic, in her own name, three chapters after blocking one, and she says in the chamber that she was out-argued and not persuaded. She pays for the record out of her own ward's clerical allocation at four pounds ten and she keeps the name-and-duration clause **out**, because she does not know the size of the population the column would describe. **Not softened, not converted, an obstacle and not a villain.** Appears in three more volumes after this one, not thirteen.
- **Alma Festinger, 56.** Not on the page in batch 0003 and **not to be asked again anywhere in Volume 01.** The second asking is Volume 06, in a gallery, and the reader should recognise her face. The affected-district station in Chapter 25 is filled by a **different** Green Span rota resident, unnamed on the page, and the near-collision is not a reveal and must not be treated as one.
- **Ostrand was on the page in Chapter 25**, at the middle station of the rail with the ledger the size of a paving stone, copying notices and not wishing to be asked. **Ostrow was not on the page in this batch** and must not be promoted into the Question Hall.
