# Chapter 108 — Forty Minutes

On Monday the seventh of the second month Leda Vale worked a double and did not go home between, because the ward was short and the free master needed watching and the line clocks needed writing down every four hours whether or not anybody read the card.

She had done the round twice. Eight of the eleven got what they were due on the free master, at the right minute, and were written beside the line clock as being roughly two hundred minutes out, which is a true statement about a number and a useless one about a stomach. The ninth had waited fifty-one minutes for the second dose because the free master's escapement had a dirty pallet and would not let go, and the fifty-one minutes were on the card in Cobb's hand and Cobb had written them without softening them, which was the practice.

The ninth was a man of about seventy who had come in with a hip and who was cheerful about it in the way that people in that ward are cheerful about a hip, and who had said the thing twice that patients in that ward say, which is that he could not tell whether it was the pain or the waiting, and Leda had written *pain* and *waiting* in two separate columns, and the fact that there were two columns was a decision somebody had made in a room in December, and the room in December had not met this man.

Orrin came at eight in the morning with bread and cheese in paper and sat in the nurses' room while she ate it standing up.

"You look worse than the clocks," he said.

"I am better regulated," she said. "Forty minutes of slack across eleven patients and three staff, on the best night of the year. Six hours is nine times forty minutes. You wrote that on a coping stone once. I say it in a room now."

"You say it in a room because a coping stone is free and a room is not."

"Yes. And because a room can be argued with." She swallowed and looked at the card on the board across the corridor. "That is the whole of what a room is for. A stone cannot be argued with and neither can a column."

The card carried the day and the number and four names, one of them E. Cobb, nineteen, paid at the ward rate, who had asked twice to be named and had not been taken off. The worst single interval on the sister's own card was nine minutes twenty seconds, total six hours twenty-four minutes, and the fourth line was hers. Orrin read it and did not copy it, because a ward's figures were the ward's and the Office's figure was nowhere in the row book and neither belonged in his, and a number a man took off a ward board and wrote in a shop would arrive back on that board in somebody else's hand inside a week and be worth nothing.

A porter came up at half past nine with a token in a twist of paper and a note on it: *Found in laundry, G, 100. Is it ours?* Leda held it to the light, weighed it in her hand, and gave it back.

"It weighs what it weighs," she said. "What it is for is not in any form. Take it to the assay if you want to know what it is made of. If you want to know what it is for, ask the woman who lost four months of nights."

"He'll want to know," the porter said. "He's been carrying it two days and his wife says it's the ward's and I say it isn't and we've not asked anybody."

"Will you have it weighed?" Orrin said.

Both of them looked at him. Leda said no before he had finished the question.

"And why not?" he said.

"Because a real one has to be spent and a false one has to be thrown away, and both of those are decisions, and I have eleven patients and one of them has already had fifty-one minutes out of her afternoon and I am not adding a brass decision to a floor that is already short." She put the twist back in the porter's hand. "Tell him the ward did not have it and the ward does not want it, and tell him the assay office on Weigh Lane will tell him what it is for nothing, four minutes, and that he should ask his wife which of the two things he actually wants to know."

"He asked me that already," the porter said. "She wants to know if it was hers. It isn't hers. It came out of a coat that went to the wash on Thursday and there is a laundry on Bellweather with a hundred coats in it on a Thursday and that is the whole of what I know."

"Then it is hers until somebody proves otherwise," Leda said, "and nobody is going to prove it, because the only proof is four minutes on a beam and four minutes on a beam tells you what it is and not where it was. I want him to hear that from a nurse and not from his wife, and I want him to hear it on a day when he is not holding it."

The porter went down with it, and the ward went on, and the free master ticked over on the landing where four people could hear it and nobody could see it.

They sat quiet a while. The ward beyond the door breathed and ticked, the free master clean, the line clocks stepping fifty seconds a night whether or not anybody watched.

"Do you go home tonight?" he said.

"No."

"That is four days."

"It is four days and the seventh of the month and I have done four days before." She did not look at the card, and Orrin understood that she could have said the figure without looking at it, the way a man can say the price of a clock he has mended a hundred times. "The whole of the argument I have with Nella is about what a hundred minutes is worth, and the whole of the argument I have with myself is about what six hours is worth, and I have not told her about the second one, and she has not told me about hers."

"Will you spend it?" Orrin said, meaning the one on the counter, meaning all of them.

"No," Leda said. "Nella wants to and I disagree and neither of us has been asked to choose, and we are not going to be. My reason is different from hers and both are true. She wants medicine on time. I want a number said out loud before it is spent, because the day I stop saying it is the day it becomes ours. We are the only people in this building who can be asked, and the second we are also the only people who can spend, we stop being asked."

"Do you think that is what the market is?"

"I think the market found the same thing by accident and charges four and six for it," Leda said.

Then she sat down on the end of the bed, which she had not done all night, and looked at him, and he understood that she had been waiting for three days to do it.

"Do you forgive me?" he said. It was the first time he had asked it in plain words in a year.

She did not look away and she did not soften it, and that was the third thing about her that had been true since they were children and had never once been said out loud. "For what? For the forty-one heads at two shillings? For the three hundred and sixty minutes? For not telling me about the lamp when I was nine?"

"For making you hold the cost of every number I put where people could see it."

"That is the correct sentence and it took you a year to arrive at it." She turned the cup over in her hands. "I will tell you what it actually cost, since you have asked. In the autumn of the year you were in the glass case, I sat in a room with two matrons and a man from the Directorate and I said a number I could not defend, and afterwards I had to write down on a piece of paper that I had said it, and I had to give the paper to Mother, who reads everything, and Mother said, *you'll be alright, you were tired*, and I have not forgiven Mother for that, and you are not in that at all."

"You put the tank down as a tank."

"I put the tank down as a tank, and I had eleven people watch me do it, and I had already had the six hours." She set the cup down. "That is what I hold. Not the lamp. You were fourteen and I was nine and Mother had been dead three years, and we were in different buildings on the worst night of this city's life, and there was nothing in the world either of us could have done about a lamp. Anybody who tells you otherwise is selling us a childhood we did not have so that we can be forgiven cheaply."

He put his hands flat on his knees, because there was nothing to do with them.

"There is a lamp," he said. "I have never once been able to put it in a sentence in a room, and you have never once asked me for it, and I have spent a year deciding that the not-asking is the thing I have to answer."

"I have not asked you about the lamp because I know what the lamp is," Leda said. "It is the one fact you had that nobody had asked you for, and you did not have it for me, and you have never once tried to give it to me, and I would have taken it if you had, and that is the reason it has never come up between us and not the reason I am angry. I am not angry about the lamp. I have been angry about the number on the coping stone since October and you know that too, and today you finally said it out loud in a room instead of on a stone, and that is not the same as being forgiven, and it is not nothing."

She put the cup down and looked at the card on the board across the corridor, and then she said the thing she had been building the whole conversation towards, and she said it in the flat voice she used for handing over a drug chart.

"I have not forgiven anything because nothing has been asked of me. You told me the tank was a tank and named no one, and that was right. You told Mara the tank was fourteen hours with a sheet of paper in a coat, and that was right. Tell me the next one before you spend it and I will think about forgiveness when I have time, which is never."

He nodded, because that was an answer and not a mood, and he took it as one, and did not thank her, because she had not given him anything and had been careful to make sure of it.

He mended the ward's bracket clock in the afternoon, cleaned the movement, set the beat, refused to certify, and wrote the work in the ward's book with the fault observed, the work performed, and the recommendation, and left the recommendation column short because there was no column for *I am not sure* and never would be.

The fault was a worn click spring and a bushing he did not have in his roll, and he wrote *replacement required, part held at bench, fitted on receipt* and the date, which is the only kind of note he knew how to leave behind him that somebody else can act on in eleven days.

Cobb read the entry upside down from the end of the bed, which is the way a night sister reads things at two in the morning, and said, "That is the first time anybody has written a next step in that book."

"There is a next step in everything," Orrin said. "Nobody writes it down because writing it down makes a person responsible for it, and the recommendation column with nothing in it is the same column with a different name on it."

"Put the bushing in tomorrow," Cobb said, "and write *fitted, held* on it, and then there is a line on this book that begins and ends in a week. That is worth more to me than the recommendation was."

Nobody thanked him for the bread, for the beat, for the refusal, or for the true answer about the token, and he did not look for it, and she went back onto the floor at four o'clock and he stayed until the change of shift and then went down the stairs and out into the second of the month going dark at half past four.
He did not go home either. He sat in the shop with the letter on the shelf and the narrow book open and wrote down the one thing the three days had actually settled, which was not an answer to anything: that a man with no licence could still leave a next step in a ward's book, and that a ward would act on it, and that this was the whole of what was available to him in February and it was not nothing, and it was not enough, and both of those were true at the same time on the same page.

On Tuesday the eighth he beat the clock again, found it holding, and fitted the new bushing, and wrote *fitted, held* on the line where Cobb had asked for it. On Wednesday the ninth he wrote the three days in the grey ledger: not a victory, not a settlement, a man and a woman in a room with bread and a clock, and hours belonging to named people, and a number said out loud.

*Ward slack forty minutes. Token found in laundry, G100, not spent, not weighed. L. disagrees with N., neither chooses. Nothing certified. Nobody thanked.*
