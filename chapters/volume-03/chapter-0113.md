# Chapter 113 — Three Clocks and a Hole

On Saturday the twenty-sixth of the second month Orrin walked Hobden Street because a man had chalked a card in a chemist's window and Orrin had seen it from the other side of the road and had come back across.

The card said, in paint pencil, in a hand that had been taught lettering and did not have much of it left: *THREE CLOCKS. ONE QUESTION WRITTEN BEFORE THE HOUR. CRANK AT THE BACK. IN AT SEVEN.*

The chemist had let him up because Orrin said he was a clockmaker and the chemist said the man upstairs had asked for exactly that word on the Thursday and had been refused by three others. The stair was a wooden one over the shop and it went to a door that had been painted eleven times, and behind the door was a room about eleven feet by sixteen with a slope in the floor of about two inches from the wall side to the street side, which is a floor that has been put in by a man who was not a builder and had wanted the noise to go away from the people below.

There were three clocks on the wall, all of them second-hand, and Abel Nye was standing in front of the middle one with his back to the door.

He was about forty-six, in a leather apron with a burn on it the shape of a coin, and his hands were the hands of a man who has spent thirty years making other people's weight correct. He had a workshop two doors down with one apprentice in it and the workshop let to him on a lease and the back room let to him by the chemist for four shillings a week, and he said both of those numbers before Orrin had asked for either.

"Forty-six," he said. "Mr Nye. I make scales. Beam balances, counter scales, the sort that a shop uses to be sure it has not been robbed. Eleven of them out at the moment, mostly for grocers, two for a seed merchant, and I have got a hundredweight of dead brass in the workshop that I have not been able to sell since the ration book came off."

He said it the way a man says a thing that is true and that he has said before, and then he turned round and shut the door, which he explained afterwards was not about the door.

"Right. Before you look at anything. The rules of the room are on that card and the card was written on the Saturday and the question was written on the Saturday and the names were written on the Saturday, and I am not changing the question now, and if you want to change it you can come back next week, and I would rather you did."

The card was pinned to the door and it was longer than the one in the window. It gave a date, the twenty-eighth of the second month, seven in the morning, the question, five names, and then four lines underneath.

*The question is written before the hour and is not changed at the hour. Each person writes their own figure and their own name and hands it to nobody. Where two figures differ the larger difference is the answer and it is written down. Nothing here is a certification. Nothing here stops anything. Nothing here can be used in any office in this city. No person is thanked.*

"You wrote that in four lines."

"I wrote that in four lines in about ten minutes and I have had it in that window since Thursday," said Nye, "and in the eleven days before Thursday three people asked me what the room was for and I told them all three of them that it was for finding out how late a bell is."

The question was written out in full in his own hand and it was the question Orrin had expected and it was better than he had expected.

*If the works' bell at the junction of Bellweather Road and Weir Lane is moved off the works' own line onto the ward's line, how many seconds is it late at the top of Bellweather Road at seven in the morning?*

"That's a proper question," Orrin said. "That is a thing somebody could physically do on a Tuesday and a thing that could be measured, and it names where and it names when and it names what we want to know. Do you know how many of the questions people bring to a room like this are not that?"

"I know what mine is," said Nye. "Mine is a bell. Because my apprentice's mother lives at number nine and the bell at the end of her street goes at ten past six and she is out at six and she has told me twice, and I have been putting that number next to the hospital number and the Branch number and the figures on the arcade board, and there is no way on this earth to get from one of those to the other, and I am a man who makes scales."

He said it without any weight on it at all.

"And the room is called a reading room," he said. "I want you to know that I know that is not the word yet. The thing is not built yet. There is no such thing licensed in this city or unlicensed either, because there has never been one of either, and I read a notice about it in the Advertiser in January. But a room where a person can be told a figure about a thing they are affected by, in plain words, with their own name on the paper, and where anybody in it can stop it — that is what a reading room is going to mean when it means anything, and I have put the words up first because the alternative is that somebody puts them up for something else first."

He was not answered, and nobody in the room corrected him, and Orrin did not, and the reason he did not was that he had asked Anja Voss by post seven days earlier whether a person in a building may refuse a door, and had been refused one of his three questions in one sentence, and that a sentence is not a rule and a rule is what a man in a shop would need. He had no standing whatever to correct a man about a room in Hobden Street. His licence was suspended and expired in twenty-one days.

"There is no spindle in it," he said instead. "There is no instrument in that room."

"There's a crank," said Nye.

"Show me the crank."

It was at the back of the room on a board, and it worked, and it was good. Nye had turned an empty milk can upside down over a frame with a float in it and a wire off the float to a pen, and the crank turned the can, and the float rose, and the pen drew a line on a card, and the card said how much reserve the room had spent, and it took four turns to put a hundred minutes on the card, and it took a hundred and twenty nights at fifty seconds a night to put a hundred minutes on the card.

He had built it because he had a tray of six brass tokens in a drawer at home that had come out of a clock at a laundry in 1946 and had been in a drawer since, and he wanted to know what one of them was worth on paper, and he had worked it out from a card the Weigh Lane counter had given him, and he had not bought one to spend, because he did not know what spending one was.

"It is not connected to anything," Orrin said.

"No."

"It is connected to this room, and this room is connected to nothing, and so the figure on the card is what this room spent, and this room spent it out of nothing."

"That is exactly right," said Nye, "and I have never had anybody say it to me before, and I have said it to four people and three of them told me I was building a machine for nothing."

The hole in the window was not the original pane. Nye had taken the glass out of the top half of the sash in the middle of January, and put back a pane with a hole in it about the size of a saucer, and boarded the rest of the opening over, so that the room had one circular hole in its street wall at the height of a seated man's eye.

"It was cracked and I could not get a glazier," he said. "I did not put it there to be tidy. I put it there because a room you cannot hear out of is a room nobody can stop, and a room nobody can stop is not a thing anybody is allowed to have. That hole is the only reason my apprentice's mother could come up the stairs last week and say, from the doorway, that my card said four minutes and the bell says four, and can I come in and say so, and I could say yes in front of her."

"A circular hole in a window is a hole in a window."

"It is a hole in a window that I cut out with a trowel in eleven minutes," Nye said, "and I would take the trowel out again for anybody who wanted a smaller one, and the whole of what I have learned in this room is that the smaller the hole the better the room is, and I have the opposite of what everybody else in this city wants, and I want the hole to get smaller, and I will not have it smaller than a saucer because after that you cannot get your hand through it and a hand in the hole is the only thing in this room that has ever stopped anything."

On Sunday the twenty-seventh he did not go up the wooden stair, because it was a Sunday and the chemist was under the stairs and the market at the Weigh Lane end was shut and the arcades were shut, and there was nothing on that street on a Sunday that was not a shut door, and he had written the run out in the narrow book the night before and the three dates agreed.

On the Monday the twenty-eighth he went at half past five and stood in the room with five people in it before it was light, and the card on the door was the card from Saturday with nothing added to it, and he read it again in the doorway and then went in.

Abel Nye had the three clocks: a long-case with a seconds pendulum, bought for eleven pounds out of a house in Kestrel side; a bracket clock off a laundry, bracket and all, with the laundry's number still painted on the back plate; and a drum-head regulator, the sort that goes in a boiler house, off a boiler house in Cardew Street that had closed in 1949. His apprentice was a boy of nineteen called Ferris, who wound them.

Wilfred Tace, fifty-five, had delivered milk in Hobden Street and Bellweather Road for nineteen years and was at the corner at ten to seven with a board and a piece of chalk. Miriam Coss was at the top of Bellweather Road with a card and a pencil. That was the five.

"It is your room," Orrin said. "Say what we do."

"We write our own figures and hand them to nobody," said Nye, "and where two differ the larger difference is the answer. That is on the card. Tace rings nothing, he only writes the minute he hears it, because the man at the junction starts it and not Tace. Mrs Coss writes the minute it reaches her, because it is her street and it is her bell that is being asked about. Mr Vale and I each read all three clocks against the Quarter public clock at the corner, which is the only one in this city that anybody can be trusted with, and we write three figures. Ferris winds at twenty to seven and goes to bed."

"And if the figures differ?"

"Then we write that they differ," said Nye, "and that is the finding, and the card says the finding is the answer."

The question was read aloud. Then the minute was written down and the hour was written down and the date was written down, and then Tace went out into the dark with his board.

They read the three clocks at three minutes past seven and wrote them down separately and did not speak.

At 7:11 they read them again.

Then Tace's board came back, and it said 6:59, in his own hand, in chalk, and underneath it in the same chalk: *started on the minute.*

Then Miriam Coss's card came down the road with her brother, because she would not leave a card in a house with a child asleep in the next room, and it said 7:03, and beneath it she had written *and it is a different sound, it is not the works' bell, it is the ward's, and it goes past the top instead of turning at the corner.*

Four minutes and eleven seconds.

Nye read it twice and then went and looked at his own three clocks and said, "Well," and then said, "Now the other thing."

He took the cards off the wall and read the two separations out loud, and he had written them on the Saturday, and he read the Saturday's and then the Monday's.

"Seven minutes and two seconds, and nineteen minutes and forty-one seconds, on Saturday afternoon," he said. "Seven minutes and fourteen seconds, and nineteen minutes and twenty-four seconds, on Monday morning. Twelve seconds one way and seventeen seconds the other, in a day and a night, and nobody has touched them, and they are on my wall."

"They are not regulated," Orrin said.

"They are not regulated to anything," said Nye. "I have never compared one of them to the district reference in its life, and neither has the man who sold me them, and neither has anybody in this street, and that is why they are moving, and I know they are moving and I wrote it on the card and I did not write it on the card because I knew it would stop anybody coming."

He said that last part without any drama in it at all, and then he looked at the board on which the crank had drawn its line, and then he said the thing that made the room, in the end, worth having.

"You can buy a hundred minutes for four and six in the arcade on a Tuesday," he said, "and I have a card here that says the same hundred minutes and I can draw it with a crank in four turns, and neither of those two things can tell this street how late its own bell is. And a hundred minutes is six thousand seconds and the bell is four minutes and eleven seconds, which is two hundred and fifty-one of them, and there is a counter in this city that would sell you one twenty-fourth of a token and there is no shop in this city that will sell you a twenty-fourth of one, and that is the whole of the problem in a single sum, and I did it in my head in November and I have been carrying six brass in a drawer in a dead flat since the winter because I did not know what to do with the two numbers being next to each other."

The boy called Ferris said, from the doorway, that his mother's bell went at ten past six and not before, and that the room had never once told her anything, and that he was sorry and did not mean to be.

"You are not sorry," said Nye, "you are eighteen and you have been awake since four. Put the kettle on."

Orrin went out on to the stair at about ten past eight and did the thing that would have taken a man in this city four minutes and a shilling, and it took him four minutes and cost him nothing, because there is one person in this city who is allowed to be his own standard and that person is him.

He set his own watch by the Quarter public clock, walked to the corner, stood where Tace had stood with his back to the wall, and watched the man at the junction start the bell, and then walked up Bellweather Road to number nine at a steady pace and looked at his watch when the sound came past the top.

Four minutes and nine seconds.

He went back down and did it again, because a man who gets one figure and writes it down is a man who has been in a warm room all his life. It was four minutes and ten seconds.

So: the room had four minutes and eleven seconds, and two men with a watch and a wall had four minutes and nine and four minutes and ten, and the room was out by between one and two seconds, and it had cost eleven pounds of second-hand clocks, a hole in a window, six weeks of a man's evenings, and a piece of type he had cut himself, and it was out by one or two seconds, and the one and two seconds were not in the clocks.

"Two seconds," said Nye, when he was told. He was not disappointed, and he was not triumphant, and he said the next thing himself before Orrin could. "Two seconds is the price of a room that anybody can walk into. If I had put the three clocks on the district reference in January and kept them on it every month, the price would be about four shillings a night of my time and I would not have a room at all, I would have a certificate with a hole in it, and there is no form in this city for the first thing and there never will be, and that is not a complaint, it is the trade."

On the Tuesday the first of the third month Orrin walked down to nine Bellweather Road at about half past seven in the evening and went up four floors past a man playing with a knife and put a card on the table of a kitchen nine feet by eleven with a gas ring and a list on a nail.

The card had the date on it, the hour, five names, and a figure.

*If the works' bell at the junction of Bellweather Road and Weir Lane is moved off the works' own line onto the ward's line, how many seconds is it late at the top of Bellweather Road at seven in the morning. Answer, twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth second, by five persons, each writing his own figure: four minutes eleven seconds. Two other figures taken by a stopwatch by one person on the morning of the first of the third: four minutes nine seconds and four minutes ten seconds. The room was out by one second or two. Nothing here is a certification and nothing here stops anything and no person is thanked.*

Miriam Coss read it twice and then looked at the brass token, which was where it had been on the twenty-sixth of the first month and where it was still, worn bright on one edge, on a folded piece of Hale's priced paper.

"Six weeks," she said.

"Six weeks and a day. You bought it on the seventeenth of the first and I have not touched it."

"Six weeks and a day," she said, "and I have carried it up and down those stairs every day and down to the shops and up again and I have not spent it, and you wrote me a refusal in January that I have read about nine times and I would like to say something about it and I have not had it ready in six weeks and a day."

"Say it."

"Four minutes and eleven seconds is not a hundred minutes," she said. "Four minutes and eleven seconds is four minutes of my husband's bell, and it has been four minutes for years, and nobody came, and a hundred minutes is a hundred and twenty nights, and I have been sitting in this room for seven weeks trying to work out which of those two things my four and six was for and nobody in this city is going to tell me, including you, and I want to say that I know that, and that I still have not spent it, and that it is not going to spoil."

"Nobody is going to tell you," Orrin said, "because there is no instrument in this city that can say, and I have looked, and the reason there is no instrument is a hole in a form about a hundred and forty years old, and the hole is the whole of the difference between the bell and the nights, and the bell is a wire and a hammer and a man at a corner and the nights are not."

"Then the bell is the smaller thing."

"The bell is the smaller thing and the smaller thing is the one that is actually happening," he said. "That is not a comforting sentence and I have not got a better one."

Coming down Bellweather Road on the Tuesday he had come within a sight of the Weigh Lane end of Pindar Row, which was open, and had counted the tray outside number fourteen, and there were eleven brass out on it and a piece of card on the tray-edge with the price, and the rate card was fresh, and the number of people standing at the end of the row reading a board in chalk was four times what it had been a month ago.

Nobody in that row thanked him and he did not go in. He wrote the whole of the twenty-eighth in the grey ledger and the whole of the first of the third, and the rule at the bottom of the room's card went into his own book on its own, in his own hand, because a rule written by a man who makes scales and has never been in a hall is worth more than one written by a man who is standing in one.

*Room over a chemist's in Hobden Street. Three clocks, one question written before the hour, five names, no instrument in it, no spindle, nothing admissible, nothing certified, nobody thanked. The crank works and is connected to nothing. The hole in the window is the best thing in the room. Separations moved twelve seconds one way and seventeen the other in a day, because no clock on that wall has ever been compared with the district reference, and that is the arithmetic of the defect and not a fault of the man. Room's answer four minutes eleven seconds. My two answers four minutes nine and four minutes ten. The words are in a window in a city that has not built the thing yet.*
