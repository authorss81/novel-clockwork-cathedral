# Chapter 108 — Forty Minutes

On Tuesday the seventh of the second month Leda Vale worked a double and did not go home between, because the ward was short and the free master needed watching and the line clocks needed writing down every four hours whether or not anybody read the card.

Orrin came at eight in the morning with bread and cheese in paper and sat in the nurses' room while she ate it standing up.

"You look worse than the clocks," he said.

"I am better regulated," she said. "Forty minutes of slack across eleven patients and three staff, on the best night of the year. Six hours is nine times forty minutes. You wrote that on a coping stone once. I say it in a room now."

The card on the board carried the day and the number and four names, one of them E. Cobb, nineteen, paid at the ward rate, who had asked twice to be named and had not been taken off. The worst single interval on the sister's own card was nine minutes twenty seconds, total six hours twenty-four minutes, and the fourth line was hers. Orrin read it and did not copy it, because a ward's figures were the ward's and the Office's figure was nowhere in the row book and neither belonged in his.

A porter brought up a token in a twist with a note: *Found in laundry, G, 100. Is it ours?* Leda held it to the light, weighed it in her hand, gave it back.

"It weighs what it weighs," she said. "What it is for is not in any form. Take it to the assay if you want to know what it is made of. If you want to know what it is for, ask the woman who lost four months of nights."

"Will you spend it?" Orrin said.

"No," Leda said. "Nella wants to and I disagree and neither of us has been asked to choose, and we are not going to be. My reason is different from hers and both are true. She wants medicine on time. I want a number said out loud before it is spent, because the day I stop saying it is the day it becomes ours."

They sat quiet a while. The ward beyond the door breathed and ticked, the free master clean, the line clocks stepping fifty seconds a night whether or not anybody watched.

"Do you forgive me?" Orrin said. It was the first time he had asked it in plain words in a year.

Leda finished the cheese and folded the paper. "For what? For the forty-one heads at two shillings? For the three hundred and sixty minutes? For not telling me about the lamp when I was nine?"

"For making you hold the cost of every number I put where people could see it."

She looked at him steadily. "I have not forgiven anything because nothing has been asked of me. You told me the tank was a tank and named no one, and that was right. You told Mara the tank was fourteen hours with a sheet of paper in a coat, and that was right. Tell me the next one before you spend it and I will think about forgiveness when I have time, which is never."

He nodded, because that was an answer and not a mood, and the chapter's change was that he accepted it as one.

He mended the ward's bracket clock in the afternoon, cleaned the movement, set the beat, refused to certify, and wrote the work in the ward's book with the fault observed, the work performed, and the recommendation, and left the recommendation column short because there was no column for *I am not sure* and never would be.

Nobody thanked him for the bread, for the beat, for the refusal, or for the true answer about the token, and he did not look for it.

On Wednesday the eighth and Thursday the ninth he beat the clock again, found it holding, and wrote the two days in the grey ledger: not a victory, not a settlement, a man and a woman in a room with bread and a clock, and hours belonging to named people, and a number said out loud.

*Ward slack forty minutes. Token found in laundry, G100, not spent. L. disagrees with N., neither chooses. Nothing certified. Nobody thanked.*
