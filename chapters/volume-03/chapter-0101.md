# Chapter 101 — A Hundred Minutes of Brass

On Friday the thirteenth of the first month Orrin Vale opened the shop at eight and swept the step before he touched anything, because the step was the one part of Vale's Bench he could put right without asking anybody, and the bench inside was already set for a week's paid work and a letter he had not answered.

The letter was on the shelf behind the bench, under the narrow book. Four lines on the Cathedral's own paper, dated the eleventh, signed Anja Voss. He had read it on the twelfth in the pumping ward's outer room with Bevin Sarr in the room and had not read it since. He knew the shape of it by heart and that was worse than not knowing. A door was not a form. He had spent two volumes learning the difference between a thing that could be refused and a thing that could not, and now a woman who ran the building that had taught him had written the difference down in one sentence and asked him to come on a Tuesday.

He put the kettle on and left the letter where it was.

The grey ledger was open at the week. He had written the week's money in on the Thursday night in pencil before ink, the way he did everything now since the forty-one seconds. Eighteen shillings of bench work booked, a school clock and two watches, and three weeks of electricity owing, and the hall's out-of-shop rate of four shillings and sixpence an hour written in the margin because that was the rate everything else in the city was about to be measured against and he wanted it where he could see it.

The bell over the door went at half past nine.

The customer was a printer's devil of about seventeen, with ink under his nails and a parcel wrapped in proof paper, and he put the parcel on the counter like a man delivering something he had been told not to drop.

"Mr Vale? Mr Coss sent it. Said you'd know what it is and not to let his men fiddle with it."

Orrin unwrapped it. Inside was a bracket clock of about eleven inches, japanned case, white dial, the kind of office clock that gets knocked off a shelf once and never tells the time again. And beside the clock in the paper, loose, a small brass disc with a hole in it.

He picked the disc up before he touched the clock. It was about twenty-two millimetres across and three thick, with a raised rim, stamped on the head with a Q and the figure 100, and on the rim with a year and a die number so small he had to hold it to the window. It was cut clean. It weighed in the hand like a half-crown and sat warm from his own fingers in about four seconds.

"What did Coss say about this?" he said.

"Said it was in the clock. Rattled. One of the men found it when they moved the office. Said it's money."

"It isn't money," Orrin said. "It's a token."

The boy waited. Orrin set the disc down on a piece of white paper and did not pick up a tool yet, because a customer who thinks a thing is money will watch what you do with it, and a customer who has been told not to let men fiddle will count your movements.

"A token is cut from the surplus plate of a public clock that held more surplus than it needed at a return," he said. "It can be spent in the clock it was cut from, to hold a wheel off a dead escapement for a stated time. That is what the hall's rule was written for. The whole of what the law has ever said about a token is what it is made of."

The boy frowned. "So what does a hundred mean?"

"A hundred minutes."

"That's four shillings and sixpence worth?"

Orrin looked up. "Who told you that?"

"Man in the arcade. Said he'd give four and six for any with a Q on."

Pindar Row was off Weigh Lane, forty-one pitches under a glass roof, and Orrin knew it the way a Quarter man knows every place that sells what he mends. He had walked through it on Tuesdays for eleven years without stopping at the six pitches that had begun trading in tokens. He had never held one over his own counter before.

He turned to the clock. The back came off with two screws. Inside, the movement was sound and dirty, the crutch bent about a sixteenth where it had taken the knock, the pendulum suspension kinked. Ordinary work. He could see the whole order of it and then, halfway through seeing it, lost the thread of which screw came out first, and stood with the screwdriver in his hand and no sequence in his head.

He put the driver down, took the narrow book from the shelf, and wrote in pencil before he began anything: *back, two screws in tin; crutch out; suspension off; clean; bush centre; straighten crutch; hang; beat.*

The boy watched. "You always write it down?"

"Since September," Orrin said. "Forty-one seconds. I lose a sequence and I don't know I've lost it. The book is the sequence now."

He did the work in the order he had written. The crutch came out, the suspension came off, he cleaned the plates, bushed the centre wheel, straightened the crutch against the bench anvil and hung the movement on the test stand. It beat clean in four minutes. He set it to the Quarter's public clock through the window and wrote the rate on a beat-card.

That was the moment the third correction offered itself, the way it had five times since the summer. The dial feet were loose, and the quick way was to lift the dial on its pins with a bar and ease it true by eye, eleven minutes of hand work saved, no receipt and no form. He had done it twice in his life and both were in the hall's W-4 now. He could feel his hands wanting the bar.

He did not take it. Not because a clock made right stops being a public instrument a whole street can compare itself by — that had been December's reason, on Sluice Row, and December was done. This was a different reason and he said it out loud because the boy was there and somebody ought to hear it said once in a shop.

"If I lift that dial by hand I will have mended the clock and left no way for Coss to know whether I mended it or moved it," he said. "The book behind that counter says who pays for the record. This shop's book says who did the work. A hand correction is a record with nobody in it, and I have got two of those for my life and I am not having a third in a week with a letter on my shelf I haven't answered."

He pinned the dial properly, screwed it down, cased the movement and wrote the invoice. Three hours at four shillings and sixpence is thirteen shillings and sixpence, materials one shilling, total fourteen shillings and sixpence. The addition was exact and he wrote it twice.

"And the brass?" the boy said.

Orrin wrapped the token back in the proof paper separately from the clock. "It goes back to Coss in paper, not in the movement. Tell him a token in a clock he does not own is a hundred minutes he cannot spend without putting them into somebody else's margin. A hundred minutes is a hundred and twenty nights at fifty seconds a night, which is four months. Tell him that is arithmetic and not advice."

The boy put the parcel under his arm. He did not say thank you and Orrin did not look for it. Nobody had been thanked in that shop since the autumn, and the chapter of that was not courtesy but method: a man who is thanked for carrying a thing stops writing down that he carried it.

After the boy had gone Orrin stood at the bench with the narrow book open and added one line under the order of operations, in ink this time.

*Token weighed by eye only. Not assayed. Returned loose. Count of my own hand corrections is two.*

He closed the book. The letter was still on the shelf. He had not answered it, and not answering was also a thing with nobody in it, and he wrote that down too, because the sixth time in a year the correct thing had been to write a thing down in a book in a shop and put nothing at the bottom of it, and this was the seventh.

On Saturday the fourteenth he beat the clock again in the morning, found it holding two seconds a day slow against the Quarter's public clock through the window, and sent word to Coss that it was ready. The token stayed in its paper on the counter all day where anybody coming in could see it and nobody asked about it twice.

That evening Mara Venn came in for four minutes with a printed sheet in her hand and put it on the counter without ceremony. It was the ward's rate notice for the pumping district, struck on the Office's figure with the working on it, three shillings in the pound on six thousand nine hundred and sixty, one thousand and forty-four pounds a year, and her name was on the schedule and not the Office's.

"I am the name on it," she said. "I wanted you to see it before somebody reads it to you wrong."

He read it twice and gave it back. "Is it right?"

"It is the sum of four hundred and eleven printed head counts, added in three sittings, the two foot totals agreeing. Fifty-one thousand four hundred and eighteen. It is on the return as a schedule before the rate was struck. Whether it is right is a different building."

She looked at the token in its paper. "Has it started?"

"It was on my counter this morning," he said. "A boy said a man in the arcade would give four and six for any with a Q on. I sent it back loose."

"Good," she said, and went out into Verge Lane with the rate notice in her coat, and neither of them had thanked the other for anything, and the shop was quiet.

He locked up, wound the test movement down, and wrote the day in the grey ledger. At the foot of the page he wrote the figure the whole volume would be measured against, the way Leda wrote a number on a card whether or not anybody wanted it.

*A night of drift is fifty seconds. A hundred minutes is six thousand seconds, which is a hundred and twenty nights, which is four months. A token costs four shillings and sixpence.*
