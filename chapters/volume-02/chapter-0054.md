# Chapter 54 — A Dead Pump

The list said *fourteen, Ferrywright Row, a cellar, four feet of standing water since the flood, and nobody has looked at it since the flood.* On the Saturday morning of the eighteenth of the sixth month he went down with a lamp and a bucket and a folding rule and a spanner and a length of sailcloth, and he was there an hour before anybody else because the hour he was to be there was ten o'clock and the lamp wanted winding.

---

The cellar steps at 14 Ferrywright Row are nine, stone, and about two foot six, and he went down them on the Friday evening to look and came up again because the water was over the fourth one.

On the Saturday at about ten he went down with the sailcloth round his waist, because there is a way of doing this and it is not a dignified way, and he had learned it in a pumping ward in the fifth month of the year.

It is a brick floor laid in sand, and the sand is soft, and the water is about four feet two inches, and standing in four feet of cold water in a cellar on Weir Lane in June is a thing a man can do for twenty minutes and think about for the rest of his life.

The lamp showed a wall on the left in old white brick with a York stone lintel, and a floor of bricks in sand, and a hole in the floor about the size of a dinner plate, and the water coming up through the hole in a slow cone about the height of a coffee cup, and going away again at the far end, so that it was a slow rotation and not a fountain.

He put his hand in it and it was cold and it was moving and it had grit in it.

Then he went along the wall on his hands with the lamp up, at about the height of the waterline, looking for a stain, and there was one, about nine inches high and about eleven feet long, in the shape of a band, and the band was the colour of tea and it was the right shape, because a lead ferrule under a floor does not come out in a round hole in a brick. It comes out at a coupling, in a band, along the line of the pipe, and the pipe is under the floor and the floor is sand, and the sand is what let the water go into the wall instead of into the room.

He came up at about twenty past ten and sat on the top step for a while.

**Then he went up two flights and got a piece of paper.**

The paper is in the book, and it is the reason the whole of the first half of this is about a wet cellar and not about a machine, and it is copied out of the district works' own defect return, because Orrin Vale can get a sight of a district works' return in about nine minutes a week by being the sort of man who stands at a counter and asks, and Ambrose, who is about thirty and has been at that counter eleven years, is the sort of man who gives a man a sight of it.

> **DEFECT 6/331. Fourteenth, Ferrywright Row, Lower Weirs. Water standing in cellar. Reported by occupier, the fourth of the sixth. Attended the ninth of the sixth, half past five in the morning. Cause: ferrule, lead, 1903 connection to 1911 rising main, failed. Isolation required and effected. Not made good. Carried to the maintenance schedule, programme year three.**

Attended the ninth of the sixth, **half past five in the morning.**

The isolation is a stopcock at the foot of the main in the lane, and the stopcock is clock-driven, and it is on the branch, and the branch at the second court is one hour twenty-seven minutes and thirty seconds out.

So the crew had set out at about half past five because the clock said the window opened at half past five, and it opened at about seven, and the water came up four feet in about two hours.

"It would have been about fourteen inches," said Orrin Vale, to a room on Weir Lane on the Sunday evening, to five people. "I have been down there with a folding rule and I have worked it out twice, and I am not going to publish it, because the temperature of that cellar has been about fifty-one degrees for two days, which is four and a half degrees above the return in the works' own book for the sixth month, and a fourteen-inch figure computed from a cellar in that state is a number I would be ashamed of in about a year.

"Four feet it was. That is on the return, in a man's hand, in ink, and the ink is dry. Everything I have just said to you is me arguing with a figure that is in a book, and I am telling you I do not know what the figure is, and I am telling you what the number on the return is, and those are two different sentences and I have been keeping them apart all the way through this."

"So the water is the clock's fault," said Bessie Crane.

"No," he said. "The water is a lead ferrule from 1903 letting go on a floor of sand, and it is joined to a rising main laid in 1911, so the joint is eight years older than the pipe it is let into, which is backwards, and a ferrule like that is done once in a life and then it is done for ever. It was going to let go some time this decade whatever anybody did. The cellars in this row are all like that. There are four of them on this side and I have been in two.

"The clock is not the ferrule's fault. The clock is why it is four feet and not fourteen inches, and it is four feet in a cellar that belongs to a man of seventy-eight, and a man of seventy-eight with four feet of water in his cellar cannot get to his front door at night, and the district works' return does not have a column for that and neither does anything else."

---

**Then the practice, which is on the Sunday and is a different thing entirely.**

There is a pump room under the Bellweather Road washing-yard. It is below ground, it is eight feet by eleven, it has a wooden bench, a vice, a coke stove that is out, and about eleven feet of headroom, and it is where Bessie Crane's father used to keep the fire insurance certificates and where nobody has put anything since the laundry took a second boiler in 1949.

Bessie Crane let him in on the Sunday morning at about ten because he asked, and because he had been down a cellar in Ferrywright Row and had not been able to be wrong about the ferrule, and because he had carried two buckets out of it on the Friday without being asked and without making a thing of it, which she has noticed and will mention twice in a year and no more.

On the bench there is a second-stage circulator out of the galvanising and wire works, and it came out of that works in 1922 and it was scrapped on the ninth of the fourth month of this year, and it has been dead and drained and open ever since, and it is the safest object in the Lower Weirs.

He cleaned his hands. He put his palm flat on the case.

He asked one question out loud, in plain words, about a named load, which is the whole of a rule he set himself in a kitchen on Weir Lane in the second month of the year, and which is four words long and which he has never once been tempted to break in public:

**What is about to let go.**

And he got the four things.

Heat, at the joint on the delivery side. Vibration, in the shaft, and a small one, at the top. Resistance, and a place where the resistance is not the resistance it should be. And an edge at the limit of it, which is the fourth thing and which nobody who has not had it can be told what it is like.

And he wrote it down, and the price, and the reason, under it, in the shop book, on the Sunday night, in a hand that was not as steady as it usually is.

> *Nineteenth of the sixth. Bellweather Road pump room, beneath the washing-yard. Second-stage circulator, 1922, out of service the ninth of the fourth, dead and drained.*
> *One question, plain, about a named load. Four things back. The same four things, in the same order, that I got on the seventeenth of the fifth month off a blown pipe in a flood at forty minutes past one in the morning, which was in fact on a machine that was working.*
> *A hundred and fifty seconds. Entered as a reading cost with the reason written under it, which is the seventeenth of the second month's practice and not a toll and I am not going to call it anything else.*

Then he wrote the thing he had actually learned, which is the only reason the hundred and fifty seconds was worth anything at all.

> *And here is what I have got this month that I have not got any month before, and I have had it in my hands for a day and I did not want to write it.*
> *It is a dead machine.*
> *I cannot tell. There is no difference. I have had a dead circulator from 1922 in my hand and I have had a blown pipe in a flood in my hand and what comes back is the same four items in the same order and I have been treating that as a fact about the machine.*
> *It is not a fact about the machine. It is a fact about me. My hand does not know whether the thing it is on is running, and the reason it does not know is that the running is not carried the way the strain is carried, and everything I have ever been able to do has been the second thing and never the first.*
> *Which means that a man with my sort of ear, alone, at night, on a city this size, is going to put his hand on something one day that is alive. It is not a danger to the public in the way Mr Pell says it is. It is a danger to me, because I will take the four things as an answer about a machine and they are not an answer about a machine, they are an answer about a thing and the thing may not be there.*

---

Ivo Pell was on the step of the pump room at about eleven o'clock on the Sunday morning with his hat off, and he had been there some time.

"Say that last bit again," he said.

Orrin Vale said it again.

"No," said Ivo Pell. "Not that last bit. The other one. About the joint on the delivery side."

"The joint on the delivery side is hot."

"It is hot," said Ivo Pell, "and it is hot because it has been off a fire and dry and open for ten weeks and there is a plate of oxide on it two thousandths thick, and the oxide is a worse conductor than the iron under it, and if you had asked me I would have told you that in about four seconds without touching it, and I would have been right."

"Yes," said Orrin Vale.

"So that is what you do," said Ivo Pell. "You get four items in the right order and one of them is an ordinary fact about an old joint, and you write all four down as though they came out of the same place, and two days in three you will get three of them that are true and one that is the machine's own rust, and you will not be able to tell which was which, and in about a year you will be a man in this city who says he can feel a pipe and who has hurt somebody."

He came down the eight steps and stood in a pump room with a dead pump in it and looked at the book on the bench, open, and did not touch it.

"I said in February that a bench man's ear is not an instrument," he said, "and I have not changed my mind and I am not going to pretend to have come down here to say something new. A man who practises on a dead pump is behind his trade and a man who practises on a live one is a public hazard. I said it once. It is still true. You have been on that thing for an hour and a half and it has told you about its own oxide and you have written it down as though it told you anything about the city."

Then he said the sentence, and he said it once, and he said it without any emphasis whatever, the way a man reads a figure off a return.

"You cannot feel a thing that is not connected," said Ivo Pell, "and everything that has been done to this district for eighteen years has been done by disconnection."

---

Nobody said anything for a moment and the coke stove that has not been lit since 1949 sat there.

"There is a room under the Cathedral," said Ivo Pell, "with a keeper, that is not open to petition, that is not empty, and that has a name on it and no date on it. I have been in the building twelve years and I know it exists and I have never been in it and I have never applied to be, and I am telling you it exists because you are going to go and look for it, and because it is better that you hear the shape of it from a man in an operator's coat than that you go at it in the dark and get refused twice.

"And that room is not a mystery, Mr Vale, and neither is anything in it, and neither is anybody in it. It is a *place*. It has a floor. It has a door and a keeper and a lock, and there is a form somewhere that puts a name into it, and every single thing that has ever been done to a person in this city in a way they did not know about has been done by making a place, and then not going into the place, and then putting a line in a footer in letters smaller than my signature.

"You have been reading footers for five months. Go and stand in a room."

"I have no licence and I have no business under the Cathedral," said Orrin Vale.

"You have no business in about nine of the buildings you have been in this year," said Ivo Pell, "and you have been in all nine, and in two of them you were asked to leave by a man in a good coat who was within his rights. That is not a complaint. I am telling you the shape of it. The moment you want to be able to do that lawfully, and there will be a moment, you will need a licence, and a licence is a person, and this city has three hundred of them and the ones in this district are at the end of a bus."

He put his hat back on.

"I am not going to teach you anything," he said. "I have said that once this morning and I am not going to say it again. I do not teach men who take a toll to ask a machine a question. I have watched four of them and two of them are dead and one of them is a Director."

"I am not taking a toll," said Orrin Vale.

"No," said Ivo Pell. "You are not, and that is the entire reason I am standing on this step instead of walking past it, and I have not worked out what to do about it yet, and neither have you, and I would rather we both went away and worked it out separately than worked it out in a pump room with a dead pump in it."

---

The arrangement was agreed in about four sentences at the top of the steps at one o'clock, written out that evening, dated the nineteenth, and it is four lines long and it is in his own hand, and **Ivo Pell has not signed it and would not.**

> **MR M. VALE AND MR I. PELL. Nineteenth day of the sixth month. Set out by Mr Vale and read over to Mr Pell at the top of the steps and agreed word for word.**
> 1. The dead pumps, scrap circulators and retired ram pumps in the basement of the district works' store, and the two retired timers in the store at the Green Span outpatients' annexe, may be used by Mr Vale on any working day, on his own responsibility, and he will leave them as he found them.
> 2. Mr Pell will come and look at what Mr Vale has written down, at a time of his own choosing, about once in a fortnight, and will say what he thinks of it.
> 3. Neither of us will describe this to anybody as instruction, and neither of us will be thanked for it, and if either of us is thanked we will stop.
> 4. **Mr Pell gives his reason for agreeing to it, and says it once, and does not repeat it.** *A man with that ear and no instrument is going to find a live one eventually, and I would rather he found a dead one first.*

And underneath the four clauses, in a smaller hand, because he wrote it afterwards and put it under the rule on purpose:

> *This is not a favour and it is not a licence and it confers nothing. He has no standing and he will not get any from it. It is written down because a thing done in a pump room on a Sunday is a thing that is exactly what it looks like, and the only defence I have ever had against anybody is a piece of paper with a date on it.*
>
> *He has not signed it, and he will not, and the reason he will not is the fourth clause: if either of us is thanked we stop, and a signature is the easiest thing in this city to turn into a favour, and the whole value of this sheet is that it is not between two men, it is between two men and a date.*

---

**On the Monday the twentieth of the sixth month, at about four in the afternoon, they went down the Ferrywright Row cellar together**, with a lamp and a bucket and a length of board, and Pell did not go in. He stood on the top step for the whole forty minutes with his hands in his pockets and did not offer to help and did not go away, and when Orrin Vale came up the nine steps with his knees ruined he said one thing.

"You will want to write a figure on that," he said. "Four feet two inches on a rule on a Monday afternoon. Do not."

"I was not going to."

"You were thinking about it," said Ivo Pell, "and I have watched you think about it, and you have that look you get. So I will say the thing about it and then I will go.

"Four feet two inches is four feet two inches on a folding rule in a cellar at ten degrees under a stone stair on the eighteenth of the sixth month. It is not a duration. It is not a duration in anybody's column, and there is no column in the country for how long a man of seventy-eight has been sitting in his own front room with a door he cannot get out of, and the works' return has four feet two inches in it in a man's handwriting and it is on a programme in year three, and if you go and put a number next to that number you will be doing the thing that made this district, and you will be doing it because you are frightened, and being frightened is not a reason and it has never once in the history of this city been a reason."

He went up the nine steps backwards without using the handrail, which is the only piece of showing off he has done in three weeks, and the handrail in a cellar in the Weirs is as old as the cellar and fits him, and that was appropriate.
