# Chapter 50 — The Column With Nothing In It

The Toll Stair had been washed twice by the rain in ten days and it was chalked on the Tuesday morning of the seventh of the sixth month at six o'clock by a man with a bucket and a board, and Ostrow has done it every morning for nineteen years, and the stair is a stone stair in the Cathedral with a rail down the middle and a wall on one side, and about four hundred people a day go up it and about the same number come down, and it is the one place in Morrowfen where people stop to read things.

Ostrow scrubs it at half past five and chalks it at six. He had a chamois and a lump of chalk and a board he had made himself out of a piece of a shutter, and he is not a clerk and he is not an officer. The two new columns had been chalked out on the wall in full for the first time in the life of the stair on the evening of Friday the third of the sixth month, and the Office's note had been in the pumping ward's case since the Friday afternoon, and on the Monday a district works man had measured the gap between the existing columns and the stair wall with a folding rule and it was about two feet, and the two new columns were chalked inside those two feet, and the rule and the chalk were both in the picture, and a stranger could see that they had been done by somebody who had measured.

---

So on the Tuesday morning the seventh of the sixth month, at six o'clock, the stair carried four columns.

The first two were forty-three years old. The minutes column, filled the way it always has been filled, was: **3**, and beside it **Clockwork Quarter**, and beside that, in a small upright hand about a quarter of an inch high, a man's initials, because the custom of the stair is that a district's own figure is put up by a person of that district, and the three minutes went on that stair on the morning of the eighteenth of the first month and have been scrubbed and re-chalked every morning since by whoever is of the Quarter and can be got, and the last six of those mornings had been done by a clockfitter out of Verge Lane because Ostrow asked him and he came for six shillings and would not take a note.

Under the three, the twenty-six, and beside it **Clockwork Quarter**, in the same hand, because the same clockfitter did that one too on the morning of the ninth of the third month and Ostrow had asked him.

And then the two new ones, and in the first of them, in a hand that was not a district's hand because there was no district to write it, four letters and a full stop and a letter, in the same small upright hand as the two above it because the clockfitter had come and Ostrow had asked him:

**VANCE, P.**

And in the second of them, nothing at all.

Ostrow looked at that for a while, and then he did the thing he does about a quarter of three times a year, which is to put the chamois against the wall below it, and look, and take the chamois away again, and put the chamois back, and leave it, because the fifth line of the Office's note says that an empty column in that book is a statement that the interval was not recorded and is not to be filled in by inference, and Ostrow read the note on the Friday and he is fifty-four and he has been on that stair nineteen years and he said one thing about it, which was to the woman from the Office and not to anybody else, "Then that is a thing that is on the wall and is empty on purpose, and in nineteen years that is the first one of anything I have chalked that I can say what it is for."

He was there at about half past ten on the Tuesday morning because he had gone up to read the notice about the classification — no, there was no classification on that board any more; it came off on the eighth of the fifth month when the reference period expired and Rill did not renew it, and Orrin had gone up to look at the empty nail four times in a fortnight.

A man stopped on the stair about two-thirds of the way up, at the height where the four columns are, and read them, and stood there, and read them again.

He was about thirty-five, or forty, in a good coat, and he had come up the hill for something else and had stopped because that is what this stair is.

"Three minutes," said the stranger.

"Yes," said Orrin Vale.

"Out of the Quarter's clocks."

"Out of the Quarter's reserve, which is the same thing on a good day, and the Quarter's reserve is full and the Quarter had no interest in the answer, and the machine charges in proportion to what a district holds." He had said that sentence eleven times since the fifteenth of the first month and he said it without any weight at all. "That is the first line and it is the one everybody reads."

"And the two on the right," said the stranger.

"A name and a duration. For every toll taken out of a person — their attention, their coordination, their memory. It is new. It went into the ledger in the pumping ward on Friday, and the Office of Public Reckoning required it, and it is on this stair because the stair is the only public board in this city and I asked for it here, and the man who chalks it takes six shillings and would not take a note."

The stranger looked at the two new columns for a while.

"Vance," he said. "P."

"Yes."

"What is the other one."

"There is no other one. The column is for how much. And no instrument in this city timed how much of that shift the reading took, and I was in the building on the night, and I have had a rule and a tape and a stopwatch and about twenty weeks with them to try to work it out, and I am not going to put a number in that column that I made up, and that is not modesty, that is the only thing I have got to put there."

He heard himself say it and heard how thin it sounded on a stair, and he let it sound thin, because the alternative was a sentence about how much he cared, and there is no column for that either and there never has been.

The stranger turned round and looked at him properly, which nobody on that stair does.

"And who took it?" he said. "The three minutes. Out of whom."

Orrin Vale stood on the other side of the stair with the chalk still in his hand — he had it because he had come up to do the two new columns at half past six that morning with Ostrow and had put the rule down afterwards and had forgotten about the chalk, and had been carrying it about the Quarter all morning like a man carrying a thing he had no reason to be carrying.

It is not a difficult question. He had been asked it, in effect, by a woman in a stairwell and by a man in a bus and by a man on a landing and by a woman on a wet fourth floor of a block in the Weirs, and he had not answered any of them, and the reason is that every answer he has is about one man.

"Perry Vance," he said. "Forty-one. He was on the night crew at Weir Two. He went down on his own initiative to close a hand valve that the model had already said was shut, and the pump that should have stopped did not stop, and the Directorate put a notice on the pump house door the next day and every word of it was true."

The stranger said, "Right," and then said the other thing, and it was the thing nobody had asked him since the fifteenth of the first month.

"How many more?"

It is the wrong question to ask a man on a stair and it is the only question anybody in this city has ever wanted to ask, and Orrin Vale has had the answer in various forms since the fifteenth of the first month and it has never once been a number.

He could have said: sixty-one calibration-strain entries in one committee's files in a year, in four wards holding a hundred and ninety thousand people, in a city of two and a half million. He could have said: four hundred and eleven notes sent out of one small office in nine years, and a drawer of thirty-one cards on Monday, and the drawer was eleven in the third month. He could have said: a hall's labour book with nine series and about a thousand pages and no name on any of them, and a batch of forty-one entries, and a public index with a countersignature on it, and an index entry that is a statement and not the thing. He could have said: a box under the Cathedral with a keeper, that is not open to petition, that is not empty, and that has a name on it and no date on it and is the only room in this city that anybody has ever been refused entry to twice.

He could have said all four of those and every one of them is a document and not a number, and there is a column for a name and a column for a duration and there is no column on that stair, or in the Office of Public Reckoning, or in the Assembly's minutes, or anywhere in this city, for the size of a thing that nobody has counted.

"I don't know," said Orrin Vale.

And the man on the stair nodded, the way a man nods at a thing he expected, and went up, and did not say anything else, and was not a journalist and was not from a paper and was nobody, and he will not be back.

Orrin Vale stood on the stair with a piece of chalk in his hand for about a minute after he had gone, and then he went down and did the only thing there was to do with a chalk and an empty column, which was to leave it empty, and put the chalk in his pocket, and go down Cathedral Rise to a shop with a lease on two benches and a sign in the window that said **HOROLOGER. REPAIRS TO ANY DESCRIPTION.**

---

On the Wednesday the eighth of the sixth month he was on the second step of the Cathedral at a quarter to twelve in the morning with a plate in a cloth under his arm, because the third step is the one you can stand on, and he has stood on it twice.

The Cathedral's public clock is the clock this city keeps its reference in, and it was the clock he had certified in the first month of the year in two hours and forty minutes with a gauge and a second watch and a sounding board, and it is a good clock and it is right, and the street below it is the busiest in Morrowfen at a quarter to twelve and there were about four hundred people on it and a tram going by and a man selling hot water.

He counted. He counts. It is nineteen years of standing and it is not a superstition and he would be embarrassed to have it called one; it is that a clockmaker who stands under a public clock and does not count is doing something else, and he was doing what he does, and he counted nine of them, and the last stroke came down through the frame of the door and into the stone, and it had a shove in it.

Round, and low, and not at all the sound of a wheel. It came up out of the ground under the pavement the way water comes back into a pipe it has been out of, and it arrived the way it has arrived twice before in his life, with eight of them and then one over, and it went on past the tick with a weight on it somewhere he could not name and could not go and find.

It gave him nothing. There was no night in it and no face in it and not one word. It cost him nothing at all. It is not a warning, because a warning costs somebody something and this has cost him nothing, and it is not an answer, because nobody asked it anything, and it is not Aftershock, because Aftershock is a load on a named machine that answers a question asked in plain words and costs a keeper's toll every single time, and this is not that and he knows the difference better than anybody alive and has known it since a kitchen in the second month of the year.

It is the ninth thing in a machine that does eight, and the eight is the time, and the ninth is not.

He stood on the second step for about four minutes in front of four hundred people and a tram driver, and then he went down to a shop on Verge Lane with a lease to Michaelmas and two benches, and put the cloth on the bench, and got out the grey book, and stood at the end of the counter where the light is best, and wrote the third and last entry about it that there will ever be in his life.

> *Sixth month, the eighth. Cathedral, a quarter to twelve. Eight, and one over. The same as the fifteenth of the first month under the crown case, and the same as the seventeenth of the first month through the pump house floor, and the same in every particular.*
>
> *Nothing in it. No night, no face, no word. It cost nothing and it is worth nothing and it is not a Reading, because a Reading has four lines and a blind margin in a footer and this has not got a margin of any kind, and it is not a warning, and there is no person in this city I could tell.*
>
> *Three times in five months, and I have a book with two thousand pages in it and a tape and a level and a licence that does not let me certify anything at all, and I am going to go on not finding it, and the next one will be the fourth, and it is still, exactly, unexplained.*
