# Chapter 58 — Sixty-Two Shillings

The class is abolished on the first of the seventh month and the class was called the maintenance-deferred class and the price of being in it was eightpence the head for a year where the ordinary class is two shillings, and the difference between the two is a shilling and fourpence a head, and **the one shilling and tenpence in the pound that everybody in this district is talking about is the ward's water rate and not a class price at all**, and the two get run together in this city about once a week, and the reason the class existed was that a landlord could put a building's people into a cheaper grade and let the difference fall on the ward's rate, and the reason nobody had ever found that out is that it had been in the price schedule for twenty-two years underneath two other grades.

---

On the licensing floor of the Guild of Horologists on the Thursday the thirtieth of the sixth month there is a board on the wall by the counter that was not there a month ago, and it is four feet by three, and it is ruled in the guild's own type, and it says:

> **CERTIFICATES ISSUED BY A MASTER, COUNTERSIGNED BY A HOLDER OF THE FIRST CLASS. Published monthly at this counter, in accordance with the standing orders of this hall, from the first day of the sixth month.**
> *First month of publication: nineteen certificates. Two of them were to sites in the Lower Weirs. Both are listed. The name of the master is at the head of the list. The name of the countersigning first-class holder is at the foot of the list.*

That is the whole of what Gerren Hask's punishment looks like on a wall, and it is a list of nineteen lines in a hall where four hundred people a week walk past the counter, and it is going to be there for the rest of his life, and he put it up himself, and the putting-up took about four minutes, and he did it on the first of the sixth month, the same morning as the determination, about half an hour after it.

He is at the counter at about ten o'clock on the Thursday morning of the thirtieth. He is fifty-five. He is a repairer under public oversight. He did not confess.

---

**And the class goes, because the standing order says so, and the standing order is the guild's, and the guild can only do one thing with it, which is operate it.**

Two of the class's costs are on the page in four places and neither of them is a scandal. The first is that the class is abolished within twelve months of the first of the sixth month, and any site in it is to be entered in the ordinary class or not at all, and **the ordinary class is two shillings the head for a year and one shilling where a man is the occupier.** The second is that the class's last Weirs sites are four, and three of them were entered in the ordinary class in the first week of June by somebody who read the standing order, and the fourth was not, and it is the Bellweather Road laundry.

"There are nine left in the city," said Gerren Hask, on the Thursday, to nobody in particular, because there were about nine people at the counter and he was reading a list and not making a speech. "Eleven sites, and four of them are in this district, and the one everybody is going to read about is not a site. It is a laundry on Bellweather Road with thirty-one women in it and it has been in the deferred class since the year the class was invented, and the price of putting it into the ordinary class is two shillings the head for a year and there is nobody in it who is the occupier of the property, because a woman who works in a laundry is not a ratepayer and never has been, and one shilling where a man is the occupier does not apply and is not available to anybody in that building."

"So it is sixty-two shillings," said the clerk.

"It is sixty-two shillings," said Gerren Hask. "And there is no form on which any of those thirty-one women is entered in the ordinary class individually, and there is a site schedule, and a site schedule is a site, and the site is a laundry, and the site carries a head count and the head count is entered as a number and not as thirty-one names, because the guild's price schedule has never in twenty-two years had a column for a name in it, and I am not going to invent one in the last month of a class I did not invent."

He put the list down.

"And the other half of it, which is the half that will be printed, is that a laundry on Bellweather Road in the Lower Weirs has been paying two shillings a head for the ward's water rate for twenty-two years through this class, which is not two shillings the head for the guild's schedule. It is a shilling and fourpence a head a year less than the ordinary class charges, and the difference over twenty-two years on thirty-one heads is about forty-five pounds nine shillings, and the class was invented to keep a landlord's water cheap and it has been used on a laundry's water instead, and I have been a master in this hall for thirty-one years and I did not know that until a week ago and I have checked it twice."

---

**Bessie Crane came in on the Friday the first of the seventh month at about ten past ten in the morning with sixty-two shillings in coin in a cloth bag and a receipt from her own book, because she keeps a book.**

She is about sixty and she has run the Bellweather Road laundry for nineteen years and she was the one who put four pounds ten of the council's money into an appendix before the committee in the fourth month of this year and got thirty-one women their Saturday morning back for a fortnight, and she is not a martyr and has never been thanked for any of it and does not expect to be.

Sixty-two shillings is seven hundred and forty-four pence. There are fifty-two weeks in a year. That is a shilling and two and a third pence a week, and the hall's counter works in halfpence and will want the sixteen pence in even money, and sixteen pence is about a quarter of an hour of a presser's time at the laundry rate, and the quarter of an hour is the real cost of the form and always is.

"I have worked it out four ways," she said, and put the bag on the counter, and did not put it down until the clerk had counted it and written it. "Sixty-two shillings a year is a shilling and twopence a week. My margin is a penny a sheet. We do about four hundred sheets on a Friday. So sixty-two shillings is a shilling and twopence a week against about four shillings a week, and if I do the arithmetic honestly the arithmetic is not the problem.

"The problem is that I have got a book with thirty-one names in it and a form with a head count on it, and if I send the form in with the book then I am telling the hall what thirty-one women are, and there is no column on that form for what they are, and it will go in a drawer and be a number in a drawer, and in about four years somebody will pull it and it will be a number in a drawer with a date on it and no anything else.

"And the other problem is worse and I have thought about it on the walk down here. The class is abolished and the site is entered in the ordinary class or not at all. So I enter. And I am a site with thirty-one heads in it, in a district, and my rates are made on the ward's return like everybody's. And in October I am one of the reasons that a notice with a number on it and no name on it goes up in October, and the name on it will be mine and not the hall's, because I am the one who handed the form in."

"Then do not hand the form in," said Orrin Vale, who was standing four feet away and had said nothing for about twenty minutes.

"No," said Bessie Crane. "I have decided to hand it in. I have decided it on this walk. And I want it written down that a man on the licensing floor told me not to and I told him no, and I have not got anything against him, and I would like whoever reads this in four years to know that I thought about it for a walk and not for an hour."

---

And then a woman of about forty-five asked him the thing nobody in this building has ever asked him, and she asked it quietly and she was not asking for charity and she had a child with her and a bundle that had not been collected.

She said, "Is it possible to do that in parts."

And Orrin Vale stood at the counter of the licensing floor of the Guild of Horologists at about twenty past ten in the morning on the first of the seventh month, with no licence, and answered her, and it is the only time in the whole of this year that anybody has asked him for money.

"No," he said. "I'm sorry. It is one form for a year and the year starts on the day it is entered, and there is no part-year rate and there is no instalment and there is no hardship clause, and I have looked, and I looked in February when I could have certified one and there is no hardship clause then either."

"Thank you," she said, and went, and it was a genuine thank you and it cost him more than the determination did, and nobody saw it, and he did not say one word about it for three weeks and then he did say one word about it, in a book, on the seventh.

He could have paid it. He has about eleven pounds in the world and the lease on two benches runs to Michaelmas and a licence that does not let him certify anything runs to the nineteenth of the third month next year. He could have paid it in about four seconds and he is the only man in that hall who could have done it without anybody noticing, because he put eighty-two shillings into the same series on the first of the fifth month with a wet stamp and told nobody, and the four-line card that came back says the crew is part of a certified shift for that year.

He has a private act in this city that makes him a person who pays, and it does not scale, and it is in a drawer in a kitchen on Weir Lane with no key written down, on purpose, and there is no index of it anywhere in this city.

---

**Gerren Hask, on the way out, at about ten to eleven, to nine people at the counter who had not asked him anything:**

"Mr Vale. A thing, and then I am going to Northglass, and I am not going to be thanked for it.

"That laundry is in the ordinary class now and I have signed it and a first-class holder has countersigned it because that is what the standing orders now say, and the countersigner is a man in the Sloughs who came in for it on a bus and who has signed nine certificates this month and does not know a single one of them.

"And the reason I am telling this room is that a woman has just paid sixty-two shillings into this counter and it will be in a drawer in about four years and nobody will ever look at it, and she has put herself in a book with thirty-one names in it and sent the names out of the room.

"I did not ask her to do that. The ordinance put her to it and the class put her to it and the class is going because I declined a certification the ordinance let me make, and a system that punishes the man who said no does this, and I have thought about that this morning and I have not got an answer and I am not going to pretend to the room that I have got an answer.

"That is all. Nineteen lines on a board. I am going to Northglass."

He was not thanked. He has said in a pump house and in a hall, twice now, in public, in advance, that he is not to be thanked for things, and a man who has said that himself is a man who cannot be thanked afterwards, and nobody in that hall tried.

---

**At about half past four that afternoon Orrin Vale wrote in the grey book at the end of the counter in Verge Lane where the light is best**, and the entry is five paragraphs and the third one is the restraint, and the restraint is a rule he has made for himself out of a sentence a woman on a pump house floor said in the fifth month and which is, in his own hand on the page, the hardest thing he has ever had to leave alone:

> *First of the seventh. The licensing floor. The class is gone. The laundry is in the ordinary class at two shillings the head for a year and Bessie Crane paid it in coin out of a cloth bag with a receipt out of her own book, because she is a person who keeps a book, and I told her not to and she told me no and asked for it written down that I told her, and it is written down, and she is right, and it is in a drawer in four years and nobody will ever look at it.*
> *The figures are the figures and I have got them right. Sixty-two shillings. A shilling and twopence a week. One shilling where a man is the occupier, not applicable to any of them. It is a shilling and tenpence in the pound on forty-eight thousand, which is two thousand six hundred on the ward and thirteen shillings a year on a rated house of twelve pounds. And the protection the committee gave on the fourth of the sixth is worth about three months and the audit it waits on takes about fourteen, and the report is due at the ninth month's sitting and the committee has not undertaken to renew it and cannot.*
> *And on the works' own schedule there are six hundred standpipe brackets at one thousand eight hundred and ninety pounds, and I have had that number since the fourth month and I have had it exactly, and Bevin Sarr wrote both figures on the same page of her night book in May and said she was not going to be the woman who called them comparable, because a sheet does not know that and a stair does not know that and a room does not know that, and she said it so that it would be said, and she said nobody in that room was to write it down.*
> *So I am not going to write it down. I am not going to put those two figures in one sentence, in this ledger or in any other, in this year, and I am not going to explain why in a book either, because a man who explains a rule to himself in a ledger is a man who has already broken it once by accident.*
> *Second. A woman with a child and a bundle asked me if it could be done in parts, and there is no hardship clause, and I said no, and I could have paid it in about four seconds, and I did not, and the reason I did not is not generosity and I want that written down in the same hand as everything else: **I did not pay it because the last time I paid one of these I told nobody, and the telling-nobody is the only part of it that is worth anything, and there is no version of me that pays a stranger's form and gets to keep the telling-nobody.***
> *Third. There is a board on the wall of the licensing floor with nineteen lines on it and it is there for the rest of his life and he put it up himself in four minutes on the morning of the first of the sixth, half an hour after he suspended me, and he is right about the schedule, and he is right about the forty-five pounds nine shillings nobody noticed for twenty-two years, and he does not know that he is the only person in that hall who could make me feel anything at all, and he is going to go on not knowing, and that is the correct arrangement, and it is very nearly a friendship and it is not one and it is not going to be one, and I have written down that I noticed and I have not written down what I noticed, because I have noticed three times in six months and every time I have written it down I have stopped being able to see the man.*
