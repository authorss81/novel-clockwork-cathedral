# Chapter 8 — The Form Number

The public Archive room on Cathedral Rise is on the ground floor, past the clerks' hall, and it is warm, and it smells of paper that is kept properly, which is a smell like a bakery and twice as quiet.

The room kept a Saturday for petitioners, because the people who need an archive on a Tuesday are foremen and clerks and lawyers, and the people who need one on a Saturday are everybody else. There were eleven of them at the tables. A woman with a rent dispute and a lease. Two men from a factory arguing about a shift card. A boy of about fifteen with a school request for a birth certificate, working slowly, with a pencil, because the form asked him to write his surname and he was not sure of the spelling.

At the desk, in a chair with a cushion that had been patched four times, sat a man of about fifty-five in a coat that had been good in a different decade, who looked up when they came in and put down his pen and stood up, which nobody else in the room did.

"Sindri Lake," he said. "Keeper. If you have come about a record, sit at the second table and not the first, the first is for forms still in use." He looked at Orrin, and at the slip Mara Venn had put in his hand before they came up the stairs. "And you have brought an investigator's slip with a returned stamp on it, which means you want the thing I have just refused, and I am going to have to be careful with you, because being careful is what I do, and it is the only thing I have."

"You refused it?" Orrin said.

"I did not refuse it. I forwarded it. I hold the public record. I have no power to close a record and no power to open a sealed one, and the difference between those two facts is the entire content of my working life." He sat down again and folded his hands. "You may sit. Both of you. Not at that table, though, that is the birth register, and I would rather you did not make the boy nervous."

They sat at the second table. Lake brought a card of index slips and a date wheel and offered it to them, and Orrin understood that this was not a courtesy but a form.

"Whose record?"

"The Lower Weirs pumping ward," Orrin said. "Weir Two floodgate and second-stage pumping station. I want the night shift record for any month in the last twenty years. Any month. I do not know the month."

"That is a wide request."

"I know what it is."

"Then it will take two days and it will cost four coppers and I will tell you now that you will not get it, because the night shift is not recorded. There is no night shift record. There has not been a night shift record at Weir Two since the works were connected to the reserve in the year the union won the nine-hour." He said it kindly. "What there is, is a maintenance line. I will show you the maintenance line."

He went and got the volume, which was heavy, and set it on the table with a sound the whole room turned to look at, and opened it, and turned it round.

"Second-stage pumping station, Weir Two, Weirs. Annual maintenance. The record gives a period, a quantity of hours, a description of work, and a certification. That is all. That is what the record is *for.* It is not a roll of men. If it were a roll of men, then when the number of men changed, the record would have to be amended, and you would have to have a man alive to amend it, and in the Weirs half the men who are alive are not the men who were here." He put his finger on a column of figures. "Fourteen hundred and six hours. That is Weir Two, one year, maintenance. It is honest work and it was paid for. I will say again that I am not saying anybody stole anything. I am saying what the paper is, because you keep asking me for a paper that does not say what you need it to say."

Mara had said almost exactly this on the Rise two days ago, and Orrin thought: *of course she had. She has been here before.*

"Then how does a person disappear from a record?" he said. "Not go on to something. Not die. Disappear from the index."

Lake stopped writing.

"That," he said, "is the only interesting question anybody has asked me this month. Sit still for a moment."

He turned back a few leaves, to a different part of the volume, where the entries had a ruled column on the left that the maintenance lines did not have.

"Every record that leaves this room is registered," he said. "Otherwise the public record would simply have holes in it, and a hole is indistinguishable from a lie, and the whole purpose of keeping an honest archive is that you can tell the difference between *there is no record* and *the record was taken away.* Forty-three years ago somebody in this building insisted on that, against an argument that is still being made in three other cities. So: a transfer out of the open record is entered here, with the date, the batch, the receiving room, and the number of the request that asked for it."

He turned the volume another degree and put his finger on a line and moved it two inches to the left so that it was in front of Orrin.

The entry was four lines long and it read: *transferred, batch 21/4, Weirs series, to the sealed archive, Cathedral sub-basement, on request form GH-9, standing withdrawal, maintenance-deferred class, one batch of forty-one entries, countersigned for the affected district by the pumping warden of the Lower Weirs, deputy — KADE, S.*

"There," Lake said. "Eighteen years ago, in the storm month. Forty-one entries out of the Weirs series, transferred to a room under this building, on the authority of a request form. And the request form, Mr Vale, is not a Cathedral number."

"It isn't," Orrin said.

"No." The keeper of the Archive sat back and looked at him with the first real interest he had shown, and it was a bad moment for Orrin, because the interest was not in him. "It is a guild number. *GH* is the Guild of Horologists' own series, and nine is the maintenance-deferred class, which is a request to record work as not having been done because there was no money to pay for doing it." He turned to Mara. "There is a price schedule with it. I have seen one. The hall's price schedule for standing withdrawal is two shillings the head for a year, and one shilling for a standing where a person has already been paid once."

He had never heard the name. It took him four seconds to be certain that was true, and then four more seconds to understand that a name he had never heard meant a person standing in a room somewhere who had signed this, and that the person was not a monster and had almost certainly been twenty-six.

Orrin sat very still.

Perry Vance had been on Weir Two nights for twenty years. Five men. Two shillings a head for a year. He could not have told anybody what it cost, because it was not charged to him; it was charged as a saving, and the saving was the point, and the price of his being in the water at three in the morning instead of in a bed was two shillings a year, forty shillings over twenty years, and ten pounds for the five of them, and the money was the guild's and the hours were the district's.

"Forty-one entries," he said.

"Forty-one entries in that batch. There have been others since, in other series." Lake began to close the volume, and then he stopped, and took out a thin card from the pocket of the volume, which was the transfer's routing sheet, and read a line of it, and then read the whole thing because he was a man who finished what he had started.

"The routing copies went to four places. The hall. The pumping ward, being the district affected. The Continuity Office inspection file, which keeps a copy of every transfer, because it is the Office's business to know what is in the model. And one to a Green Span public-health rota, on the ground that the removal affected the district's health visitors and they were to be told." He read the fourth line. "*Rot. 3rd Weds, Green Span. Copy to attendance: FESTINGER, A.*"

He put the card down.

"That is all of it," he said. "That is a rota of volunteers, and a name, and I do not know what it means, and I have not looked for a meaning, and I am telling you it is there because you asked me for a record and that is the record and there is no part of it I have selected."

Nobody at the table said anything for a moment.

---

"You cannot take testimony," Orrin said.

"I have no form for it." Lake began to stack the volumes on his trolley, one at a time, with the care of a man doing the only thing in the world he is good at. "The Archive holds what is given to it. It does not hold what people remember, and it cannot be made to, and the day it could be made to it would be a room full of opinions with dates on them."

"Then a man's name in somebody's memory—"

"Is worth nothing in this building. It is worth a great deal in a kitchen." He wheeled the trolley a foot and stopped. "I will tell you one thing that is not on the paper, Mr Vale, and then I am going back to the birth register. The Archive is not the room where a district gets believed. It is the room where a district is *read*. Those are different rooms and they have never been the same room, and I have worked in this one for nineteen years and I have watched four of them come to my counter with a board and a room full of people, and I have never once been able to do the second thing for them, because the second thing is done in a room with witnesses in it, and that room is not mine, and I have never been invited to it."

He said it without any bitterness at all, and then he said, "Four coppers for the search, and I would rather you did not pay it, because you will be back, and the next time you will know which volume."

---

The kitchen was over a chandler's shop on Weir Lane, and it was hot, and there were three women in it and a lamp and a long table with four ledgers on it, squared off at the edges like books in a school.

The woman who let them in was about thirty-eight, in a shawl over a work apron, with a pair of scissors hanging at her belt for no decorative reason whatever.

"Wenna Pruitt," she said. "You'll be the clockman. Sit down, the kettle's on. You've brought the Reckoning woman, that's good, we never see the Reckoning woman." She looked at Mara. "You want to know about a form. Take your coat off, it's the only warm room on this lane and I'm not having the door open for it."

"You know us?" Orrin said.

"I know what came off the tram on the Wednesday with a handcart, and I know the whole of this district, and there are only four people in the Weirs who don't, and three of them are dead." She set cups down. "You've come about the form that took the night crew off. Bevin told me on the Thursday that a man from the hill had been in the pump house and had asked after the board. So you want to know who the five are, and I'm going to tell you that you don't need me for that, because Bevin has it in chalk, and the chalk is good work. What you want is the eleven years."

She put a hand on the nearest ledger without opening it.

"Eleven years I've kept this district's shift book. Not the works' book. Ours. Rent days, water days, gate-crew days, pump days, who stood watch, who was up at three, who was sent for at two in the morning. Eleven years and it goes back twenty, because the one before me was my aunt and she kept it forty years before that in the same kitchen with the same table." She sat down opposite. "And here is the thing about it, and then I'll make the tea. I can tell you that on the seventeenth of this month, at about ten in the evening, Perry Vance went down a ladder into eight inches of water at Weir Two to shut a valve by hand and came back up it. I can tell you the hour. I can tell you what he said to the crew before he went, because Marta Dey was standing at the top of that ladder with the lamp and Marta Dey is in my kitchen every Thursday. And I can tell you that there were four men on that shift and that the board has five names on it, and that the fifth name is a boy who does not stand a shift, has never been entered on one, and was two streets over the whole of the night Perry Vance died."

Mara had a pencil out and had not written anything.

"That," she said, "is remarkable."

"It is worthless," said Wenna Pruitt. "That's what I've been trying to say to a man from the hall for six years and he is not here today, and he is not here today because he did not think it was worth his morning, and I have made my peace, which I did not enjoy making." She looked at Orrin. "You've got the paper and you haven't got the person."

"I have the paper now," Orrin said, and put the routing card on the table, and told them the number.

The room went quiet. Not the quiet of people being surprised; the quiet of people who have been arguing about a thing for six years and have just been handed the other side of it.

"Two shillings the head," Wenna Pruitt said. "For a year. So Perry Vance's twenty years cost the hall forty shillings, and ten pounds for the five of them, and if you put it together you could buy a good coat with what his not being in the model cost, and I have never in my life wanted to hit anybody as much as I wanted to hit somebody on the day that number came into my kitchen."

"You have been saying that a district which produces the hours and cannot produce anybody's signature is not going to be believed this century," said Orrin. "You said it in the lane, in the rain, in about eleven words, and I have been turning it over since."

"I said it eleven years ago, in this kitchen, to a man from the guild, and he told me it was a records problem, and he was right, and it has been a records problem for eleven years, and here is the whole of what a records problem has ever been worth to this district: a rent book with my aunt's hand in it and a board in a pump house with Bevin Sarr's hand in it." She put both palms flat on the four ledgers. "Nobody has to believe us. That is the part I have got used to. Believing is a thing you get if somebody with a stamp puts their name next to yours, and there is no stamp in this room, and there is no stamp in the room you came out of, and there is no stamp in a pump house at two in the morning. So we keep our own hours and nobody ever sees them, and then a man comes off the hill with a feeling about a valve and everybody in the district says, well, of course he did, he is one of them, what would you expect, he is here every week taking our clocks."

She looked at Mara.

"That is what I do, love. I keep the hours they will not read. Ask him the question you were going to ask, because I have asked it every Thursday for eleven years and I have never once been able to get to the end of it."

Mara wrote that down. "Is the number in the routing card?"

"It's in the index at the Archive and it is in the price schedule which is in the hall's own book, because the price schedule is a price schedule, they are not ashamed of it."

"Will you give me the number?"

"I'll give you the number and the date and the batch and I'll swear to all four, and I will do it in a room with a door that shuts, and I will do it tomorrow, and today I am not going to do it, because today you have brought me one piece of paper and I have brought you eleven years, and if I hand them over both at once then a clerk upstairs can say the whole thing came from a woman in a shawl, and it won't."

Mara said, "That is the most sophisticated piece of evidence handling I have encountered in nine years," and Wenna Pruitt said, "I have been in the rent business thirty years, love, we invented it."

Then she put her hand flat on the ledger and asked the question.

"Right," she said. "You've got a form. The form says the standing was withdrawn on a guild request, in a maintenance-deferred class, at a price, on a date, with a batch number, and it names a warden as a witness to the withdrawal. Whose name goes on the form, Mr Vale? Because the form says there was no crew. That's what it's *for.* That's what the class means. So when somebody asks you in a room in five years, in front of a magistrate, who was on Weir Two nights when Perry Vance drowned — the form says the answer is nobody, and it says it in writing, and it was certified, and it cost two shillings, and there's forty-one more of them somewhere in this city for that month alone."

Orrin had no answer. He looked at the table, and at the ledgers, and at the only two people in the room who could give one.

"No," he said. "I don't have it."

"No," said Wenna Pruitt. "Nobody has. That's what I've been telling the hall for six years." She got up and made the tea.

---

They were still at the table at seven, with three of the four ledgers open, when the tram went past in the street below and they heard it, both of them, at the same moment, and looked up.

It was not a sound from the tram. It was the sound of the lane's clock in the chandler's window, which had been saying the right thing all day, saying the same wrong thing it had said every afternoon for years, and was now saying it eleven minutes out.

Not slow. Wrong. The minute hand stuttered, went back two, and came forward, and the second hand in the case above it had a hitch in it like a man limping.

"The Weirs are out," said Wenna Pruitt. "Listen."

Orrin listened. Down the lane, over the roofs, out past the gasworks, there was the sound of a whole district's hourline going soft, the way a wire goes soft when it has been stretched too many times, and a tram signal on the canal road let go its window two minutes early, and somewhere a church bell that nobody had asked to move rang eight minutes late, and the whole of the district had been having its clocks corrected out of work that was not on the book for longer than anybody living could remember.

Mara Venn had gone very white.

"That's the hospital line," she said. "That's Green Span's line. That's not the Weirs, that's a — Marta, is that Green Span's road?"

"It is the tram that goes over the hospital trestle." Wenna Pruitt was already at the window. "Green Span's line, and the hospital's on it, and the hospital keeps three clocks, and if the line's slipping then the ward clocks go with it because they hang off the line like everybody else."

"How much reserve does the Weirs have?" Mara said, turning round. "As a reserve. Not as a schedule. Can the district absorb a slip?"

"Nothing," said Wenna Pruitt. "Nothing and a bootful. Our reserve went years ago. It's been standing on a pump reserve the warden moved out to the works in the spring and nobody has ever put a number on it, because there is no number, because the only thing anybody in this building could put a number on is a page of my own book, and the works' book says we have reserve to the fourth mark, and the fourth mark is a lie with a wet coat on."

"Whose decision was the pump reserve?" Orrin said.

"I know whose decision it was," said Wenna Pruitt, and did not say it, and began to clear the cups.
