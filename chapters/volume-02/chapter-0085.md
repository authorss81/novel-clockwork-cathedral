# Chapter 85 — The Fifteenth Night

On the night of Friday the fourth of the eleventh month, at about a quarter past ten at night, the hospital branch of the Weir Two line stopped carrying water because a stopcock under the floor of a cellar in Ferrywright Row had been shut in September 1951 and had never been opened, and it ran for six hours and twenty-four minutes, and there was no fault in it and no step in it and nothing anybody had done wrong, and eleven patients on a third-floor landing in Green Span worked their medicine intervals off a bracket clock eleven inches high by hand, and a man of eighty-one went down into four feet of black water with a paraffin lamp and could not turn the iron, and the whole of the cost of the night is on a card in a nurses' room with a date on it, and it is the only instrument in this city that has ever caught one.

---

**Friday the fourth, and it is the eleventh month, and the rehearsal is not being run, and nobody in this district is going to hear the word run again until the twenty-fourth of this month, and two people in this city have already said in advance that they will not put their names to it, and both of them meant it.**

He wrote the figures on the wall in the morning with a date on them and no name on them, in the middle height, in the small upright hand, and the working went under them, because the working is the argument.

> *Friday the fourth of the eleventh. Nights since the sixth of the tenth: the sixth, the seventh, the eighth, the ninth, the tenth, the eleventh, the twelfth, the thirteenth, the fourteenth, the fifteenth, the sixteenth, the seventeenth, the eighteenth, the nineteenth, the twentieth, the twenty-first, the twenty-second, the twenty-third, the twenty-fourth, the twenty-fifth, the twenty-sixth, the twenty-seventh, the twenty-eighth, the twenty-ninth, the thirtieth, the thirty-first, and the first of the eleventh, the second and the third. **Twenty-nine nights at fifty seconds is 1,450 seconds, which is twenty-four minutes and ten seconds.***
> *The gate: 2 h 29 min 20 s, and twenty-four minutes and ten seconds, **2 h 53 min 30 s.** The hospital branch: 1 h 36 min 50 s, and the same twenty-four minutes and ten seconds, **2 h 01 min 00 s.** The pair on the Quarter hill: 40 min 50 s, and the same, **1 h 05 min 00 s.***
> *None of these is a fault and none of these is a step. A step is a displacement and a displacement is permanent. A rate is a thing a committee can vote on, and there has not been a vote since the sixth of the tenth, and the rate is fifty seconds a night and it will be fifty seconds a night in the spring, and the far end of this branch is a gate with nineteen households drawing off the main under it, and by the twelfth month of next year it is going to be an hour and a half further out than the day the stoppage ended, and none of that is anybody's fault either.*

---

**And then, at about a quarter past ten at night, the hospital branch stopped carrying water, and it was not the branch, and nothing about it was a fault.**

The branch comes off the low main at Green Span, and the low main runs back eleven miles under the canal road and up under Ferrywright Row and along behind the basements off Weir Lane. It is a 1911 main and it has a stopcock under the floor of a building wherever the man who laid it thought one would be wanted, and there are four of them on that length.

**One of the four had been shut on the sixteenth of September 1951 and had not been opened since.** The works' own record of the low main is a card in an index in the district works' yard and it says: *low main, Ferrywright Row to Weir Lane, relined from the top end, 1951, reconnected 18.9.51, pressure tested, satisfactory.* Every word of that is true. The relining was done from the top end. The stopcock at number fourteen was shut to keep the new work dry while the collars were set, and the collars were set in a day and a half, and the stopcock was not opened, and **the card has no column on it for a stopcock, because a stopcock is not a defect and not an interval and not a rate, and a card about a main records the main.**

A shut stopcock is not a dry pipe. A shut stopcock is a full pipe. That is the whole of it, and it took six years and a Friday night to find out. **The four standpipes on that length had been dead since 1951 and the water in them had been standing in them since 1951, and about two hundred and eleven households in the basements and at the second court had been carrying water up a flight of stairs off the front main for six years and had mostly stopped saying anything about it in the first year.** The four standpipes on the canal front are on the front main and were not touched on the Friday night at all, and there are twenty-seven street clocks on the canal front and none of them is on this main, and the whole of the front went on running that night and nobody has ever noticed that before and nobody is going to notice it now.

The hospital branch was taken off the top end of that stopcock.

---

**The ward, from ten past ten. Eleven patients and three staff, the same eleven and the same three as in September.**

There was no alarm on the ward. There has never been an alarm on the ward; a hospital's water is not a ward's water, and the branch has no reserve of its own because a branch is a draw and not a supply, and that has been on a form since 1951 and the form is in a store.

Leda Vale was on and did the ordinary thing in the ordinary order. She got the free master and the docket and two watches and walked the ward. The free master is a bracket clock of 1949, eleven inches high, on the third-floor landing, and it is the only clock on that ward that was not on the branch, and it is inside a second a day and has been since the twentieth of July.

**Elsie Cobb was on. She is nineteen and she is the night sister and she is paid at the ward rate and she has asked twice to be named and has not been taken off, and she has a folded card in her apron with about seventy columns of pencil on it, and a column at the end with three lines in it and a note under the third in a different pencil.** She ruled the fourth line herself on the Saturday morning with a pen that skipped.

> *New col. 4th Nov. INTERVAL WORKED BY HAND. Night sister E. Cobb, ward rate.*
> *Line pressure lost 10.17 p.m. Restored 4.41 a.m.*
> *11 pts, 3 staff, 26 intervals worked off the free master at the bedside, each checked with two watches. **Worst single interval 9 min 20 s.***
> *Total hand time for the round, 10.17 p.m. to 4.41 a.m. — **6 h 24 min.***

Six hours and twenty-four minutes. **The ward's whole slack between free clocks and line clocks, across eleven patients and three staff, is forty minutes, and the figure forty minutes has been on that card since June, and six hours and twenty-four minutes is about ten times forty minutes.** Leda Vale said that out loud in the nurses' room on the Monday morning the seventh, in front of eleven people, and put her own name under it. That is the third time two names have gone under a figure in that ward in eleven years and nobody in that room argued with it.

---

**And the part of the night that was not a ward, which is a cellar, a lamp and an old man.**

Somebody had to find the stopcock. The ward is a mile and a quarter away and has a hand-wound bracket clock and eleven patients. Eleven clocks in the basements and at the low end go free when the driving water stops and have to be hand-wound, and about two hundred and eleven households had a standpipe go dry in about a minute.

The stopcock is under the floor of the cellar of number fourteen, Ferrywright Row. **The cellar has four feet of standing water in it since the fourth of May and has had since a young man went down into it in May with a lamp and found the water and wrote it on a folded piece of paper, which is item one on that paper, and the paper is in a young man's pocket and has been since May.**

**Nobody knocked on number fourteen, Ferrywright Row, at any point in the year before the Friday night, and nobody knocked on it on the Friday night either.** The reason it happened at all is that a man of twenty had a piece of paper in his pocket with that cellar on it, and a man of eighty-one in the top flat had said in a works' yard in the last week of September, to a question about a brass pointer: *yes, and there is no form, and there has not been one since 1951, and I am eighty-one and I have stopped writing.*

They went down at about eleven, and the light went at about eleven, and the last of it was a paraffin lamp with a bent handle, because there is no light in that stair's fanlight and has not been since about 1948, and the length was out of water and therefore out of electricity from about a quarter past ten.

**Alden Fenwick is eighty-one. He has been at the top of number fourteen since 1941, and there is no light in the fanlight, and he has a lamp, and he went down his own four flights into four feet of black water with a man of twenty holding the lamp above him and found the iron.**

It is a 1911 stopcock with a square head and about eleven inches of spindle, under four feet of water on the floor of a cellar, and it has not been moved since 1951, and the spindle is stiff, and the head is under six inches of iron, and there is one wrench in that cellar and it is the wrong wrench and it belonged to a man who has been dead since 1948.

They were in that cellar from about eleven until about half past two in the morning, and then Arden went up four flights and telephoned the district works' night man, who is a different building and four miles away, and a fitter came out with a pipe wrench and a lamp of his own.

**The stopcock was freed at about ten past four in the morning.** Pressure came back at **4.41 a.m.**, and the branch is two hours and one minute out of the reference and has been going at fifty seconds a night since the sixth of October, and a bed of eleven patients came off a free master of 1949 at the same minute.

The man of eighty-one was on his own landing for about an hour afterwards with a blanket over his knees, and he was not taken anywhere and was not examined by anybody, and he was asleep at about half past four.

**And on the Tuesday the eighth, in daylight, at about eleven in the morning, a works' fitter came with a ladder and freed it properly and drained the cellar into the street, and there is a card about it in the district works' yard with a date on it. The card does not say what it was for, because a card about a main records the main. What it says is *stopcock 4, Ferrywright Row, seized, freed*, and a stopcock that was seized is a stopcock that was shut for six years, and that is not a line item and it never will be, and the man who wrote it has not been asked what he thinks about it and does not think about it.**

---

**And the cost of the night is on the card, and it is the only place in this city where it is, and it is not a form.**

> *THE NIGHT OF THE FOURTH OF THE ELEVENTH. What it cost, and who paid it, and the fact that there is no column anywhere in this city for any of it and no form on which it could be entered.*
>
> ***The ward.*** E. Cobb, night sister, ward rate — **6 h 24 min.** L. Vale, sister — **4 h 50 min**, two rounds and the card. The third member of the staff, whose name is on the ward roster and not on this card — **4 h 10 min.** Ward total: **15 h 24 min**, across eleven patients and three staff, on a branch two hours and one minute out of the reference, and the ward's slack is forty minutes.
>
> ***Number fourteen, Ferrywright Row.*** A. Fenwick, 81 — **3 h 30 min**, from about eleven at night to half past two, in a cellar, in four feet of water, with the lamp held by somebody else. **Not on a duty. Not a shift. Not on a roster. There is no form in this city on which an eighty-one-year-old man in a cellar is a person doing anything.**
>
> ***The lamp.*** T. Arden, 20 — **4 h 30 min**, of which about two carrying a lamp in a cellar and the rest on a landing outside a door. He put the hours on the folded paper in his own hand and did not give them to anybody and nobody asked him for them.
>
> ***A standpipe at the second court.*** One pair of hands out of about nine, **2 h 30 min**, carrying water up a flight of stairs, and **there is a list of the names of that household in a laundry's book of 1951 and there is no list of their names anywhere since, and they are not on this card, because nobody took their names.**
>
> ***The low end.*** Eleven clocks free-running and hand-wound — **2 h 10 min** — by a night porter at Green Row whose name is on a works' maintenance line in the fourth-inch column with *hours as returned* against it, and by two others. **The night porter's hours were returned and a works' clerk entered them, and there is no column on the return for the reason a man was in a cellar and a basement at two in the morning, and he did not give one, because there is nowhere to give it.**
>
> ***Ivy Pike***, who signs for the Weir One gate and has kept a book of her own for eleven years and has never shown it to anybody — **2 h 5 min**, on the gate, because the gate is a gate whatever else it is.
>
> **Of the hours that could be counted: 30 h 9 min. It is a figure and it is not the cost. Nobody was hurt. Nobody is dead. There is no hero in it. Eleven patients got their medicine.**

He read the card in about nine minutes in a shop on Verge Lane, and he did not check any of it, and the reason he did not check it is on that wall in his own hand from the ninth of the tenth and it is the same reason: **if I check it then the only thing this district has ever produced by three ordinary people is a thing that had to be produced by me, and that was the argument in June and I was on the wrong side of it in June.**

---

**And then he did the one thing he has not done all year, which is to ask one question, and it was not a question about the night.**

He asked Alden Fenwick on the Sunday the sixth, at the top of four flights, in a front room with a lamp on the table, whether anybody had ever asked him about the cellar.

"**Twice,**" said Alden Fenwick. "You, in May. And a man from the water office in about 1953 who wanted to know whether I had noticed a smell, and I said no, and he wrote *no* on a card, and I have thought about that card for about four years and I have never worked out what it was for, and I am eighty-one and I have stopped writing."

"Did he ask about the stopcock."

"**I did not know there was a stopcock.** I knew there was a floor and water on it and I have put my hand on the bracket over it four times a day going down with a lamp since 1922, and I have wanted somebody to ask me about that bracket since 1951, and I said so in a works' yard in September and nobody has been to this flat since the second of the seventh except you, and the reason you came is that you wanted a brass thing put on a standpipe, and I told you that at the time and you have not improved on it."

He put the lamp on the table.

"You want to know what I want."

"I want to know whether you want anything said in writing. I have been told twice this year that a thing a man has written down is not an instrument, and I am trying to find out whether that is true, and you are the only man in this district I can ask, because you are the only one who has already refused me."

"**No,**" said Alden Fenwick. "**I do not want anything said in writing, and I have told you so four times and I will tell you a fifth, and the reason is the one I gave in the yard and it is not about the lamp.** My cellar has been under four feet of water since May and nobody has asked me about it, and a man of eighty-one who is asked about a cellar by a young man with a piece of paper in his pocket gets a card, and I have had a card and I have thought about it for four years. **I have got a lamp and a wick that wants doing and I came into this building in 1941 and I will be eighty-two in the spring and I am not going to be the reason there is a second card.**"

So there is no second card and there is no letter and the folded paper in a young man's pocket has one line on it and is not going to get a second, and the night of the fourth of the eleventh is in a nurses' room on a third-floor landing in Green Span in a column with a date on it and four names under it, and it is the only instrument in this city that has ever caught a cost, and it works, and it is a piece of card in an apron, and there is no form for it and it cannot be demanded and cannot be produced in a room and it is the reason a man of eighty-one came out of a cellar at half past two in the morning and slept, and it is not enough.
