# Chapter 2 — Non-Certified Standby

He had rehearsed it on the way down, which was a bad sign. He had a sentence about a low sound in a correct movement and a request that a qualified man look at the case, and he had decided to say it before Hask could offer him tea, because a man who is fed and comfortable says the useful thing an hour later.

The Clockwork Quarter guild hall occupied the ground floor of a building that had been somebody's warehouse in the first flood year, with the original loading doors bricked up and the stairs to the licensing floor worn into a curve. It smelled of paraffin, hot solder, and the particular dust of paper that people had been turning for fifty years. On the wall by the door was a board of honorables with dates, and under the board a rack of printed cards giving the reportable conditions in the trade, which Orrin had read so often he could recite the middle column, and which he never entirely agreed with.

Gerren Hask's office was the second door, and it was warm, and there was tea.

"Sit down, Orrin. You look like a man who has been awake thinking about a bell."

"I have a defect note."

"You have a face." Hask pushed the second cup across. The cup was good porcelain, chipped at the rim, and it had been in that office since before Orrin was apprenticed. "Say the note."

Orrin said the note. He said it well. He said that a public clock in the crown case was correct to a tenth of a second twice over on his gauge, and that on two separate counts he had felt a low pulse arrive from below the case, at the interval, and that the water under Cathedral Rise moved through pipes he had run out to Northglass and back and knew the layout of, and that a pipe full of moving water makes a noise, and that he would like somebody with a plate to listen to it.

Hask did not say no. That was the thing people who had not met him expected and the thing Orrin had never been able to explain to anyone. What Hask did was take his time, and be reasonable, and describe.

"A note goes in the book," he said. "A note in the book summons a hall engineer, and the hall engineer goes up on the morning tram, and the hall charges the hall, and the hall will not charge the Cathedral, so the hall charges the Quarter's rate fund, which means every clockmaker in the Quarter pays a little more into it next quarter. That's the money." He turned the cup a quarter turn on its saucer, which he did when he was arranging his thoughts. "The attention is worse. The Cathedral receives a note about its crown case and it doesn't send a man, it sends a letter. The letter says the case was certified on such-and-such by such-and-such, which is your name, and the letter asks whether the certifying maintainer withdraws the certificate."

"I would not withdraw it. It's correct."

"You wouldn't. But the letter makes an official somewhere decide whether you're the sort of man who hears things." Hask looked at him, and there was nothing unkind in the face; that was the difficulty. "The Quarter supplies clockwork to the Cathedral, the hospitals, the schools, and the waterworks. That is four customers who can go elsewhere and one that cannot. A letter about a bell is a letter about a bell, and then it is a second letter, and then it is a reputation with a shop's name at the top of it."

Orrin sat with that. He had known all of it before he walked in, in the abstract, and it had been different in the abstract.

"And anyway," Hask said, and here he was genuinely helpful, and that was worse. "The clock is right. Your own paper says it is right. A building that keeps its own accounts does not care for being told what it is carrying."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer there is until somebody gives me a defect." Hask sat back. "Which I do not think you have, and I would not file if you did, and I will say that as your master and not as your friend, because I have been your master for fifteen years and I have never once lied to you and I am not going to start over a noise."

It was true. That was the thing. Everything Hask said was true, and each true thing sat on top of the last until the actual question was somewhere underneath, and you would have to go and dig for it, and the dig would take a day, and the day would be a day without a defect note.

Hask had gone back to his desk while he was saying it, which was his habit, and which meant that the ledger was open in front of him with his hand on the corner of it, because Hask was a man who read while he talked and who had never in fifteen years pretended otherwise. It was the hall's labour book: ruled, quarto, a column of dates and a column of works and a column of hours against each, and the handwriting all the same, because the hall's hours were copied up fair for the tax man.

Orrin's eye went down the page the way an eye does when it is looking at a room it knows, which is to say without any decision in it at all. The dates, the works, the hours. And then, in the ninth line, in a different hand — smaller, and pressed harder, and slanting the wrong way for a copying clerk, and entered on a day when four lines above it the ink had a Sunday in it —

*Weir Two, night pumping, non-certified standby, 1 crew, 3 nights.*

Three nights. It was the only line in the column that was not the hall's hand, and it was the only line in twenty pages that said *crew* and a number, and Orrin had seen the phrase perhaps forty times in his life and had never once, until that second, asked what it was a *standby* to.

He did not ask. He noticed, at the level of a man noticing a wet floor, that he was not going to ask, and that Hask had put the book where a man could read it without reaching for it, and that this was not an accident, and that there was no way in the world to raise it now that would not sound like a man who had been reading over the guild master's shoulder.

"There is water in the third bay," Hask said. "Do not carry your case through it, it is grit and it will get into the escapement. Sit by the window if you are going to sit."

"Right," said Orrin.

Orrin thanked him for the tea and did not file the note.

He should have gone. He knew he should have gone, standing in the corridor with his hat in his hand, and he stayed eleven minutes longer because the hall was warm and it was raining and there was nothing on his list until the ropeworks, and the ropeworks would keep until eleven.

---

The hall floor had four long benches, two of them occupied, and a smell of hot metal that would follow him into his clothes for the night. The man at the third bench on the left was not one of the hall's people, and could not have been: he had the flat, unhurried hands of a man who did not repair things, and he was working at nothing at all, simply sitting with a cup and watching the room the way a man watches a floor he has to stand on eventually.

A girl at the licensing counter was stamping quarterly returns for a row of operators, which Orrin understood to be the part of the quarter that operators did not do themselves.

"Pell," she said, when the man showed her a seal. "Twelve years, is it, Mr Pell."

"Twelve and a bit."

She wrote it down without looking up, which told Orrin that operators were people the licensing counter did not have to be polite to, because they could not leave. He had known that without knowing it. Ivo Pell, he thought. Ivo Pell, who had come out of the trade twelve years ago with other people's ears in his hands.

Pell looked up, saw Orrin's shop coat and the calluses and the small case with the gauge cradle, and read the whole of him in a second and a half.

"You're the Crown Case man," he said.

"Yesterday."

"Then you didn't hear it."

"No."

"Course you didn't." Pell turned his cup. "Listen to a bell once a year and call it a condition. That's what the trade does. A bench man's ear isn't an instrument, it's a trade habit." He said it without malice, the way a man reads out a rule he has learned to enforce. "You'll get to be good at it. I've watched it happen twice. Never once watched it be worth anything."

"I certify things," Orrin said.

"You keep time," said Pell, and went back to his tea, and the girl at the counter put a date on his return and gave it back to him, and he got up and went out into the rain without finishing it, and that was the whole of Ivo Pell, and Orrin stood there with his hand still half-raised to a man who had already left.

---

Under the window at the end of the hall floor was the case.

It had been there since Orrin was fourteen. Six feet of black frame and four inches of glass, a guild crew photograph, and foxing in the emulsion in a pattern like weather, and a small printed card beneath it in the guild's own type, which read, in full:

*LONG NINE. Night of the storm. Shift 4 / 6.*

No names. No ranks, no stations, no posts, no numbers but the shift, and the date was not on the card either, only the night, because everyone in Morrowfen had been born knowing the date.

Orrin stood in front of it the way he had stood in front of it perhaps a thousand times, and counted faces, because that is what you did. Eleven men and two women in oilskins on a stair of old iron, and behind them the shape of a wall with a hole in it, and the hole was a floodgate and the stair was the outside of the Long Nine works and it was dark and the flash had caught nine of them at once so that all the faces were half-lit and no face was doing anything particular.

He found his father the way he always found his father, which is to say that he did not. There were eleven men and he had one photograph and a childhood, and the childhood did not come with a caption. He had gone through this frame before: the third from the left with the upturned collar, the one at the far right with his hand on the rail. Both of them had been his father, on different days, when he was ten, and he had learned a long time ago to stop choosing.

Twenty-two faces, and two hundred and twelve dead, and those two numbers had never had anything to do with each other on paper. The guild had published its liability annex, somewhere, at some point, and the annex was about the night, and the annex was about the hours, and the hours had stopped being worked on the night of the Long Nine. Orrin had read the annex once at seventeen and had understood none of it, and had understood, without knowing that he understood it, that a hall that has to say *the hours stopped* is a hall that is about to have an inquiry.

He counted the faces once more, and went back upstairs.

---

Hask had the regulator out when he came back, wrapped in a guild cloth and shaped like something that had been alive.

"Sit down," Hask said. "I want your eye and then I want you to do me a small thing."

The cloth came off. It was a governor, and it was a good one: a hand-turned brass body about the size of a fist, a weighted sleeve, a toothed quadrant, a cut pin, a hairline, and on the back a stamped plate with the guild's mark and a number and a date in a year that Orrin had to look at twice before he let it out of his face.

Eighteen years. It was eighteen years old, and it was in the state a governor is in when it has been stopped in the middle of its travel and has stayed there, which is a state that no fault in the world produces, because a fault lets go.

"Look at the pin," Hask said.

Orrin looked at the pin. The pin was a stop pin and it was engaged, and the teeth it engaged were the quadrant's, and the engagement was not where a fault-driven stall would leave it. He turned the body a quarter in the light, and then he put his thumbnail against the flank of the sleeve where a man working fast would have braced a lever.

"Dent," he said. "Two of them. Here, and — here. That's a jaw off a bar."

"That's a jaw off a bar."

"That's not a fault," Orrin said. "A thing like that doesn't come off a machine, Hask. It comes off a man holding the machine still." He turned it again, and his own hands knew it before his mouth did; he had thrown stops like it himself, thirty times, on live work, with a bar braced against the casting and a count in his head. "This was stopped in travel. On purpose. By somebody who knew what travel position meant, and set it here on purpose, and then left it here."

He was aware, halfway through the sentence, that Hask had stopped moving entirely, which was the first time he had seen the man sit still while another person was talking.

"That's a regulator from a decommissioned assembly," Hask said.

"It's a regulator from the Long Nine, because the plate is the Long Nine series, and if you want me to write that down you'll have to say it yourself."

"I'll say what I need said." Hask put one flat hand on the cloth. "Reduce it. Bench it to scrap, take the brass out of the quadrant, and leave the body in the bin. Don't enter it in the books. There's no customer, no fault report, no machine to attach it to, and if you enter it I have to explain it to a tax man."

"I can't scrap a regulator with a cut pin."

"You can scrap anything with a jaw mark, Orrin, it's a lever, that's all it is."

"No." He heard his own voice go flat in a way he did not like. "No, I can't, and I'd like to know why you need it binned in a way I can't explain."

"Because the alternative is that it stays in this room in a drawer, and this room is a room people come into, and you are asking me a great deal for a man's dignity at half past eight in the morning." Hask's voice stayed pleasant all the way through, which was the frightening part. "You can do it and not record it. That is what I am offering. Not a bribe. There is no paper for it and there is nothing to be found later, because I will not have written it and you will not have written it, and that is precisely why it is safe for you."

For eleven seconds Orrin looked at the dented flank of that governor, and at the pin, and at the state a thing is left in when a man with a licence and a bar stops it halfway so that it can be started again by a hand that knows where it is.

"If I scrap it," he said, "I'll do it here, in the hall, on the anvil, and I'll enter it, as unaccounted stock, under today's date."

Hask's mouth got smaller. "That is not what I asked."

"No," Orrin said. "It isn't."

He put the governor in his case. He felt, going out down the worn stair, that he had won something, and by the time he was in the lane he understood that he had done the other thing: he had taken eighteen years of somebody's last deliberate act out of a drawer and put it in a case in his own shop where he could look at it whenever he liked, which was a thing nobody had asked him to do and nothing he could file.

He filed nothing. He had asked nobody for anything, in public, all morning, and he had walked out with a piece of the Long Nine in a case he was not supposed to be carrying.

---

The call came at half past four, at the bench, from a boy on the Quarter's speaking tube with his hand over the mouthpiece, which is why the message was unintelligible twice and the third time, shouted, came out as: certified clock four minutes fast, pump underneath it, Lower Weirs, Weir Two, first thing in the morning, and the hall is sending you because you did the cathedral this week and you were the cheapest one who would come.

Orrin put the tube down and looked at the case on the shelf, and at the grey ledger behind it, and at the rain, which had not stopped all day and had not once been mentioned by a single person in the guild hall, the Cathedral, or the city.

Then he went and got his coat.
