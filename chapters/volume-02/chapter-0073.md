# Chapter 73 — A Certificate Says Within Tolerance

There is a form in this city that says a machine is all right, and it is one of about a hundred and forty years old, and it is correct, and the man who fills it in is not asked what time it is. The question a clockmaker brought to the licensing floor of the Guild of Horologists on Monday the fifth of the ninth month was not about that form. It was about a second form, in a different building, on a different subject, printed about forty years later, which has been saying out loud since about 1938 exactly what the first one has refused to say since 1883.

---

**Monday the fifth, ten past ten in the morning, at the counter, and the clerk gave it to him in four minutes and then refused to do the rest of it.**

He had it written on one sheet and he did not put it on the counter, which was the correct thing to do and which she noticed.

"Ask it," she said.

"**How many certificates of condition issued in this city in the last twelve months say *within tolerance* about a machine that is more than an hour out of the district reference?**"

"That is a good question and I am not going to answer it and here is why, in one sentence, and then I will tell you the half that is on my side of it."

"Because the hall does not keep the offsets."

"Because **the hall does not keep the offsets.** The offset is in the inspector's column, and the inspector's column is in the certificate, and the certificate is in the works' file or the district's file or the hospital's file, and the hall sees the certification and the certification is four words and a signature. We do not hold the offsets. Nobody in this building has ever held an offset."

"Then somebody does."

"Somebody does, in about nine places, and I could name them if you wanted nine names and you would not be able to do anything with nine names, and that is the whole of the difficulty and it has been the whole of the difficulty for a hundred and forty years." She looked at him. "**Mr Vale, I gave you four things about that signal's certificate in August and I have thought about them since, and I am going to say the fifth, and I have never said it to anybody, and I am saying it to you because you are the ninth person in about two years who has gone at this and the other eight went at it as a trick.**"

He waited.

"**A certificate of condition is a piece of paper that a person relies on.** That is the whole of it. Not a report, not a return, not an observation. A thing a man gives his wife. And the reason the offset is not in the certification column is not a mistake and it is not the printer's fault, and it is about a hundred and forty years old and it is right: **if the offset went into the certification, then a certificate would be a statement that a machine is wrong about the city, and a machine is not wrong about the city, it is a machine, and a man cannot be given a certificate that says his own city's clock is an hour and a half away from his.**" She put her hands flat. "So it is in the next column. And the next column is where observations go. And you cannot get an observation acted on in this city, and there is no form for it, and there has not been one since 1883, and the reason there has not been one is that the hall asked for one in 1891 and was refused by a committee which was right, because a hall that can act on an observation about a district can be asked to certify a district and a district is not a machine."

"That is the best answer anybody has given me this year," said Orrin Vale.

"It is not an answer, it is a reason, and you will find it is the reason for about four other things this city does, and it is a hundred and forty years old and nobody remembers the committee's name."

---

**And then she told him the half that was on her side, and it took ninety seconds, and it is the finding.**

"**The hall does publish something. Since the first of the sixth, the standing orders require every certificate issued by a master to be countersigned by a holder of the first class, and a list of them to be published at the counter, monthly, and the name of the countersigning holder to go at the foot of the sheet.**"

"There is a board."

"There is a board. Four feet by three. It has been there since the first of the sixth." She said it carefully. "**I want you to understand what that list is, because nobody in this city has ever understood it and it is the only public record of its kind anywhere. Every line is a machine that somebody in this hall has certified. The countersigner's name is at the foot, so you know which holder stood behind which line. Nineteen lines in a month, some months nine, and the hall will tell you it runs to about a hundred and forty in a year, and those two figures have never been reconciled in this building and it is not my job.**"

"Which means I can count the machines."

"Yes. **You can count what has been certified. You cannot count what they are out by, and the two numbers live in two buildings and have never been on the same table, and that has been the case since 1883, and the reason it has never been on the same table is that this hall is a hall of three hundred and forty people and a counter and it is not anybody's business what the city is doing.**"

He stood at the counter for a minute.

"Who put the board up?"

"He did. Gerren Hask. On the first of the sixth, about half an hour after he suspended you." She said it without any weight at all, which was the correct way to say it. "**He is under review and he certifies nothing alone and his class is determined by the licensing floor and it is published, and it is a list, and it is there for the rest of his life, and about nine people a week read the whole of it.** Write to him."

---

**Tuesday the sixth, and the three sheets, and the count of machines.**

He copied them out at the bench between jobs. Three sheets, June, July and August, cream, a printed head, a rule, and the lines in two hands because the countersignature is in a different hand from the signature, and the countersigner's name at the foot of each sheet in a third.

**Fifty-four lines in three months.**

He went through them with a pencil, because that is all there is to do with a list and a pencil, and he was careful and he was slow and he did it in the order the lines were in and he did not sort them.

> *Thirty-one of the fifty-four are certificates of condition on a machine that belongs to somebody: nineteen public and bracket clocks of the district, seven signals of the tramways, two station clocks of a pumping district, one master clock of a hospital, and two things he could not place and did not pretend to.*
> *The rest are something else: shop clocks brought in by their owners, turret clocks, a jeweller's bench regulator, four regulators in a brewery, a mill. Those are not civic machines and the clause does not touch them and he marked them out and said so.*
> *All thirty-one carry an inspector's column. **All thirty-one have an observed offset from the district reference written in it, in figures, in ink, by a man with a watch, on a day.**

And that was the first thing he had not known, and it was the whole of the count, and it took him about four minutes to write down.

**He had spent five months assuming the offsets were not kept. They are kept. Every one of them is kept, in ink, by a named inspector, on a stated day, in about nine buildings in this city, and there are between a hundred and two hundred and forty new ones a year, and the reason nobody had ever counted them is not that they are not there. It is that nobody has ever had a reason.**

**And of the thirty-one, six carry an offset of more than one hour.**

Six. Not six machines out of thirty-one in a fortnight of a bad month. Six out of thirty-one, and the sheets are June, July and August, and the schedule in force for the whole of those three months was the two-cycle variation, and the step had stopped on the second of the eighth, and **these six had been somewhere else in this city for eleven years.**

> *1. **A public clock at the second court, Bellweather Road — 1 h 36 min 40 s.** Certified in June. On the line.*
> *2. **The public clock at the works' gate, Northglass — 2 h 29 min 20 s.** Certified in July. On the line. **And the gate clock at Weir One, a mile and a half the other way, is 2 h 29 min 20 s, and neither of those two figures has ever been written next to the other and they are the same figure and they are both true.***
> *3. **A public clock in the Clockwork Quarter, not on any line — 1 h 12 min 00 s**, and it has been that figure for about eleven years, and about thirty or forty people a day set their watches by it.*
> *4. **A tramways signal, Northglass line, at the far end — 1 h 12 min 40 s.** Certified in June.*
> *5. **A bracket clock on a hospital outpatients' annexe, Green Span — 1 h 15 min 20 s.** Certified in August. **It is the same clock the ward stopped using in March 1951 and put in a store, and it is still in use on a certificate and nobody in that hospital has looked at it since the first of the fourth.***
> *6. **The signal at the Green Span terminus — 1 h 12 min 40 s**, in a certificate dated the third of the seventh, which is the sixth line of the July sheet.*

Every one of the six is certified *within tolerance, tolerance five seconds*. Every one of the six was within five seconds of its own local beat on the day a man with a watch wrote it down.

The sixth is the signal at the Green Span terminus, and Orrin Vale had walked past the sixth line of the July sheet on about forty occasions in about ten weeks, and the certificate says:

> *Timing checked against the signal's own local beat. **Within tolerance.** Tolerance, five seconds.*
> *District works' inspectorate. Observed offset from the district reference at time of examination: 1 h 12 min 40 s.*

And the certificate is dated the third of the seventh and the offset in it is what the branch was on the third of the seventh, and the branch is now 1 h 36 min 50 s out, and the certificate is still true, because the signal is still within five seconds of its own local beat and its own local beat has gone out by another twenty-four minutes and ten seconds along with everything else on the line.

**He wrote that down and then he sat on the bench for a while and did not do anything else.**

---

**And on the Wednesday morning he went and read the two men, and the two men are the whole of what this city has instead of an answer.**

The second-class holder who signed that certificate on the third of the seventh is a man of about fifty-eight who has a shop and a bench in the Sloughs, eleven miles off, on the other side of the river, in a district where the district works' engineer has never in his life been asked to look at a signal.

**He has never been to Northglass. He does not know what the Northglass line is. He said so, in about eleven words, at a counter, on the Wednesday morning, and then he was annoyed about it and said nine more.**

"I get a book with a description on it and a box of figures and I go and I time it against itself and I write down what I saw, and I have been doing it since I was thirty-one and I have never had anybody ask me a single question about the outside of it." He shut the book. "**I will tell you what I said in my own hand at the bottom of that one. I said the beat is good to within the tolerance and the beat is what I am paid to certify. If you want the city written on my certificate you want a different hall and there is not one.**"

And the first-class holder who countersigned it, in accordance with the standing orders of the first of the sixth month, came into this city on the bus on the third of the seventh, countersigned nine certificates that month, went back on the bus the same evening, and does not know one of the nine.

He is a holder of the first class of fifty-two. He has a room over a shop and about four certificates to countersign a month, and nine was a good month, and he did them on a bench in a borrowed room with a lamp and a watch and the originals in front of him, and he checked the arithmetic and the plate and the maker and the tolerance, and he checked all of it properly, and he countersigned nine certificates in about four hours.

He has never been to Northglass either. He does not know that the sixth of the nine is a signal, or that there is a hospital on the line, or that the man whose book he checked in July at about half past six in the evening on a Friday did the sixth of the nine in about nine minutes and went to his tea.

**Both of them did it correctly. Every word of both certificates is true. The tolerance is five seconds and they kept it to five seconds and they were entitled to certify what they certified and they could not have certified anything else without a different hall and a different ordinance and about forty years.**

---

**And on the Thursday the eighth of the ninth month there were nine people in the outer room of the pumping ward on the canal road, and the finding was put on the wall, and it is the whole of the finding and it is on the wall in chalk for anybody to rub out.**

Bevin Sarr wrote it in chalk because that is what the wall is for, and she is thirty-three and she has a glass case in that room and a key on a string and a night book in a pump house four doors away, and she is not the only person in this city who can be relied on to write a number on a wall and neither of them is thanked.

The one-line change is one line, and it is this, and it is to be added to the certification clause of a certificate of condition:

> ***And shall state the offset from the district reference at the time of examination, that offset being an observation and is not a certification.***

"**And that is the whole of the change,**" said Orrin Vale, "and it is already law in another building. The tramways ordinance, section four, since about 1938: no service carrying the public shall be entered on a public timetable unless four timings in the running of the service, taken against the district reference, agree to within five seconds. **The same five seconds. The same number. The same city. Two pieces of paper about forty years apart about different subjects.** And the tram's paper asks for the city and the hall's paper does not, and nobody has ever been able to say why, and it is not because anybody wanted it that way."

**And then they did the part that took ninety minutes, which is who loses it, and there were three answers and the third was his and it was the worst one.**

**One. The hall.** A second number on every certificate is a number about the city, and a hall that publishes numbers about the city is a hall that will be asked to act on them, and the committee of 1891 was right. The hall would also lose the only thing it has: nineteen lines a month on a board four feet by three, entirely correct, entirely about machines. **Put a second column on it and it becomes a list of complaints with a hall's name at the foot of it, and in about four years it will be a document somebody uses against somebody.**

**Two. The district works' inspectorate.** Its column would stop being an observation and become the certification, and the inspectorate reports to a works, and the works' return would carry about a hundred and forty numbers a year about machines on other people's lines, and the works' return goes to an inspectorate, and there is no version of that in which a works' man is glad.

**Three. The Lower Weirs.** Orrin Vale said it and nobody argued with him and he had it worked out twice before he came in.

"If the offset goes on the certificate, then this branch's figure goes on about thirty certificates a year in nine buildings in ink, with a date and an inspector's name, and **that is the only number in this city about this district that anybody could act on. And the only instrument anybody has for acting on it is the number of pumping cycles. And in October the number of pumping cycles is a matter for nine men with a property qualification, and in the spring it is a matter for a rate notice on a ward, and the notice carries a number and no name.**"

He put his hand flat on the wall under the six figures, which have not moved since the second of the eighth.

"So I am going to say the third thing and then I am going to stop, and I would like it minuted, in whatever this room's equivalent is. **The reason nobody in this city has objected to the Lower Weirs is that our error is not written down anywhere a person can act on. It is in a wall in chalk and in a return in a file and in a card in a nurses' room, and a rate became a figure on the second of the August and a figure is not a thing anybody can vote on, and that has been the only thing standing between this district and an October for eleven weeks.** And you have just asked me to write it down in thirty places a year, and I am not going to, and the reason I am not going to is that the number of pumping cycles would go back to four, and the step would come back, and the far end of that branch would be an hour further out by the spring, and **you would be asking me to write down, in thirty places a year, the exact figure that four hundred men will be asked to stand in front of, on a road, at half past six in the morning, in October.**"

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That is the third one," said Bevin Sarr, "and you have said it on my board and I am not going to rub it out, and I am going to say the thing nobody in this room is going to say, which is that you have just described the only instrument anybody has ever found for stopping a district being charged for something, and it is a gap in a form, and you have found it by accident, and you have found it by accident in the ninth month while a foundry is standing at a gate four miles up the canal."

"Yes," said Orrin Vale.

"Then I am not going to say anything about that either. Chalk it if you want it. I do four hand-windings a year."

---

**And the hall's own answer came back on the Friday the ninth, in writing, on a sheet of the hall's paper with a form number on it, and it is eleven words, and it is correct.**

> *The hall does not make the forms.*

And underneath, in a second hand, which is the hand of the clerk who told him the fifth thing on the Monday and who has signed nothing in this district all year:

> *The certification clause was made by the Assembly in 1883 and the hall has asked no body to alter it since. The hall's view is that a hall which proposes a change to the paper it certifies on has become an instrument and not a hall. The hall would rather be wrong about a city than be right about a form.*

And under that, at the foot, a third paragraph, in the hand of Gerren Hask, who is a repairer under public oversight and who may not certify alone and whose class is determined by the licensing floor:

> *You asked me whether the list is the only public record of its kind. It is. It was put up on the first of the sixth because a standing order required it and I did it in four minutes and I have not thought about it since.*
> *It is a list. **A list is not a shield and it is not a sword and it does not put anybody's hand up.** You have read three months of it off the wall with a pencil and got a number out of it in an afternoon, and I am told what the number is by a man in the Clockwork Quarter, and I am not going to be asked about the number, and if I am asked about the number I will say that the list does not contain the offsets and that the list never claimed to.*
> *I have read your letter. I have not been thanked for the board this year and I am not going to be thanked for it now and if anybody in your district says you got the count from me you are to correct them in writing, because a correct use of a list is not a favour.*

---

**And on the Friday evening the last thing that happened about it happened in a shop on a hill, and it is a word, and it is not a permit.**

He got the narrow book out and wrote the order of operations for a job on Monday, in eleven lines, because he does that now, and then he wrote this on the back page of the grey ledger where the first of the sixth is:

> *Ninth of the ninth. Thirty-one certificates of condition on civic machines in three months. Six of them over an hour. Thirty-one offsets in ink in nine buildings and no reason until this week to want any of them.*
> *The change is one line and the line is already law in the tramways ordinance, and I have written it out and I am not going to send it, and the reason I am not going to send it is on the wall in the pumping ward in chalk in my own hand and it is the third of the three and it is the district's own error and it is the only thing this district has got.*
> *And a thing like that one line is called a directive, and I know the word because I have read about four of them in my life and never seen one, and a directive says what a thing is. **It is not a permit. There is no permit in this city, and if there were one it would be a piece of paper that lets a person ask a question without the rule, and the rule is the only reason the machine has never once been wrong, and a woman of twenty-six told me that at a counter on the eleventh of the seventh and I wrote it down and I have not stopped writing it down.***
> *I want the word on this page because in about four years somebody is going to try and do it with a directive instead of a vote, and it will work, and everybody in the room will be pleased, and it will be a way of asking the question without the rule and I will not have been there.*
