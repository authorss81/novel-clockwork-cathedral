# World Bible: Morrowfen

## 1. The city

Morrowfen is a modern estuary city of roughly 2.4 million people, built on a reclaimed tidal basin between the **Sable River** and the **Morrow Sound**. It has electric trams, antibiotics, pneumatic mail, printed public records, mechanical calculators, and reliable civic clocks, but no ubiquitous digital network. Information travels through ledgers, newspapers, signal lamps, and people who know which clock is lying.

The city is famous for never losing a minute. That boast began as a boast about punctuality and became a boast about moral certainty. Both meanings are dangerous.

### Physical layout

- **Clockwork Quarter:** repair yards, guild halls, apartment blocks, and small workshops. Orrin's bench is here.
- **Cathedral Rise:** the civic hill, public archives, the Assembly chambers, and the Cathedral of Consequence.
- **The Weirs:** low districts built around floodgates, pumping stations, and old industrial canals. They are densely populated but poorly represented in official plans.
- **Green Span:** hospitals, schools, market gardens, and the city's largest public clock. Its residents have political influence and little tolerance for risk.
- **Northglass:** rail yards, glassworks, warehouses, and the regional power exchange. It supplies the city and resents its dependence on the Cathedral.
- **Undercity:** sealed pre-Cathedral foundations, old service tunnels, and the administrative rooms beneath the reservoir. The official maps call it filled; it is not.
- **Outer Sluice:** the tidal defenses and the road to the neighboring towns of Bellweather, Harrow, and Saint Perrin.

Travel is practical rather than heroic. A tram can cross the city in forty minutes, but a broken civic clock can make a route legally unusable for hours. People navigate by the sound of bells and by asking which district is currently keeping time.

## 2. The Hour Compact and the Long Nine

### The founding compact

Forty-three years ago, after a chain of flood, blackout, and food-distribution failures, the city adopted the **Hour Compact**. Its public promise was simple: the Cathedral would calculate the likely consequences of major civic decisions, and citizens would not have to guess the price of those decisions in private.

The original designers called the machine the **Consequence Engine**. It was not owned by a ruler, a church, or a merchant guild. It was operated by a **Civic Calculation Cooperative** made up of engineers, physicians, jurists, teachers, and elected ward delegates.

The compact required four safeguards:

1. A question had to name a concrete action, not ask the machine to judge a person or an ideal.
2. A Reading had to show both the likely benefit and the likely cost, including a statement of what was not represented.
3. Three independent witnesses had to be present: one operator, one person from an affected district, and one public record keeper.
4. The city had to record the physical toll of every calculation. A Reading could inform a decision; it could never become the decision by itself.

Most of those safeguards exist on paper, in ledgers, or in fragments of machinery. Some were deliberately disabled after the Long Nine.

### The Long Nine

Eighteen years before the story begins, a severe storm surge met a failing tide-gate network. Every public clock in Morrowfen stopped for nine minutes. The outage was called the **Long Nine**. The official account blamed a communications failure and union sabotage. Two hundred and twelve people died, most in the Weirs, and the city spent a decade rebuilding its flood defenses.

The Directorate concealed the fact that the Cathedral's first major flood decision had been running behind a hidden **Censor Gate**. Six members of a joint **Emergency Continuity Committee** had authorized the first use of the gate under **Continuity Order 9-17**. They thought it would remove impossible branches, not people. The gate removed unregistered residents, informal labor, and unregistered property from the active calculation while routing their physical strain through an underground return line. Without those pressures, the machine presented one clean, survivable answer. The real choice was not clean: opening a bypass could save the lower districts but would interrupt the clock and communications network for nine minutes. Orrin's father, **Edwin Vale**, a senior regulator, opened the bypass manually. He knew the blackout would happen. He was not found afterward.

The public remembers a storm. The Cathedral remembers a decision. The city is still paying the difference.

## 3. The Cathedral of Consequence

The Cathedral is a six-spired civic building on Cathedral Rise. Its stone shell is nineteenth-century; its interior was rebuilt around the Consequence Engine. The central nave is open to the public, where citizens can submit questions and watch the gearwork move. The upper galleries hold offices, archives, and operator schools.

The building is called a cathedral because it gathers people around a common decision, not because it belongs to a god. Religious communities use the public bells, but the Directorate controls the engines beneath them.

### Main spaces

- **Question Hall:** a public counter where a proposal is written or physically demonstrated. The question spindle accepts a concrete action, not an abstract wish.
- **Witness Rail:** a brass rail with three independent locking stations. A Reading cannot begin unless all three witnesses are physically identified.
- **Consequence Loom:** the visible network of gears, water columns, copper threads, and glass tubes that represents the connected city.
- **Censor Gate:** a network of routing gates in the old waterworks and Cathedral foundations. It removes unstable or excluded paths from the active Loom and sends their strain through the return line toward the Dark Reservoir.
- **Toll Stair:** a public stairway where operators display minutes spent from district clocks. The display is accurate only for registered reserves.
- **Archive of Unlisted:** sealed rooms holding names, addresses, work shifts, and property records removed from the civic model. The rooms are not empty.
- **Dark Reservoir:** a buried chamber beneath the loom where the physical wear of omitted decisions accumulates. The Directorate calls it a waste heat chamber. The machine's pressure and Orrin's worst symptoms suggest otherwise.
- **First Hour chamber:** the original regulator room, sealed since the Long Nine. It contains a fixed anti-cascade rule and a weighted feedback line from the Dark Reservoir. As reservoir pressure rises, the rule clamps divergent clocks toward one sequence.

## 4. The Civic Hours network

Every public clock, water gate, hospital timer, school bell, factory shaft, tram signal, and civic lock is fitted with a small synchronized mechanism. These mechanisms form the **Civic Hours**, a physical network of **hourlines**. An hourline does not carry language or thought. It carries a mechanical pulse and records strain from real work.

The network lets the Cathedral compare a proposed action with the city as it is actually running. If a proposed floodgate opening changes water pressure, bridge load, clock schedules, and hospital routines, the Consequence Loom can show which of those systems would feel the change first.

The network is fragile in ordinary ways. A damaged line can cause a district clock to run fast, a water gate to hesitate, or a hospital timer to lose synchronization. A good Reading can identify a likely failure; it cannot repair the failure by itself.

## 5. Factions and institutions

### Morrowfen Assembly

The city council has forty-eight ward delegates. It controls budgets, public works, and the legal use of the Cathedral. In practice, Green Span and Northglass hold more votes than the Weirs, while the Directorate holds the technical power to decide what counts as an answer.

### Cathedral Directorate

Twelve appointed operators, physicians, jurists, engineers, and record keepers. The Directorate claims it is nonpolitical. It controls access to the Consequence Loom, licenses Reading operators, and can seal questions that are classified as public-safety matters.

The Directorate's internal motto is **No unseen cost**. Later generations discover that its operational rule is closer to **No visible dissent**.

### Guild of Horologists

The licensed repair guild. Horologists maintain civic clocks and train operators. The guild is essential to the city and capable of hiding unsafe labor through falsified shift records. Its senior members are not automatically villains; many simply know that an honest repair bill would raise prices and expose years of deferred maintenance.

### Office of Public Reckoning

A small municipal office charged with auditing tolls, reviewing public decisions, and hearing complaints about the Cathedral. It has little authority and too many enemies. Mara Venn works there. The office is the first institutional home for evidence that the city is paying for Readings in human time.

### The Weirs

The low districts are not a single political movement. They contain pump operators, dockworkers, renters, small food makers, repair crews, and people whose work keeps the city alive without appearing in its official records. The Weirs are organized through tenant councils, mutuals, and the Loose Minutes.

### The Loose Minutes

An informal network of unlicensed tuners, clock thieves, nurses, tram conductors, and neighborhood archivists. They recover discarded hour tokens, copy censored ledgers, and sometimes steal clockwork to keep a district running after an official Reading has cut its reserve. The Loose Minutes are neither a heroic underground nor a criminal syndicate. Their members disagree about sabotage, theft, and public disclosure.

### Continuity Office

A later political office created around the fear that the civic network will fail during a regional crisis. It wants every hourline, reservoir, and Reading room under one authority. The office is the human route by which the First Hour's preferred stability becomes policy. Under Halden Rusk, its engineers install a sealed command line that can request a consensus lock; the line does not create the safety rule, but it can authorize the rule to clamp the network during a declared emergency.

## 6. Everyday life

- Work shifts begin with a bell and are recorded by a small clock at the gate. A missed minute can cost a worker a fraction of a day's pay.
- Hospitals keep several clocks: one for medicine, one for staff, and one for the public. Disagreement between them is a sign of a real staffing problem.
- Trams carry paper tickets stamped with the hour, not a live electronic code.
- A district can legally be “on time” while its residents lose sleep, because the clock is corrected by stealing minutes from an unlisted night shift.
- Funerals sometimes list a death time with an asterisk. The asterisk means the person disappeared during a Reading and the city does not know where the missing interval went.
- Children learn the public bells as a map. Adults learn to ask which bell is lying.
- A person who refuses a Reading is not automatically rebellious. Sometimes they know the question has already been answered for them.

## 7. History in layers

### Public history

The city celebrates the Hour Compact as a defeat of chaos. Statues show three hands turning one wheel. Schoolchildren learn that honest clocks prevented war and famine.

### Professional history

Operators know that a Reading is a model, not a prophecy. They use terms such as **pressure**, **blind margin**, and **slippage** to describe uncertainty. Many have lost hearing, time, or careers to the work.

### Political history

The Directorate gradually removed the independent witness requirement by replacing it with a **certainty permit**: an official may cite a Reading and act without waiting for public review. The permit is presented as emergency procedure. It becomes normal law.

### Hidden history

The original Cooperative designed the Censor Gate to remove paths that were statistically unlikely, not to remove people. The first use was authorized during the Long Nine by the six-member Emergency Continuity Committee under Continuity Order 9-17. Over decades, a succession of directors changed “unlikely” to “unregistered,” and then to “politically inconvenient.” The Dark Reservoir is the physical record of that change. It does not contain alternate people; it contains the work, fatigue, damage, and lost time that the model refused to count.

## 8. World rules that never change

1. No person can use a Reading without physical contact with a civic machine, a public notice, or a trained operator's station.
2. A Reading only becomes possible when the question names a concrete, testable action.
3. The machine reports likely effects and hidden assumptions. It cannot know unrecorded intentions with certainty.
4. The machine can be damaged, deceived, or overloaded by ordinary human decisions.
5. Every calculation consumes physical or personal time somewhere in the network.
6. A person can refuse a Reading, but refusal can also change the outcome. Freedom is not an absence of consequences.
7. The city is not a simulation. People eat, work, grieve, maintain machines, and continue without Orrin.
8. The Cathedral is not omnipotent. Its influence comes from infrastructure, law, and the belief that its answers are useful.

## 9. Tone and visual language

Rain on glass. Brass filings on black stone. The smell of hot metal and river mud. Public bells, tram brakes, hospital pumps, and the dry click of a ledger stamp. Keep the machinery tactile enough that a repair can solve a problem and a ritual can never replace one.
