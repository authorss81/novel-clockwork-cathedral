# Chapter 82 — The Last Person Who Knows How a Night Shift Was Certified

Gerren Hask certified a night shift in the maintenance-deferred class for nineteen years and did it in a book that is now published in full at the counter of his own hall for the price of the paper, and on Thursday the twentieth of the tenth month a clockmaker of thirty-two read that book from the front, in a public room, over three afternoons, without asking the man one question, and found at the front of it a printed slip and pinned inside the front cover a second slip in a master's own hand that says *I have read all of it. Do not ask me about it. I will not say* — and the finding of the three afternoons is not about a man, and the man is not a martyr, and nobody thanked him, and he was never in a room.

---

The hall's counter is open from nine and the labour book is on a lectern at the end of it, unbound, in nine parts, series 1 to 9, eighteen years, about a thousand pages, and it is sold at a price that covers paper, which is one shilling and twopence, and it is public, and it has been public since the first of the sixth, and on the Thursday the twentieth of the tenth month at about half past nine in the morning there was one man in the room and a clerk and the book.

He read it the way he reads a movement, in the order the parts are connected, and not in the order that is convenient.

**Part one, series 1.** The columns are: date; site; class; heads; time on; time off; countersignature. There is no column for what the work was. **That is the first thing about the book and it is not an omission in it; a shift in this district is a set of hours and a class and a price and a man, and there is nowhere in this book to write what the hours were for, and there never has been, and that is why a book of about a thousand pages of eighteen years of night work in a district of a hundred and forty thousand people has never once been cited in a room.**

He read every line. It took him the Thursday, the Friday and the Monday, from half past nine to about one, and the clerk refilled his cup twice and did not ask him what he was looking for, because she is a clerk of that counter and he has been in that room before and he is a person who buys things and reads them.

Here is what the three afternoons produced, and it is a list and not an argument, and every line of it is a fact in a printed book.

> **The maintenance-deferred class was created by this hall in 1931 and abolished by a standing order of this hall on the first of July of this year.** The standing order is minuted. It is four lines. It was moved by the master of the hall and seconded by one holder of the first class and it carried, and the fourth line is the operative one: *that any site entered in the maintenance-deferred class shall be entered in the ordinary class or not at all, and that no site in this district is of neither, and that this order is not to be varied except by a further standing order of this hall.*
> **There has been no further standing order of this hall since the first of July.** The list of standing orders is published at the counter on a sheet and it has nineteen lines on it and the last of them is the twenty-eighth of June.

> **The certification form for the class is a printed form of this hall, edition four, and the hall's forms register — which is a list, a printed list, on a card at the counter, and anybody may read it — carries a line against edition four that reads *superseded, 1st July, see standing order 20*.** There is no edition five. There is no reprint of edition four. **The hall has not reprinted the form since 1931 and there is no form in this city on which a night shift in the maintenance-deferred class could be certified today, and the hall does not keep blank forms of it, and does not know where the last packet went, and has said so in writing to a man who asked on the eighth of the ninth, and the answer is in the file and it is four words: *not held, not printed.***

> **The ordinary class is two shillings the head for a year and one shilling where a man is the occupier.** It is entered on a return. A return is made out on the rateable value of properties. **A pumping district is not a ward, has no rateable value, and is not in any return, and has not been in one since the first of the fifth of 1939.** So the ordinary class is a price for a thing this district has never had, and the maintenance-deferred class was a price for a thing this district had by eighteen years of nobody entering anything.

> **Six men are on the district works' own maintenance line, in the four-inch column, maintained by name: B. Sarr; A. Dench, known as Amos, to be entered as a full name and not as an initial; T. Arden, from the thirtieth ult., licensed: no; T. Arden, from the twenty-second inst., twenty-seven clocks, four hours, licensed: no; R. Morrow, night porter, Green Row, hours as returned; Vance, P., the seventeenth of the first month, night shift four, carried on the line because the line is a line and the line does not begin where a man begins. And a sixth, entered in the margin in a different ink and not in the column: a night porter at the Green Span hospital branch, entered by hand, hours as returned.**
> **Two of the lines against that column carry *licensed: no*, and they are the same man twice, and he wrote the second of the two himself.** He is not in a certified shift. **He never was.** The column says maintained by name. It does not say certified, and a man whose maintenance is recorded by name in a works' own column is not a man in a certified shift, and the difference between those two things is the whole of what has happened to this district since the first of the seventh.

**And here is one line of it, and he read it on the Thursday afternoon at about a quarter to one with the clerk's cup going cold beside him, and it is the whole of the finding and it is a line and not a book.**

> *17. iv. 47. Weir Two night, pump house and canal road. Class: **maint-deferred.** Heads: 4. On 22.00. Off 06.00. **At 03.00 water to ward: 6 h 41 m.** Countersigned: G. H.*

That is the line. It is thirty-one words and four numbers and it is a Friday in the middle of an ordinary winter in a year nobody in this room can name, and **there is no column on that page for what the four men did, and there is no column for the fact that a hospital eight hundred yards away was doing its own intervals off a bracket clock eleven inches high for six hours and forty-one minutes, because a maintenance-deferred night shift was a class and a price and not an event, and a class is entered on a line and an event is not.**

He read about a hundred and forty of them that afternoon and then he stopped counting and read the rest, and it took him three days, and here is what a thousand pages of it is:

**Every line is the same length. Every line has the same seven things on it and the same seven things missing. Every one of them is countersigned.** And the thing nobody has ever done with this book — because it is a book about a class, and the class is gone, and the book is at a counter for a shilling and twopence — is to go down the countersignature column and count how many different people are in it, and he did not do that either, **because the count would be a number about men who cannot be asked whether it is right, and he has spent this year learning what a number does to the person who makes it, and on the Thursday the fifteenth of the ninth a woman of thirty-three put a range on two boards outside a public building in her office's type, and said it out loud in the reading room the next day with about twenty-six people in it, and it cost her nothing to say and it has cost her the thing she wanted most ever since, and he has not been able to put that down.**

What he did instead, on the Friday, with a pencil the clerk lent him, was to go down the **heads** column, and to count how many lines in series 1 to 9 have a number in it that is between one and six, and the answer is a number he is not going to put on a wall in a pumping ward, because **a number about how small the shifts were is a number about a district that nobody will be charged for anything and cannot be asked about anything, and putting it on a wall would be the first thing this year that I have done which is worse than useless.**


And the last thing the book gave him, on the Monday afternoon, at about twelve twenty, when he had read the last line of series 9 and was shutting it up, was on the inside of the front cover of part one, where two things are pinned.

The first is printed. It is the hall's own slip and it has been in every part since the first of the sixth:

> *This book is published in full at the counter and for sale at a price that covers paper. Questions addressed to the master are not answered in this book. **The book is the book.***

The second is not printed. It is a strip of the same paper, torn from a form, and it is in pencil, in a large upright hand he has seen on four boards in a yard and on a certificate in a glass case and on a determination at a counter, and it says:

> ***I have read all of it. Do not ask me about it. I will not say.***

No name, no date, and he did not need either, and the clerk at the end of the counter did not look up when he read it, because she has read it too, every day for about eleven weeks, and it is pinned inside the front cover of the part that is on the lectern, and it is a master's own hand, and a man who writes *do not ask me about it* on the front of a book he had to publish is not a character in anybody's book.

**He did not ask him.** He had come to that room on the Thursday with four questions written in a notebook and he wrote them out again that evening in the shop and read them and then wrote under the four of them: *none of these is an answer to what he has written on that strip, and every one of them would require me to treat a man who has refused in advance as a resource, and that is the thing this whole book is about, and I am not going to be the first man in this district to do it with a man who has said no in advance in his own hand.*

---

And the finding is about an institution, and the institution has a price on it, and the price is not in a labour book, because the labour book has no column for a price.

> **THE ORDINARY CONDITION OF THIS DISTRICT, from Thursday the twentieth to Monday the twenty-fourth of the tenth, and it is a thing that is done before seven o'clock in the morning by six men and nobody writes any of it down.**
>
> **1. The class is gone.** Since the first of July there has been **no class in this city in which a shift in this district could be certified at all.** Not a worse class. None. The maintenance-deferred class was abolished and the ordinary class requires a return and there is no return. A night shift in the Lower Weirs is now, in the strict sense, an uncertified thing, every night, lawfully, and there is no form on which any person may be told so and no form on which any person may object to it.
>
> **2. Nobody is at fault in that, in the way anybody means by that.** The hall abolished a class it created. The ordinance of 1883 and the standing order of the first of the seventh are both correct and both were read before they were carried. A man of fifty-five who lost the right to certify in that class on the first of July did not lose it because anybody did anything to him; he lost it because a hall he ran for twenty-two years abolished a class it had invented to get round a return. **The system is well run and it has taken this district apart correctly.**
>
> **3. The dates. Not a plan. A list of dates, and every one of them is a date, and there is not one of them on this wall that anybody in this district is going to do anything about.**
> *— The first of the fifth. Form GH-9, forty-one heads, a year. **Two hundred and twenty-one days, computed in my own book on the twentieth of the ninth against the first of the fifth.** A figure. Not a plan. Not a rescue, and there is nothing to rescue, and I have written that down already and I am writing it down again because the month is going.*
> *— The second of the third. The low-level alarm on the ward's reserve inhibited. No report made. No form requires one. **To the ninth of the eleventh is two hundred and fifty-one days, and the ninth of the eleventh is the day the district works' inspection programme would have had a man stand in that outer room and read that alarm, and there is no man and there is no item and there is no alarm.***
> *— The first of the fourth of this year, being the day the district works' schedule was adopted. **Six hundred and seventy days from that day is the fourth of the second month of the year after next, and a reader can do that in one subtraction, and **there is nothing on that day.** The next adoption is the first of the fourth month of next year and a new item goes in on adoption and its first inspection is a year after that, and neither of those two dates is inside the six hundred and seventy days, and I have put the number on the wall because I had nothing else to put on it, and a figure without a date is the whole of what this district is and I have written that down as well.** On the fifth of the fourth month of next year a new item goes into that published schedule. Item ten goes with it: eleven inspections a year for three years at twelve pounds nine shillings and fourpence, **four hundred and eleven pounds eight shillings**, first inspection falling in programme year one. **There is no first inspection in it and this is the last quarter of programme year one.** Four hundred and eleven pounds is the whole of what eleven inspections a year for three years has ever cost anybody in this city and it has given nobody an hour.*
> *— The thirteenth of the ninth. The standing protection lapsed. Not extended. Cannot be. **It protected a certificate in a class that had been gone for eleven weeks, and it has expired and nothing stopped except the paper.***
>
> **4. And the finding, and it is one sentence, and it is not about anybody.** For nineteen years, a night shift in this district was certified by a man putting his countersignature in a book. From the first of July, it is certified by nobody, and the thing that replaced the countersignature is a line in a works' own column that says *maintained by name*, **and the name is the only part of it that is in a document, and the name is not an objection and cannot be refused and cannot be withdrawn.** Two lines against one name on that column carry *licensed: no*, and it is the same man on both of them, and he is twenty years old, and he wrote the second one himself, and he has never been in a certified shift and never will be, and there is no form in this city on which either of those two lines can be put to him in the second person.

He read that on the Monday morning at about eleven in the outer room of the pumping ward and Bevin Sarr copied four lines of it into the night book in her own hand with the date, and then she read the fifth line back to him before she wrote it, and made him change *the name is not an objection* to *the name is not an instrument*, which is the word she has been using since the sixth month and which she is right about, and which is in four other files in this district and in a book in a shop.

"**That is the whole of what a district learns from having had a class,**" she said. "**It learns that it had one, and then it does not, and nothing that anybody said about the having of it is available to anybody afterwards.**"

Nobody has been thanked. The master did not come to the counter and was not asked to and would have said the four words on the strip, and the clerk at the end of the counter took one shilling and twopence for the part and did not know what he had sold him, and a man of thirty-two read a thousand pages in three afternoons and got to the front of part one last, which is a thing about how a person reads and not about what was in it.
