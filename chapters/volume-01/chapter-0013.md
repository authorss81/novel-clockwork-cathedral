# Chapter 13 — Arithmetic

The gallery was nine chairs and a rail and a smell of hot paper, and by twenty past ten on Monday morning there were six people in it, and two of them were there because the seat in front of them was occupied by a woman of about seventy who had brought knitting.

The Committee clerk came out at the door with a bundle and a list and said, to nobody in particular, "Public-health committee, standing order, item two, a demand. Twelve minutes, and then whatever it takes."

She went in and the door shut. Orrin had not been in this room since the Wednesday, five days ago, when the whole of it had been refused in nine minutes, and he had expected to be frightened of it again and was not, which was worse, because the reason was that he now knew what he was listening to.

Mara Venn sat two rows along with a folder on her knees and had not opened it.

Item one was a boundary alteration in Northglass and took four minutes. Item two was called at twenty-five past ten and Councilor Imani Rook stood up and did not read from a paper, which told Orrin that she had learned it rather than written it, and which told him something else, which was that the arithmetic had been done before the room and the room was only being told.

"I am moving a demand in my own name and on my own responsibility, and I want the notice to show the name as mine and not as a committee's, because the committee has not agreed to it and I have not asked it to." She had a single sheet and she put it face down on the table in front of her. "Before I read it I want to say two things and get them both in the minutes.

"First: the demand that was before this committee on the twenty-fourth was moved by the Office of Public Reckoning and seconded by a private citizen, and it was referred to this committee for the spring with a return date, and at the time I said in this room that I would vote against it again in the spring unless somebody produced the district returns I had asked for." She let that sit, because she was a woman who understood exactly what a room does with a silence. "Somebody has produced them. They are on the table. They are four months of the pumping district's own record of its own labour, kept by the district, in a form I am told is a rent book and a piece of chalk, and I have read them, and I am moving this demand on the strength of them, and I have moved it because I was shown arithmetic and not because anybody in this room persuaded me of anything."

Nobody moved. Downstairs, out in the street, a tram signal let go its window two minutes late, and Orrin counted it without meaning to, because he could not help counting things.

"Second: I am still not convinced. I want that in the minutes in my own words, because in a fortnight one of you will tell a colleague that I have been got round by the Weirs, and it is not true and it is not going to be true later. I have not been got round. I have been out-argued, and I will say how, and you may judge it."

She turned the sheet over.

---

It took her twenty minutes and she had it with her, and the thing that Orrin took out of the hall afterwards was that she had not brought one figure she had not already checked twice.

"The demand I refused on the twenty-fourth asked for two things. The first was that the toll of every Reading be entered in this Assembly's durable record with the district it was drawn from, because the Toll Stair is a bucket and a board and a man's memory and is scrubbed at half past five every morning. That first clause I did not contest. Nobody has contested it. It costs a clerk's morning a week and it is the cheapest thing anybody has asked this committee in my time and it will be refused again on the grounds that nobody pays for it, and the mover has not produced a payer, and therefore the mover is not ready and the clause is not before you.

"The second clause asked for a name and a duration against every body toll, in public, in a book. I refused that on the grounds of panic, and I gave you a figure at the time, and I want the figure in the minutes beside the new one. Sixty-one calibration-strain entries in this committee's own files last year, from four wards. Four wards hold a hundred and ninety thousand people. The city holds two and a half million. Scale it, crudely, and you are in the low thousands every year." She put her hand flat on the sheet. "I still believe that arithmetic. I have not been moved off it in five days and I have been moved by nothing except the returns.

"Here is the return. The pumping district keeps its own hours at its own two civic water stations, Weir Two and the Weir One gate, on a board in a pump house and in a book kept in a kitchen over a chandler's shop in Weir Lane. The book has been kept by the same family for fifty-one years. The four months in question are the four to the end of last quarter. The board and the book together show one thousand four hundred and twelve hours worked at a civic water station by five men on a roster."

She let the number be a number for a moment, in a way that Orrin could see she had practised, and then she put a second sheet on the table beside the first.

"And here is the city's record of the same four months, at the same two stations, from the works' certified shift return, which is the document the city's own arithmetic runs on." She turned it round. "It is nil. Both stations. Four months. There is no certified night shift at Weir Two, and there is no maintenance line covering nights, and there is no wage paid on the return, because there is no shift. The maintenance line for Weir Two for the year shows fourteen hundred and six hours of honest daytime work, certified, paid, and good, and I have no complaint about it and it is not in dispute.

"One thousand four hundred and twelve hours," she said. "That is the whole of the arithmetic, and it is two documents and a subtraction. Five men, worked, at a civic machine, for four months. Nil on the return. Not under-recorded. Not recorded. And I want to be very careful about what I have just done, because I have been in this chamber nine years and I have watched people use a number like that to say far more than it will bear, and I am not doing it."

"Madam—"

"You may, somebody may, and I will come to it." She turned to the last page. "What that number does *not* show, and I am going to say all of this in my own voice so that nobody has to guess later. It shows no name. It shows no employer. It shows nothing about any man in it, or whether he was paid, or whether he was paid under another name on another return, or whether he was injured, and it cannot show those things, and the committee must not pretend otherwise this afternoon. It does not show that anybody has committed an offence. It shows that on this committee's own arithmetic there is a floor with no bottom under it, and the low thousands I gave you on the twenty-fourth is not a rate. It is the bottom of the column. Above it, whatever the true figure is, and I have no idea what the true figure is, and neither does the mover, and neither does the investigator standing in that gallery."

Orrin did not move and did not look at Mara.

"And the third thing," said Councilor Rook, "which is the whole of why I am standing here, and I would be obliged if the clerk would put it exactly as I say it, because it is the only sentence in this motion that costs anybody anything. The Office of Public Reckoning asked the works for certified shift returns for Weir Two for the four months in question. The request went in on the twentieth. It came back on the twenty-sixth, received and noted, and the note says the office has no record of certified shifts outside the guild series, and recommends application to the hall." She looked up. "That is not a refusal. I want the clerk to note that I said so. It is worse than a refusal, because a refusal can be appealed and a note cannot, and because it means the one body in this city whose entire function is to know what a decision cost has asked the question and been told that the question is in the wrong building.

"So the only record of those hours that exists in Morrowfen is a chalk board and a kitchen book, and neither of them can be put into evidence, and this committee is going to be asked to legislate on the strength of a thing that a single man could rub off with his sleeve." She squared the sheet. "I have moved the demand accordingly. Not the demand on the twenty-fourth. A different one, and a smaller one, and I would like the mover of the twenty-fourth to forgive me for that."

---

The motion, when it was read, was two lines long.

> *That the Assembly receive the pumping district's own returns for the four months to the end of last quarter as evidence of hours worked at a civic water station; that the Office of Public Reckoning be directed to establish, by the fourth sitting, the means by which a district may enter a record of its own hours, and report; and that the returns be printed in full with the minutes.*

"It has got no name and no duration in it," said Mara Venn, from the second row, and then stopped, because she was not a member either, and the chair looked at her and she said, "Forgive me," and sat down, and Orrin understood that she had done that on purpose so that he would not.

"Madam, is the mover taking amendments?"

"No," said Rook. "I have taken the two I wanted. Amendment one, a duration column: refused, and the mover will tell the committee why. Amendment two, a name: refused, and the mover will tell the committee why."

There were about forty seconds of the chamber's noise and then a division, taken standing, and it was closer than the one five days before had been, and Orrin counted the hands twice and got the same answer both times, which is a thing he has never been able to stop doing.

The demand was referred to committee. The return date was named for after the fourth sitting. It was read twice, and entered in the minutes, and the six returns from the district were ordered printed in full with them, which is a small thing and is the first time in forty-three years that a district's own arithmetic has appeared in the Assembly's record at all. And then it was discharged.

Forty-one minutes, against nine.

---

In the corridor outside, Rook came out of the chamber and did not stop, and Mara Venn said, "Ma'am — the record. The transcription. Who pays for the clerk who copies a chalk board?"

Rook stopped. She had a folder under her arm and she had the manner of a woman who has done a thing she is not sure of and has decided to be tired about it rather than frightened.

"You have been waiting five days to ask me that," she said. "I could tell because you had it written down and you were going to be pleased with yourself if I did not say it. Well. The copying is a ward clerk, two days a week for six weeks, and it comes out of the Green Span public-health clerical allocation, and there is no line for it, so I have put a line in the estimates under *district returns, exceptional*, at four pounds ten, and it goes to the Finance committee in three weeks, and I am telling you in advance that four of the five Green Span delegates will vote against it, and one of them will vote for it and be furious, and neither of them will understand what it is for, and in nine years somebody will read the estimates and think I have gone soft."

"That is not a small amount of money to spend on a chalk board," said Mara.

"No. And I have spent it, and I would like it noticed that it was spent, because somebody is going to say that the pumping district made me do it, and they did not, and I want it in the minutes in a form that can be quoted, and the mover has agreed to the amendment." She looked at Mara properly for the first time. "You told him the sentence. I know you told him the sentence, because the sentence has been in every room in this building for forty years and it is in yours because you are the only person in the city who audits the one instrument that shows the cost. Who pays for the record. And it killed your demand on the twenty-fourth, and it is going to kill mine in the Finance committee in three weeks, and the only reason it has not killed this is that I have a ward and a clerk in it and four pounds ten of somebody else's money." She shifted the folder. "You may write that down. I would rather you wrote it down than that you said it out loud in my corridor, because out loud in a corridor it is a conversation, and in your file it is a fact about a public-health committee that can be cited by whoever comes next."

Then she said: "No. The name and the duration are not in it, and I want you to hear the reason from me rather than work it out, because you will work it out and get it nearly right, and the difference matters."

"Say it."

"Sixty-one entries in my files for last year and four wards, and I have a scale that is crude and that I do not trust, and the honest position is that I do not know how many people there are." Rook's voice did not change at all, which was the most frightening thing about it. "That is the answer. I do not know how many, and I will not put a column in a public book beside a figure I do not know the size of, because the day a person's lost attention goes into a column in a book in this city with a district's return printed beside it, the two numbers will be read together, and one of them will be read as the cost of the other, and a hundred and ninety thousand people in four Green Span wards are going to be told what their own bodies are worth by a chalk board in the Weirs. And I would rather lose the clause and keep the returns. And in a year or two, when somebody has counted properly, that clause will come back and it will come back with a number on it, and I will not be in this chamber, and I hope whoever is will be braver than me."

"Mrs Rook—"

"Mara." She said it without thinking. "And do not thank me, because a thank-you now is a thing that will be repeated to me by the person who gets the name column, and I would like that person to have to go and earn it."
