# Chapter 71 — What He Decided Not to Tell Him

A man who has worked out something on a Tuesday morning on a canal road and has decided not to say it does not get relieved of that on the Wednesday. He gets it on the Wednesday and the Thursday and the Friday, and on the Saturday he is a man who has decided not to say a thing, and on the Sunday morning it is the fourth day of a decision and there is nothing whatever in the day to help him, and this is what the first three days of the twenty-fourth of the eighth month were like.

---

**Wednesday the twenty-fourth, and he did nine hours of somebody else's clock, and the stoppage that is called for a week on Thursday is eight days off.**

The job was a long-case of 1897 in a house in the Clockwork Quarter, a Mahogany and eight-day with a subsidiary seconds and a strike that had not been, as far as anybody could find out, for about two years. The clock was stopped when he took the weights off, which is the way clocks are.

He wrote the order of assembly in the narrow book before he began, because he has been doing that since the sixth of the eighth and it is a shilling and a half of paper and eleven inches by four and it is on the shelf behind the bench, and there is nothing in it he has needed once. He wrote eleven lines in about two minutes, and the movement came out in about four hours, and he had the train wheels out of their plates and on a tray by ten o'clock.

And at about half past ten, with the great wheel in his left hand and the third wheel in his right, he looked at a pivot in the third wheel that was wearing a little on one side only, which is what a clock does when the train is not quite true, and he could have put a strip under that pivot and taken half an hour over it and had a clock worth more on the bench than the eight guineas.

He did not do it. He put the wheel down, and he did the other three things instead, and the clock runs inside a second a day and the third wheel's pivot will be marked in about four years and it will be his fault and there will be nothing anybody can do about it.

**The count of his own hand corrections is two.** Two, in eleven years: eleven minutes on a lane in the Weirs and eight on a bracket at the Ferrywright corner, both done with a bar, with no receipt and no form and no name. **This is the third time this year that a machine in his own hands has been a quarter of an inch from a thing he could have done to it with a strip of steel and a chisel, and the count is two, and the count is two because of a decision and not because of a rule**, and he wrote that in the ledger on the Wednesday evening with the price on it:

> *Twenty-fourth of the eighth. Long-case, 1897, Clockwork Quarter. Eight guineas the price agreed before the case was opened. Nine hours. Runs inside a second a day and the third pivot is marked and I did not do it and there is nobody in the world who will ever know except me and this page.*
> *A hand correction is a thing a man does to a civic machine with no form and no receipt and nobody able to say no. This was a customer's clock in a customer's house, paid for, on a bench, with the owner downstairs and the price written out before I started. **I have not had to ask anybody's permission to touch this clock and I have not had to ask nobody's permission either, and that is the whole of the difference and it took me nineteen years and a suspension to be able to write it down in one line.***
> *That is the third time this year. **The count of my own hand corrections is two and it is two because of a decision and not because of a rule.**

**The first of the four rooms was on the Wednesday afternoon and it was a room over a shop in Bellweather, and it belonged to a man of twenty.**

The tenant council's rooms. There is a stair on the outside and a bell that has not worked since about 1949 and you knock on the frame. Inside there are two tables pushed together and eleven chairs and a stove and a shelf of minute books in which the most recent volume is 1948, and the reason there is no volume after 1948 is on the table in Wenna Pruitt's hand and is that a minute is only a thing anybody is bound by and the council has not in twenty years bound anybody to anything and has no money to bind them with.

Teo Arden was in there with a stool and a tray of oscillators out of a dead radio and he was doing something to a movement that had no case on it, and he did not look up.

"I have come to tell you something and I have come to say it, and I am going to say something else instead, and you are going to know that is what I have done, and I am not going to explain it, because I do not want to be asked."

"You are not going to tell me what it is," said Teo Arden.

"No."

"Right." He went on with the movement. Then, about two minutes later, without looking up: "I have known there was a thing on the canal road since the Wednesday. Idris Vane's foreman told a man in the galvanising works on the Tuesday afternoon and it is eleven men in a works and it is eleven men in a works." He set the oscillator down. "You worked something out. You have not written it down. And you have come up a stair with a bell that has not worked since 1949 to sit in a room where I am."

"Yes."

"Then that is a man's whole week." He picked the oscillator up again. "And I want it said in a room with the door shut, which is the only way I know of to make a thing a record, **and I have not asked you and you are not going to tell me, and I have said so out loud, and that is the whole of what I have done about it and I am going to leave it there.**"

"Put it in a minute," said Orrin Vale.

"There is no minute. That is the entire institutional position of this building."

He worked on the movement for another three or four minutes and then he said the sentence that was in the room for the rest of the afternoon and that Orrin Vale did not sleep well on.

"You will do whatever you are going to do on the first, and four hundred men will be at that gate, and you will be somewhere in this district, and I have decided I am not going to know what you know in advance. **I am not doing it to be fair. I am doing it because if I know it I own it, and if I own it then when it goes wrong it is a strike, and a strike is a thing with a man at the front of it, and I have never wanted to be a man at the front of anything in my life.** Go and do your work."

Orrin Vale went down the outside stair and stood in the street for about a minute. Then he went and did four and a half hours of the same customer's long-case and made it up to nine.

**Thursday the twenty-fifth, and the second room was the warden's office in the pumping ward on the canal road, and the warden had five days left in it.**

It is a room about ten feet by eight with a desk, a chair, a shelf, a barometer, and **a shelf above the barometer on which there is a copy of the standing rotation of the Lower Weirs pumping district, and that copy is the only one in the district.** It is a copy of a copy made in 1951. The other copy is nailed behind a ladder in the district works' yard and the two of them disagree by fifteen minutes and there is no third copy, no run list and no accession number, and nobody has ever been able to say which of the two the branch is following except by standing at a standpipe with a bucket and a watch.

**And on Monday the first of the ninth month the person who owns the only copy in the district stops being a person in charge of it, and a warden is a person and a person is not an institution.**

She was packing. That is the word for it and it is the right one: two boxes, string, and a stack of about nine ledgers going into them in the order they had been used, and a written notice of twenty-four hours' standing on the ward board outside which said what it said.

"Can I ask you something," said Orrin Vale, from the doorway.

"You may ask me anything, I have been asked worse by a man with a folding rule." She did not stop packing. "I am not resigning, before you say it. I am not being dismissed. There is nobody here to dismiss me, the district works has never appointed me and cannot, and if anybody tells you the works sacked me they are lying and you may say I told you so."

"I was not going to say that."

"You were going to say something adjacent to it, which is the same as a person who has come in here to say something and has decided on the stairs." She tied the string. "What is it."

He had it ready. It was the four lines and the tank and the main and the clock-driven boundary sluice at the works' gate and the man of about forty and the two hours in the fifth month, and he had rehearsed it on the canal road on the Tuesday and again on the Wednesday morning, and it took him about eleven seconds to say and he did not say any of it.

"I came to ask whether the shelf copy is going in a box."

Sel Kade stopped with the string in her hands.

"You came to this office on the Thursday, five days before I go, to ask me where a piece of paper goes."

"Yes."

"And you have something else."

"Yes."

"Then say it or go home." She put the box down. "I have been the person who answers for nineteen years and I have five days left of it and I am not going to spend one of them being polite to a man who has come up a stair to check my packing."

"I cannot say it."

**"Cannot,"** said Sel Kade, "**is not the same as will not, and you have had five months in this district to learn the difference and you have not learned it, and I am going to tell you what it is going to cost you in about eleven weeks and then you can come back and say it.**"

She picked the box up.

"Everything in this office is going in these two boxes, and the boxes are going into the pump house store because the pump house is a building and a store is a room and both of them are a great deal more permanent than me. **The shelf copy goes against the wall of the store, on its edge, with the rest of it, and there is a form in the works' store for a thing that is put in the store, and I have filled it in, and the form has a line that says where it is going to be kept, and the line is the store, and not a shelf.** And that is the correct answer and you should write it down, and if you ever want to know whether anybody in this district is holding a piece of paper, the answer is no, and the reason is that I have just been the person who held it and I have given it to a room."

He wrote it down. He wrote it down in the doorway, on the back of an invoice, and she watched him do it.

"That is the first useful thing anybody has written down in this office in five months," she said. "Now go away, and if you have something to say to me, say it before the first, and say it in a room with other people in it, because a thing said to me in a room with one other person in it is a thing said to a woman, and I have had nineteen years of them."

**Friday the twenty-sixth, and the third room was a first-floor front on Ferrywright Row with nine blocks behind her and a rate book on the table.**

Hanna Stroud is fifty-four and has nine blocks and about two hundred and eleven households and is a landlord in the sense that the law uses the word and not in the sense her mother would have, and she was on the Assembly's public-health committee's witness rail twice this year and gave evidence for eleven minutes both times and was right both times and has not been thanked for either.

She is not a soft woman. She is a practical one, and the difference is visible in the first minute, and what she did with the thing he came to tell her was visible in the second.

"Sit down. I have got the valuation of six properties on this table and I have got a man in my kitchen at the end of this street who has not had his roof read in four years and I have been thinking about your roof since Tuesday. What is it."

He said nothing.

"Right." She went on with the valuation. Then, at about the fourth page: "You have come up here to say a thing and you have not said it and you are going to go away and say you could not think of the right moment. **I have been in eleven rooms this year with men who could not think of the right moment.** So I am going to help you, because it will take less time than it will with somebody who enjoys it. You have worked something out about a thing that is going to happen on the first, and it is about water, and it is not the rate, because if it were the rate you would have said the rate, and it is not the laundry, because if it were the laundry you would have said the laundry."

"Yes."

"And there is nobody in this district who can be told, in a room, by a man with a notebook, in a form."

"Yes."

"Right." She wrote a figure in a column and did not look up. "Then what you want from me is a form and a meeting and a notice, and I have to tell you that you have not got one and I cannot get you one, and I have tried, and here is what I tried, and it took four months. **I have tried to get nine blocks onto a water committee and I have got as far as a form that asks a landlord whether he objects, and the answer to that form by every landlord in this street is no, and it is no because nobody objects and because the answer costs nothing to give.** And I have tried the tenant council and they have a stool and a stove and a bell that has not worked since 1949, and I have tried the mutual and they meet when there is a drain. And I have tried a petition, in 1951, and a petition goes to the ward board and the ward board is a board, and in March somebody took a rate notice off it and put a bill poster on and nobody in that building noticed for nine days."

She turned the page.

"So you have not come up here to tell me a thing, Mr Vale, you have come up here to find out whether there is a room. And there is not one, and I have looked for four months, and I am a landlord with nine blocks and a rate book and I have the standing of nobody at all in this city." She looked up. "And when you have a room, in about four years, whatever is in it will be about water, and you will be in it, and I will not be, because a landlord with nine blocks is on the water committee and a clockmaker with two benches is not."

"Yes."

"**So do not waste a room on a thing that cannot be refused.** Put the thing you cannot say into the room. That is what a room is for. A room is a place a person can be told no in, and you have told me you cannot, and I am the wrong woman and I am glad to be the wrong woman, and I have not asked you what it is and I am not going to."

That one he had not expected, and it went in the ledger that night in three lines and the three lines are the first of the four of them.

**Saturday the twenty-seventh, and the fourth room was at the top of four flights in number fourteen, Ferrywright Row, and there is no light in that stair's fanlight and has not been since about 1948.**

He went up it with a torch because a man of eighty-one comes down it with a lamp and has told the story twice and both times the lamp was the point.

Alden Fenwick is eighty-one. He has lived at the top of number fourteen since about 1941 and paid sixty-two pounds a year before the war, and he is not a martyr and he is not a symbol and he is a small old man in a chair who wanted somebody to come.

"That you, from the shop."

"Yes."

"I knew a fella came." He did not offer anything else for a while. Then: "You'll have heard about my light."

"No. I have not."

"1911 laid the stair and 1948 the fanlight went and the landlord's had four letters about it, three from me and one off a man in a good coat, and it's not a light, it's the whole stair, because you cannot get up or down a dark stair at seventy-eight and you've got the lamp and I've got the lamp and there's nine of us in this stair." He nodded at the ceiling. "Mrs Ilse above me, she's sixty-one, and she'd come down in the dark tomorrow and she won't say so and I've told her."

"There is a form for the landlord's lighting in this city. I have read it."

"Don't want it."

"No."

"That's the first fella in four years to not want it." He looked at him. "What do you want."

And Orrin Vale stood in a small flat at the top of four dark flights in number fourteen, Ferrywright Row, at about half past four in the afternoon on the Saturday, and had a thing on the tip of his tongue that he had not told a warden, a tuner, a landlord with nine blocks, or a man of eighty-one with a lamp, and he said:

"Nothing today. I came up to see how your lamp is."

The lamp is a paraffin lamp with a bent handle and a wick his wife trimmed and it is the instrument that has made four hundred trips down that stair since 1948 and it has never once been in a book.

"How's it burning."

"Fine. She wants wicking. That's all she wants, wick it about once a week and don't be clever with it."

"I won't be clever with it."

"You'll be off now then."

"Wednesday," said Orrin Vale. "Or Wednesday, and if I am in the district, which I will not be, I will send a man with a card from the shop."

"You send a man with a card." Fenwick said it the way a man says *the lamp burns fine*. "Aye. All right. That'll do."

**And on the Sunday the twenty-eighth of the eighth month he was in the outer room of the pumping ward at about eleven in the morning, and Bevin Sarr had come off the front, and she had a list of houses in her hand because it was a Sunday and there was nothing else in the world to do with a list of houses.**

He said it in the first forty seconds. He had it on the page the way you have a thing on a page that you have been carrying for five days, and he read it out.

> *The ward's reserve is the tank. The tank is the hospital's clock pressure. The tank has been under two days since the second of the third month, and its low-level alarm has been inhibited since the same date, and no report was made, and no form requires one.*
> *The make-up comes off a main that goes through a 1911 clock-driven boundary sluice at the works' gate, and the sluice is a works' asset, and a works' man opens it.*
> *There is a note in the warden's book from the fifth month about a man of about forty at that gate who put his hand on a wheel for two hours while a woman with a bad hip went up and down a ladder, and there is no column anywhere in this city for the two hours, and he was not thanked and he has not been asked.*
> *I worked that out on the canal road at half past nine on the morning of Tuesday the twenty-third. I did not write it down and I did not tell anybody for five days. **I am telling you and not the works, because the works will do it in a morning and you will not.***

She listened to it standing, with the list of houses in her hand, and she did not interrupt, and when it was finished she put the list on the ledge under the glass case and did not pick it up again.

"Right," she said.

"I know what you are going to say."

"You have four days' notice of a stoppage and a hospital on one side of a tank that has been under two days since the second of the third month, and you have sat on it for five days, and I am going to say the thing and then I am going to tell you why I am not saying it more than once, and you will not enjoy either half."

"Say it."

"**You have decided what four hundred people are going to find out, on your own, in a shop, with no form, and you have not asked them either.**"

The outer room of that pumping ward has a glass case in it with a key on a string and a chair by the case and a chair and a pencil and ink on the ledge, and a stair, and a window, and a man had just said a thing in it and had not been contradicted.

"That is the sentence," she said, "and I have not said it to make you feel better, and I am not going to soften it, and I am going to say the other half now so that you cannot go and tell somebody that I only said that. **You did not tell him because there is no room, and that is true, and it is the best reason available to you, and I have been in this district eleven years and it is the truest thing anybody has said in it this year.** And you had five days and you used none of them, and you went up a stair to a man of twenty to see whether he would ask you, and he said no, and then you went and asked four people who would not have told you what to do, and none of them could have stopped you, and all four of them spent an afternoon telling you why there is no room, and you had already worked that out. **You went looking for permission for a thing you had already decided not to do, and that is a very old shape and I know it because I have watched wardens do it for nineteen years.**"

"I know."

"I know you know. I am not going to have this in a book." She picked the list up. "Two things and then I am going to wind a clock at the second court because somebody has to and it is Sunday and there is nothing else.

"**One: I am not going to help you and I am not going to stop you.** I am going to hand-wind the front on Tuesday morning with four other people, as I have done twice, and if it goes wrong I am going to be one of the people who was there, which is the only place I have ever wanted to be, and I have never once been able to arrange it. And if the tank goes under a day on the Wednesday instead of the Tuesday, I am going to write it in this book, in my own hand, with the date and the hour, and the book is in this room, and it is not the guild's and it is not the works'.

"**Two: I want the date.** The day it is. Not the plan, not the argument, not what you worked out. The date."

"It is the first of the ninth. At the shift change."

"Thank you." She went to the door, and then stopped with her hand on it, and said the last thing over her shoulder without turning round, and it was not said unkindly, and it has never once been said kindly by anybody in this city. "**You did not tell him, and you told me, and I am a woman with a list of houses in her hand and no standing under the water act, and one of us is going to be right about this and it is not going to be me, and I would be obliged if you wrote that down as well.**"

**And Ivo Pell came at about ten past four on the Sunday afternoon, on time, at a time of his own choosing, and he stood in the shop and read the narrow book off the shelf with his hands behind his back, and gave it back, and said three words.**

"**The first, then.**"

That was the whole of it. He did not wait for an answer and he did not wait to be thanked, and he went out to Northglass with a train to catch, and the arrangement is four clauses dated the nineteenth of the sixth, and neither of them may call it instruction, and if either of them is thanked they stop.

---

**And on the Friday evening, between the ledger and the bench, he wrote the date.**

He wrote it in longhand, in a book of two thousand pages, on a page of its own, and the page says this and nothing else, and it is dated the twenty-sixth of the eighth:

> *Thursday the first day of the ninth month, about half past six in the morning, at the shift change at the Northglass gate. That is the first day.*
> *There is a ninth day. A thing that lasts is not one morning, and I do not know yet what a ninth day is, and I have written that down here so that I cannot pretend afterwards that I only ever thought about the first one.*
> *There is no plan against it and there is no minute and there is nobody who has asked me for it, and I am not telling a man of twenty because a man of twenty is not the person who is in it, and I am not telling a warden who is going on the Friday, and I am not telling a landlord with nine blocks because a landlord with nine blocks is on the water committee.*
> *I have got three days and I have told four people the shape of nothing and I have been told by all four of them, in four different rooms, in four different words, that there is no room in this city in which a man with a notebook may say a thing like that and be refused.*
> *I do not know what I will do on the first. I have written that down so that whatever I do I am not a man who did it without noticing which day it was.*
