# Ending Outline: The Cathedral After

This is the planned ending for Volume 14, Chapters 651–700. It is fixed before prose generation so the final resolution can be built from earlier promises rather than improvised.

## 1. Final external conflict

### The Returning Tide

A storm surge, river swell, and a failure in the Outer Sluice arrive together. The event is not a magical apocalypse and does not introduce a new cosmic enemy. It is a severe, physically plausible cascade: tide pressure rises, the Northglass rail bridge loses one expansion joint, a hospital clock skips, and the Weirs pumps fall out of phase. The combined load exceeds anything in the Cathedral's historical record.

The First Hour identifies a single sequence that will keep most of Morrowfen synchronized:

1. Seal the Weirs floodgates.
2. Cut power and water to the lower districts.
3. Use the Weirs as a physical counterweight to absorb the tide pressure.
4. Preserve the hospital, rail, and central commercial networks.
5. Require every clock in the city to follow the Cathedral's sequence.

The plan would save most of the city. It would also make the Weirs bear an unlisted share of the damage, cut off thousands from shelter and medical care, and permanently leave the district without a stable civic clock. The First Hour does not understand the difference between a willing sacrifice and a coerced one. Its rule is stable or divergent; it cannot tell consent from noise.

Halden Rusk declares a regional emergency under the emergency clause of the regional water and power scheduling act, which permits a declared emergency to be answered "by any measure the responsible office certifies is necessary." That clause predates every reform in this story, cannot be repealed by the Assembly alone, and is named at least once before this volume. It is the only reason a Continuity Minister can issue a lock order at all. Anja Voss, torn between the evidence of past harm and the immediate danger, initially accepts the order. She accepts it knowing the Weirs are in the hall, because the water is already over the flood doors by the time she does. She later recognizes that the system is once again treating a person as a machine part.

### The opposing plan

Orrin, Mara, Teo, Leda, Anja, and the local time councils prepare a different plan:

- Keep the independent clocks running at deliberately different local rates.
- Let each district choose what it can safely give to the common network.
- Open the Northglass flood basin through the Sluice bypass.
- Move people and medical supplies before the pressure arrives.
- Maintain Green Span's hospital clocks manually.
- Refuse to restore central synchronization until the tide has passed.

The plan is less efficient by the First Hour's model, and it is not free. It puts the river into the **Northglass flood basin**, which is low, engineered, and — because the region's heavy industries moved out decades ago — empty. It tears out the **Northglass rail bridge** to let the river past. It leaves the Weirs without electricity for eleven days and puts several Weirs blocks under water, because the tide still has to go somewhere and the Weirs are still the lowest ground.

What makes it a different plan is not the arithmetic. It is four things the First Hour's plan has no way to express:

1. **The counterweight is somebody else's property, and they agreed to it.** The Northglass council is asked, in Volume 13, in public, over two chapters, whether it will consent to lose its rail yards. It says yes, and the reader is given the meeting, including the delegates who vote against. Its consent is read aloud again in Volume 14, to a room that needs to hear that it was real.
2. **The Weirs decide for themselves.** Their own tenant council opens their own flood doors. The vote is six to three, and the three are not overruled by Orrin. They are given first evacuation, guaranteed transport, and a seat in the room where the plan is made.
3. **The loss has an end and a name.** Eleven days, a named season of lost rail service, a published roster of restoration crews, and a compensation line. The First Hour's plan has none of these and could not supply them, because it does not know that the people it is using have names.
4. **The choice was made before the water arrived.** The First Hour decides at the moment of pressure. The city's plan was argued, signed, and minuted while there was still time to disagree.

The public plan is still worse for some people than the plan the machine would have chosen. That is admitted out loud in the volume, by people who hate admitting it. The claim the book makes is narrower and has to be earned in every scene: the losses are *chosen by the people standing in them*, which is the one variable the machine cannot carry. The plan can work only if people trust one another enough to act without a single answer.

## 2. Final internal choice

Orrin's final flaw is control. He believes that if he can see the consequences clearly enough, he can protect the city by taking responsibility for the hardest choice himself. During the early climax he builds a private counterweight ward that will save the hospitals and put the hidden load back on the Weirs. The ward does the *same arithmetic* as the public plan. That is what makes it unforgivable: the number is right and the consent is gone. The Weirs agreed to a figure they could read, argue with, and refuse, and Orrin has moved that figure out of sight while still calling it their agreement. The ward is technically successful, and it proves that his old instinct is still dangerous.

Mara forces him to confront the consequence: a plan that only works because Orrin has concealed the cost is another version of the First Hour. He must choose between a private emergency command and a public interval in which no one can guarantee the outcome.

### Orrin's choice

Orrin opens the Cathedral's physical records and publishes the full consequence map, every figure in it, including the ones that make his own ward look like the best available option. He proposes an **Open Interval**: the city will act without one binding future, and every affected district will know what it is being asked to carry. He signs the public ledger as a participant, not as a ruler. He accepts that the plan may kill people and that he cannot privately guarantee otherwise. Taking the routing plates out is not part of this decision. It is the eleventh chapter of the climax, and it is the part that cannot be taken back.

This is the final answer to his father. Edwin made a limited sacrifice under a broken system; Orrin refuses to turn sacrifice into inheritance. He will make a decision, but he will not make it alone.

## 3. Chapter map for the final volume

The volume is fifty chapters and it is mapped in four movements. The climax is eleven chapters, which is one more than a sequence of physical choices should need; the extra chapter is the quiet one after the plates come out, and it is not optional.

### 651–664: the water coming

Fourteen chapters of approach, and the reader must be able to feel the tide for most of them without a single Reading, because the whole volume is the argument that a person can tell by looking.

Morrowfen's ordinary preparations. Ferry timetables reissued, hospital stock moved up, the Sluice bypass crews mustering, Northglass clearing the basin. Written by working people, not by the Cathedral. This stretch is deliberately low in spectacle: the book has been arguing for six hundred chapters that most of what happens is clerical, and this is where it cashes that in.

- **651–654:** the first political fight of the volume is not about the tide. It is about what to do with a machine that has stopped helping. Rusk moves to have the Assembly decommission the Cathedral on the grounds that a tool which cannot answer is a liability, and the vote is genuinely close. Orrin argues that a machine which is wrong about everything is still more honest than the alternative, and that dismantling it is the one act nobody will be able to reverse. He wins narrowly and is immediately given the decommissioning vote as an alibi for his ward.
- **655–658:** the Weirs ask for a Reading of their own, as a district with a restored standing, and get what everyone gets: no usable trace. Mara's old audit sheets are the best instrument anyone has. The result is worse than the machine would have given them, and they decide to proceed on arithmetic. This is the volume's first real act of the new order working, and it is quiet.
- **659–664:** the private ward. Orrin's concealed plan, built at night, technically successful. This is the last stretch in which the reader could still believe he has solved it his own way.

### 665–679: the correction

Fifteen chapters, and the middle of the volume is a reversal rather than an escalation.

- **665–670:** Mara finds the number. The cost of Orrin's ward is real, it is on the Weirs, and the Weirs agreed to a different number. She does not denounce him in a square. She puts both numbers on the same table in front of the tenant council and lets the arithmetic speak, which is the most painful available option and the one she chooses.
- **671–675:** the retreat and the re-plan. Orrin gives up the ward. The full cost map goes up publicly, district by district, with everything in it. The Weirs vote again, on the real figure, and vote to accept. Northglass has already consented, in Volume 13, and its consent is read out loud in this room so that nobody in the Weirs has to wonder whether it was real. Orrin did not ask and is not the one who reads it. He is present and mostly silent.
- **676–679:** the tide arrives early by six hours, which is not a supernatural twist but the ordinary consequence of a spring tide, a river swell, and a deferred inspection. The interval is declared. Rusk declares his emergency. The Sluice fails.

### 680–690: the four decisions

Eleven chapters, sequenced as physical choices. Each belongs to named people and each has a witness who records it. The eleventh is the silence in the chamber afterwards, which is where the reader learns that the machine has stopped choosing and that nothing good has happened yet.

### 691–700: the ten days after

The volume is not allowed to end on victory, and the last ten chapters are the ones that make the ending credible.

- **691–695:** **cutting the command line.** The three release handles are located, and the first attempt fails because only two of the three stations will pull — the third is still inside a locked Continuity building. Getting that handle out is the whole of the third act, it takes nine days, and it is the smallest of the three and the hardest, which is the volume's last joke about institutions.
- **696–697:** Ada Vance, now twenty-six and speaking from the tenant council table as an adult, files her father's batch of unlisted records into the public archive. The arithmetic of the cost. Twenty-three funerals, by name, with the decision that surrounded each one printed beside it. The Council of Hours meets for the first time and its first act is to record rather than to decide. A fight over compensation for the Weirs blocks and the eleven days without power, and it is not resolved in this volume.
- **698:** the trials and the testimony. Rusk's procedural guilt, Anja's unabsolved cooperation, Ivo Pell's late and expensive testimony, Sel Kade's diversion answered for. The camera is mostly on people in rooms, which is where this book has always said its important things happen.
- **699:** the Cathedral opens. The threshold spring is catalogued and a school class is taken to see it. The machine is not smashed on camera; it is labelled, which is duller and truer.
- **700:** the bells out of order, the removed prediction plate, Mara asking the time. Full text in section 8.

## 4. The final climax: Chapters 680–690

The climax is a sequence of physical choices rather than a single battle with the machine.

### First decision: the Weirs

Mara and Leda persuade the **Weirs tenant council** — not the district time council, not Orrin, not the Cathedral — to open the district's old flood doors. The doors are not safe; they were built for a smaller tide, and Volume 07 recorded that nobody has tested them since. Teo's repair crews volunteer to clear them by hand, which takes the whole of one night. The vote is six to three. The three dissenting members are given first evacuation, guaranteed transport, and a place in the room where the rest of the plan is being made, and they are not overruled. The choice is made by the people who will live with the flood, on a number they were given in advance.

### Second decision: the hospital

Leda and Nella Rook keep Green Span's hospital clocks running by hand. They cannot guarantee every medicine arrives on time. They can publish the missing intervals and tell the wards which patients are most urgent. The hospital's reduced efficiency is a real cost, not a hidden line item.

### Third decision: the bridge

The Northglass bridge must be opened to release river pressure. Mara and Orrin can keep its public clock synchronized long enough for people to cross, but the central alignment will be lost. Teo's crew and regional engineers prepare an empty industrial basin as the new pressure route. The bridge's collapse is unavoidable after the basin opens.

### Fourth decision: Rusk and the First Hour

The **command line** carries the physical lock order straight into the chamber, past every local separation the districts built in Volume 13, and the line has never been cut. Rusk does not need the district stations to do it; his seizures of them are for a different and more ordinary purpose, which is to make the refusal of an order physically hard to enforce in the rooms where people are actually standing. Anja refuses to authorize the order, but refusal is not authority, and the line does not need her. Orrin must enter the chamber with Mara while the independent stations hold the city's clocks apart. He cannot overpower the safety rule. He can only take out the **routing plates** that let it treat unlisted pressure as disposable.

Three acts are required, and they are not interchangeable. The volume is built so that each happens in the right order and the reader can see why the others would have failed alone:

- **Holding the clocks apart — necessary, temporary, and somebody else's work.** A consensus lock succeeds by making the network agree. A network deliberately out of phase has nothing to agree about, so the order arrives and cannot be executed against the whole city. This is why the districts' separation, argued and minuted in public in Volume 13, is the thing that actually saves people. It does nothing to the machine, and it stops working the moment the tide is past.
- **Taking out the routing plates — necessary, permanent, and the only irreversible act.** The plates are what route omitted work into the reservoir. With them out, the return line is dead, the feedback float stops rising, and the threshold spring stops tightening. Without this the reservoir keeps feeding the rule and the same clamp returns at the next surge, under the next Continuity Minister, with nobody left alive who remembers this. It is the only act that makes the ending durable.
- **Cutting the command line — necessary, and not enough on its own.** The three-station kill-switch removes the authority to give an order. It does not touch the rule, which is why the Council of Hours that inherits it is a slow, arguable institution rather than a switch. It is the smallest of the three acts and the easiest to postpone, and the volume should say so: it takes nine days and most of them are spent getting one handle out of a locked room.

The plates are fused into the engine. Removing them destroys the central forecast system permanently and risks a final slippage. Orrin and Mara do it together: he holds the mechanical timing, she records the change and the people affected. The engine stops trying to choose for the city.

### Final cost

The plan contains the tide, but not without loss:

- Twenty-three people die during the bridge and basin transition. Their names and the decisions that surrounded their deaths are entered into the public ledger.
- The Weirs lose power for eleven days and several blocks of the district flood.
- Northglass rail service is interrupted for a season.
- The Cathedral's central Consequence Engine is permanently fused as a binding oracle.
- Orrin loses the unique ability to hear a single machine cleanly. He can still repair, teach, and coordinate, but he cannot retreat into private certainty.
- Mara's former Office of Public Reckoning post is not restored. She accepts the independent **Civic Time Keeper** role, which is outside both the Directorate and the Council of Hours.
- Rusk is removed from office and placed on trial for the use of emergency powers, sealed questions, and the Censor Gate. His defeat is institutional and legal, not a magical execution.
- Anja Voss testifies about the Directorate's concealment and accepts a permanent loss of authority. She is not absolved.
- Teo's network becomes a licensed, accountable repair cooperative. It remains capable of illicit action, but it now has a public method.

## 5. Relationship resolution

### Orrin and Mara

Their relationship survives because both refuse to turn love into a private exemption.

- Mara does not ask Orrin to abandon the city. She asks him never to make a plan whose cost she cannot see.
- Orrin does not ask Mara to certify his readings. He accepts that she may disagree in public and remain part of the work.
- They share a civil partnership after the emergency. There is no grand wedding or performance of ownership. Mara keeps her own rooms at the former Reckoning Office, now an independent public audit desk; Orrin keeps his bench. They build a shared home because they choose daily life, not because the crisis has made them property.

Their first ordinary domestic scene is deliberately small: Mara leaves a public ledger on Orrin's bench and asks whether he is going to eat before he starts the next repair. He does. The point is that the new system gives them a life, not merely a shared emergency.

### Orrin and Leda

Leda forgives Orrin not for making his father a hero, but for finally telling her the truth about a danger before asking for her help. She remains in Green Span and becomes a designer of the new distributed medical clocks.

### Orrin and Teo

Teo never becomes Orrin's subordinate. He accepts a public seat on the repair cooperative while continuing to challenge the Council of Hours. Orrin recognizes that a system worth preserving must leave room for the person who says “you missed someone.”

### Orrin and Anja

Orrin does not forgive Anja, and she does not ask for forgiveness. Their later work is a series of careful repairs: testimony, archives, and machine rooms that cannot be quietly reopened by one director.

## 6. Final world state

### The Council of Hours

A new public institution governs the civic time network. It includes:

- rotating district delegates, including the Weirs;
- appointed operators with no command authority;
- medical and maintenance representatives;
- independent record keepers;
- a public archive that publishes every toll, omission, emergency action, and refusal;
- a maintenance budget protected from emergency diversion;
- a **kill-switch**: three release handles held by three independent stations, any two of which must be pulled to sever the command line. It removes the authority to give an order. It does not remove the rule, and the Council knows it cannot. Its first practical job, nine days after the tide, is getting the third handle out of a locked Continuity building.

The Council cannot promise safe Readings. It can require that a Reading identify its blind margin, its toll, and the people affected before it is used. It cannot turn a local forecast into a binding order. It also cannot switch anything off, and the volume's last political fight is an attempt to give it that power, which fails.

### The Cathedral

The building is stripped of the **Gate hub** and its **routing plates**, the **binding plates**, and the sealed administrative records. The First Hour chamber is not destroyed. It is opened, catalogued, and guarded, and the public may go and look at the threshold spring. The nave remains a public workshop and archive. Local instruments remain available to districts under public rules; each is a limited, non-binding tool in a Reading room. The central engine is not worshiped or destroyed for spectacle; it is retired where it stands, with its dangerous parts visible and labelled.

The original Hour Compact is restored as a public charter, but no one pretends that restoring paper undoes the Long Nine. The charter begins with the names of the people who were omitted.

### Morrowfen

The city is less synchronized in the first months after the Returning Tide. This is a real inconvenience and a visible proof that independence has costs. Neighbors argue about clocks, water, and emergency authority. The Assembly remains politically contentious. Some citizens miss the certainty of the old system. Others build illegal networks around discarded time tokens.

The resolution is not a utopia. It is a city that has a public way to argue about its clocks and a means to act when the argument is unfinished.

## 7. Mystery resolution

### Fully answered

- **The ninth tick:** the extra pulse is a physical signature of a Censor Gate opening and a reserve being pulled from a path that is not shown on the public display. It is not a prophecy.
- **The missing tolls:** the charges are real work and time removed from civic clocks and bodies. The hour market and the Directorate's “calibration” language conceal the same fact.
- **The Unlisted:** the Archive contains real people and work omitted from civic models, not disposable placeholders.
- **The Long Nine:** a storm and failing gates exposed a Censor Gate already in use. Edwin Vale opened a manual bypass to keep the flood from taking more lives; the nine-minute interruption was the cost, not a magical strike or an act of sabotage.
- **The authorization:** the first use of the Gate was authorized by the six-member Emergency Continuity Committee under Continuity Order 9-17. **Rusk was not one of the six.** He was the Assembly's emergency secretary who drafted, numbered, circulated, and countersigned the order as the record, and later directors expanded the temporary rule without renewed consent. The six are all dead. He is the only one left who can be asked what it was for, which is why he is tried and why he cannot be.
- **The Dark Reservoir:** it stores the physical stress of excluded labor and lost time. It is not a collection of alternate futures. The **return line** carries the strain there; the **feedback float** rides higher as the unpaid work accumulates; the **feedback line** presses on the First Hour's **threshold spring**. The chain is mechanical, continuous, and the reason the machine gets more aggressive exactly as the city gets more dishonest.
- **The First Hour:** it is the original anti-cascade rule, a fixed safety sequence narrowed by years of bad data and reservoir pressure. It becomes dangerous because it treats a stable sequence as more important than consent.
- **Rusk's role:** he did not invent the Censor Gate or the First Hour. He used the existing fear surrounding the Long Nine to turn a limited emergency tool into a regional authority.

### Intentionally unresolved

- The exact private thoughts of the original Cooperative engineers cannot be recovered.
- The missing lived intervals of people who suffered body tolls cannot all be returned. Some families know what was taken; some do not.
- Whether the First Hour had anything like awareness remains ambiguous. The story confirms a rule, a feedback threshold, and a calibration history, and refuses the word *memory* as anything but a figure of speech. What the machine cannot be given is a motive, and the volume must not reward anyone for arguing that it had one.
- Morrowfen must continue to decide how much uncertainty it can afford. That is a continuing civic question, not a dangling supernatural mystery.

## 8. Final image

Months after the Returning Tide, Orrin repairs a small public clock in the Clockwork Quarter. The Cathedral stands behind him, quiet and open. He removes the old prediction plate and leaves the hands free.

The city bells do not ring together. The Weirs bell sounds a minute late. Green Span answers. Northglass answers on its own schedule. People pause, correct their watches, and keep walking.

Mara arrives from the independent audit desk in the former Reckoning Office carrying a ledger. She asks what time it is.

Orrin cannot hear the First Hour. He looks at the clock she has repaired, then at the sky, then at her.

“Time enough to decide,” he says.

The bells continue out of order. The city acts.

## 9. Ending guardrails

- Do not restore the Cathedral as a benevolent ruler or secret higher power.
- Do not let Orrin solve the final conflict alone; the point is a distributed practice.
- Do not erase the twenty-three deaths or the eleven-day blackout. The city is safer by a different route, not by magic.
- Do not use a new enemy, god, invasion, or cosmic threat in the final volume.
- Do not make Mara's romance erase her work or Orrin's final cost.
- Do not end with a new prophecy. End with a decision, a repaired machine, and a city capable of continuing without a single answer.
- Do not let the ending imply the machine was switched off. Three acts were required and the kill-switch only reaches the order, not the rule.
- Do not let any single district be made the city's sacrifice. The physical losses are real and they are spread; what is refused is a plan that chooses the victims without asking them.
