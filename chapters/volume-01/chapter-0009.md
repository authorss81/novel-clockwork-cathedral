# Chapter 9 — What the Ward Can Absorb

By nine o'clock that night the Lower Weirs had fallen three minutes, eleven minutes, and then — the stutter — and there was no way to say how far back it went because the district's own clocks had been corrected so often out of work that was not on the book that nobody there had a clean idea what on time meant any more.

The stutter was the new thing. Repeating and skipping, over and over, at intervals that were not random but were also not regular, so that a tram signal two streets apart from a church clock would agree for four minutes and then be a minute and a half apart, and a man leaving work would come round the corner and find his street clock showing a time that had not happened yet.

Orrin got there at half past nine with his case and a lamp and no plan, and by ten he had one, and it was not a good one.

The pumping district had nothing. That was the arithmetic at the bottom of everything and every other fact sat on top of it. The reserve that had once stood behind Weir Two was gone; the water schedule was gone; the clocks had been corrected for twenty years by borrowing from shifts that were not in any ledger, and a district that borrows its time from nowhere is a district with no margin, and a district with no margin cannot absorb a slippage, because absorbing one requires holding still, and the Weirs could not hold still because the moment it tried to, the correction went looking for the nearest unregistered shift, and there were five men on a board at that pump house and they were not certified and their hours were not owed to anybody, so that was where the correction landed.

It was not the Cathedral's fault. He was careful about that, because he had gone over it four times on the walk down and would go over it a hundred more times later. The Reading had charged three district minutes and a witness interval, and that was all it had charged, and the three minutes had come out of the Clockwork Quarter, which had a full reserve and had gone on keeping perfect time by Friday morning. Nothing that had happened on the seventeenth had come back for these people. What had come back was eighteen years.

He walked the line with his hands on the iron: the lamp standards, the district clock at the end of the lane, the junction box at the pumping ward, the sub-clock on the works wall. At the works wall the hourline was physically vibrating, a fine high shivering in the copper, and it was not a fault he could have named with a gauge.

And the hospital line was in it too, because the hospital line hung off the same district as everybody else, and it crossed the canal on the old iron trolley trestle, and the trestle had a failing load that had been quietly eating the hospital's clock pressure for eleven years, and nobody had looked at it because looking would cost money and would have to be certified, and the person who could look at it had spent four years being the cheapest man in the guild.

---

Sel Kade was in the pump house and had been since six, and she had the manner of a woman who has been holding a room together with her shoulders since supper.

"Orrin Vale," she said. "You're the man who filed the question."

"I am."

"Then you'll want to know that there's no reserve here and there was none on Friday and there will not be one tonight, and that is not because of the Cathedral and it is not because of you." She put a hand flat on the board on the wall, on the chalk, without looking at it. "There is no reserve because I moved it in the spring, to Carrow Street, to keep the second-stage shed from going under every time the river comes up. That's seventy-one people on that street. Seventy-one. And when the pumping shed goes under, seventy-one kitchens get water in them and about forty of them are women who work in the morning." She took her hand off the board. "So you can put that in your hour. There is no reserve in this district, and there is a reason, and the reason is that I would rather be able to say to seventy-one people that I kept their water out than be able to say that I kept the schedule correct."

"That's a good reason," Orrin said.

"It is a reason," said Sel Kade. "You have not asked me for a good reason. You have asked me for water, and I am telling you that the water went to a place, and if you want it back you can tell me which of my seventy-one gets it instead."

"I am not here to take your reserve."

"Then what are you here for?" She said it almost gently. "Because the clocks in this district have gone to pieces in a night, and there is nothing in the three of us that will put them back, and you are the man who put three minutes into a bridge, so you are the man who knows how this works, and I am asking you plainly, in front of Bevin and the boy, whether you can do anything. Because I cannot."

From the back, where she had been standing with her coat on and a pencil out, Mara Venn said, "And nobody has asked why the Cathedral is not being telephoned."

"I did not think you would," Orrin said.

"I have audited a stair for nine years and I have watched a great many people not think it. So say it into the room, in order, and let the room decide whether the reasons are good ones." She was writing. "Say why there is no Reading tonight."

"Three reasons, and none of them is anybody's fault." He put his case down on the bench, which was the way he spoke when he wanted a room to listen. "One: a Reading is a model of the conditions present when the question goes in. Those conditions are changing by the minute tonight — the water is up two inches since I came down the slipway, the stage is at a different count, the trestle is dropping. A notice produced now would be true of a city that stops existing about the time the paper comes off the spindle, and there is no point in a true answer about a place that is not there.

"Two: there is no rail. The witness rail is booked a day ahead. There is no operator on the Hill tonight who is not already on a rail somewhere else, and there is no resident of this district to stand in the third station, because the third station is filled by a Green Span resident on a rota, and I know that because I watched it happen, and it happened to me."

Kade made a small sound. Orrin did not look at her.

"Three: the fee comes off a district reserve, and the reserve is the thing that has failed. If I filed a question tonight it would draw from this district, and this district has nothing to draw, and the machine would take what it could get and the notice would go out and we would be six minutes further out." He put his hand flat on the case. "So the honest position is that Morrowfen has an instrument for this and it cannot be pointed at us tonight, and the reason it cannot be pointed at us is that this district has no standing and no reserve, and both of those are on a piece of paper, and the piece of paper is in a drawer in a building on the Hill, and I cannot get at it, and neither can you, and that is the whole of it."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Teo Arden, from his post by the door, said, "That is the first time anybody has stood in this building and said out loud that the machine cannot be asked about us."

"Yes," said Orrin.

"Right," said Teo. "I am not going to argue with it tonight. I am going to remember it, because it is the single most useful thing I have heard in three days, and I will be in a better position to be angry about it tomorrow."

---


Leda came on the tram from Green Span at half past ten with a lamp of her own and a folded chart and the particular cold fury of a competent woman who has been told a thing is fine by a machine.

The ward's three clocks had gone out of one another. The public clock on the corridor wall, the staff clock at the nurses' office, and the little ward clock by the door, which is the one the medicine is drawn off, and they had been in agreement on Friday and were now eight minutes apart from each other, and the two of them that were wrong were wrong in opposite directions, and the medicine round on the little one was being written on a slip that was also timed by a bell on the main line, so a dose could be given at a moment the building had two opinions about.

"How long have you got me for?" Orrin said.

"Until one o'clock the ward is fine," said Leda, "because I have written the round by hand off the medicine book and the nurses are reading the labels. After one o'clock the labels stop agreeing with the bells and I am guessing, and I do not guess with insulin." She put the chart on the pump house bench and opened it. "Here. I have done this before. Not this, but this shape. Here is what the ward can absorb, and I want it read out loud, in front of these people, so that nobody can say afterwards that nobody said it."

She put her finger on a line.

"Forty minutes. That is the total slack in that ward between the free clocks and the line clocks, across eleven patients and three staff, before a dose given on the wrong clock is a dose given at the wrong time. Forty minutes is not a philosophical figure. It is the sum of what is left over in eleven people who are asleep and are owed their sleep." She looked up. "That is the whole of what a ward has. It is not more on a good night. It is forty minutes on the best night of the year, and it does not go in a book, and there is no column for it, and I have asked for a column for it four times."

"How long do you need the line clocks out?"

"Six hours, to let the trestle's feed come off the line without dragging anything with it. Six hours of free-running ward clocks. That is six hours of the forty minutes being spent in about eleven hours, which I can just about survive, and I want it written down that I said just about." She folded the chart. "And I want whoever is keeping the record to write *forty minutes of ward slack, and two hours of it unbudgeted, spent tonight by Leda Vale, in a building with no name on it.*"

She did not sit down.

"Before you say anything else," she said to him, and not unkindly, "you are going to do this with the thing you did on Wednesday night. I can see it on you. You are going to put a hand on a pipe and know something, and then nine men are going to take their coats off because you were right, and if you are wrong we will find out at four in the morning with a bridge on top of us. That is not a character question and I am not having it, we have had that argument since you were twenty-four. It is a method question, and I have got one, and it is this: if I am going to be the number you spend, then say the number out loud in a room before you spend it, and let the people whose hours they are put their names to it, because a nurse who is told at two in the morning that her ward is being used is a nurse, and a nurse who is told at seven that her ward has already been used is a wage packet."

"That is fair," Orrin said.

"It is not fair, it is cheap, and I am giving it to you anyway because it is cheaper than the other one." She sat down at last. "Now do it."

"I'll write it," said Mara Venn, from the back, where she had been standing with her coat on and a pencil out, "and I will put my name beside yours, and if anybody wants to know what an unlicensed clockmaker's word is worth, it is worth exactly as much as a ward's forty minutes and I will say so in a room."

---

Teo Arden came in at eleven with nine people, because he had spent four hours going up and down the lower lanes on foot telling people what was happening, and because nine people came, and none of them were licensed, and two of them had done the Cathedral's own dampers last year for eleven shillings a day and had the crate of tools to prove it.

He would not look at Orrin. He stood with his back to a post and listened, and when Orrin laid out what he was going to do he did not interrupt, and when Orrin stopped and asked whether anybody had a better idea, he said, "I've got one," and then did not say it, and Orrin did not press him, and that was also noted.

"Right," said Orrin, and moved to the middle of the floor, where a pump house is loudest and where nine men and a warden and a nurse and an investigator could all hear him without raising their voices, because he wanted it written down afterwards and the only way to be sure of the words was to say them in the middle of the noise. "I am going to tell you four things. Take the fourth one away and throw it away if you want, because I am not able to stop anybody taking it.

"One. I know which assembly has failed. Not what has failed. I know that the load on the conduit bracket on the trestle is the load that is going to let go, and I know it because I put my hand on it, and I cannot prove that to anybody in this room with anything, and I have no gauge for it and no certificate for it and no standing at all, and the only reason I am saying it out loud instead of keeping it is that I have been wrong twice in three days in ways that cost a man his life and I am not going to be the reason a hospital goes into the canal in the dark.

"Two. What I do not know. I do not know whether the second stage will hold to morning. I do not know whether the shed on Carrow Street floods. I do not know how long the ward is in trouble for, and I am not the person who knows that, and Leda is."

"Three. Which of my readings is a guess. The bracket is a reading. Everything I said about the gate and the water on the seventeenth is now history and I can't touch it. The half of this I am sure of is the half I could measure, and the measure is on the back of that shutter with a tape against it, and anybody can go and look at it, and if I am wrong about the bracket, the tape will say so before midnight.

"Four. What it costs. To take the trestle's feed off the line I have to hold this district at one rate by hand for six hours, and that means somebody winds these clocks by hand all night, and that means the men who would be starting work at five are starting at six, and that hour belongs to the men on this street and not to me and I am not able to pay it back. And the ward is spending forty minutes of slack, and about two hours of it is not budgeted at all. If it goes wrong instead of right, there is a woman on the second stage shed and a hundred and forty men on that works, and I will have spent the district's margin on a bracket."

He looked round at them.

"Mrs Kade, I am not asking for your reserve. I am telling you that it is not there, and that I have known since I saw the slip this morning, and that I would not have asked for it if it were."

"I would have said no," said Sel Kade.

"I know."

---

He went up to the trestle at half past eleven with two of Teo's men and a lamp and a sounding line, and the trestle was doing what he had known it would do, which was nothing, in the dark, in the rain, and everything, in a way that only took a tape measure and a man who was prepared to be shown a number.

The span had dropped at the third bracket. Not much: three-eighths of an inch, which is nothing to a railway and a great deal to a conduit. The supports for the conduit had pulled two of their four fixings out of the iron over the course of about a year and a half, and the conduit had been leaning on the third fixing ever since, and the third fixing was a cast bracket of a pattern that had not been made in thirty years, and it was tired.

He put his hand on the conduit and asked it the way he had asked it at Weir Two, in the plainest words he could find, and said the words out loud on a wet bridge at midnight with two unlicensed men and a lamp, which was the first time in his life he had asked anything aloud:

"If that bracket is asked to carry the line until morning, what fails first?"

And it came up the copper the way it had come up the bedplate, and it was a short answer this time, and it was not a vision and it had no light in it: the third fixing, and then the conduit, and then the deck, and after the deck the only thing in the picture was a ward clock two minutes fast and a bell that rang by itself.

He took his hand off and said, "There. That is what I have. I have a feeling and a tape. Measure it."

They measured it. The span had dropped three-eighths of an inch. Teo's man with the tape called the number out loud twice and wrote it on the back of a shutter with a carpenter's pencil, and put his name under it, which was more than Orrin could do with anything.

Then they worked.

Teo's men had a bearing block off a dead tram bogie and a length of conduit strap and a shim of the right steel. Leda's ward provided a hand key and a woman called Marta Dey who had held the lamp at the top of the ladder while Perry Vance went down on the night of the seventeenth and who now held the light and would not let anybody take it off her. Bevin Sarr went to the top of the lane and hand-wound the four street clocks in turn while the rest of them held the rate, and did it nine times an hour, all night, and refused, twice, to be thanked for it.

The third fixing came out at ten past one with a hammer and a drift, and the bracket came with it in three pieces, and the cast iron was grey inside and full of pits, and Orrin put it in Sarr's hand without a word and she looked at it and said, "Two years of that and you'd have a bridge on the canal," and handed it back, and neither of them said anything else about it for a long time.

The new block went in at half past three. He shimmed it by ear and by tape, and then he did the thing that he would spend the rest of his life arguing was the only moral act of repair there is, and which cost him the second of his two charges: he put a sacrificial plate on the front of the bracket, a plain flat plate of soft iron, not to hold anything, but so that when the load came back it would find a plate to bend instead of a fixing to pull, and he told them what it was for, and made the boy who put it in write his own name on the back of it, because a ward that names its sacrificial plate is a ward that can find out later who owns it.

Then, at five, the light came up grey over the river and they stood the conduit load off the line and the line came back into the district and held.

---

He felt the second one coming and had time to be afraid of it, which the first had not allowed him.

It came out of the bracket and up his arm and into his chest, and it was not four seconds this time. It was the whole night, the whole six hours he had just held in his own hand, the forty minutes of a ward's slack and the hour of six men's morning and a shunt plate with a boy's name on it and a third fixing broken in two in a woman's fist, all of it taken out of him at once, as though somebody had opened a tap.

He sat down on the wet boards of the trestle with his back against the parapet and could not get his right hand to close. He got it open on the fourth try. He stayed there for a quarter of an hour while the sky went the colour of a coin and the tram started up the hill, and he could not make it close, and he understood that it was not going to close for two days and that it was going to be worse than that if he did it again.

His right ear was ringing at a pitch that was there and not there, and he understood, sitting on the trestle in the wet, that it was not going to go. The three weeks of it before had been a symptom. This was a thing he would carry, and it would be there above a certain note and it would be duller when he was tired, and in ten years he would be a man who turned his head to the wrong side in a shop.

Teo Arden sat down on the boards on the other side of him, and did not speak for a while, and neither did Orrin.

"You said it would go," the boy said at last. "The bracket. On the pavement. You said it and then you asked somebody to measure it, and it did, and you didn't gloat, which I was ready for."

"It isn't mine to be right about," Orrin said. "I just get told. Ask the man who wrote the number on the shutter."

"I'll ask him." The boy wiped his face with his sleeve. "Here's what I want to say, and then I'm going to go and sleep for two days like a sane man. I'm not thanking you for last night. There is no version of this where anybody thanks anybody. My master is dead and there is a bridge standing and I do not know yet which of those two things I am supposed to feel, and I have had two nights to find out and I have failed twice, and I think that is going to be the rest of my life." He stood up. "But you stood in the middle of that floor and told nine people, out loud, what you did not know and what it would cost them, and the man who did the last question on that floor, in the year I was nine, did not do that. He read the answer out and went out to the gate. And you have been in this district for four days and you have cost us three minutes out of your own quarter and you have written our master's name in a book of yours, and I do not know what to do with you, and I have decided to leave you alone until I know."

"That is a generous offer," Orrin said.

"It is not generosity," said Teo Arden. "It's an audit. Don't mistake it."

---

They came down off the trestle at seven and the clocks held, and the district's time was eleven minutes out and correctable by a man with a bar in an hour, and Bevin Sarr chalked the night's hours on the board, and there were eleven people on the pump house floor being fed by Marta Dey out of a pan, and Leda Vale was on the tram with a chart rolled under her arm going back to a ward that had spent forty minutes of slack and about two hours unbudgeted, and the district was going to work an hour late, and there was a third fixing in a bucket on a bench and a shunt plate with a name on it and a shutter with a number and two names on it, and every one of those was written down somewhere, and Orrin understood as he stood in the middle of the floor eating somebody's breakfast that the total of the night was legible.

He went to the bench at ten past nine and opened the grey book and put it on the end of the counter where the light was best.

*The twentieth, and the night after it. Weir Two and the hospital trestle. Second-stage pump stopped, on a night, in order to take a tired bracket out of a conduit and put a new block in.*

*Ward slack, Green Span, forty minutes. Two hours of it unbudgeted. Spoken by Leda Vale, and written down by me, and the witness is the street.*

*District clocks hand-wound, nine times an hour, B. Sarr. Men's morning an hour short. Nobody's name on that line but theirs, and it is theirs.*

*Cost: three minutes? no. Not minutes. Hours. The question is how many hours, and to whom, and in what column, and there is no column.*

He wrote it out and then, under it, in the margin where the oddities went:

*Nothing of mine is used again without its blind margin and its toll named in public, in a room, to somebody who can object.*

He read that twice, and thought it was probably too high a standard for a man who kept the only copy of it in a shop with a lease, and then he thought: *it is written down now, which is the only part that was missing*, and that was the end of his night's work.

There were nine people in the district who had lost an hour of the morning, and eleven in a hospital ward who had spent forty minutes they could not get back, and a woman on a shift who had lost a night, and a district that had lost the whole of its margin and was still on time because the clock on the chandler's window was eight minutes fast and nobody in the Weirs could prove it wasn't correct.

Nobody had been charged for any of it. Nobody would be charged for any of it. There was a number on the Toll Stair at the top of the Rise that said three, and the number was true, and the number was the whole of what the city knew.
