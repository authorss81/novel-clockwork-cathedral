# Chapter 27 — The Licence, Renewed

He was in the shop at seven on the Sunday morning, and the lathes were cold until nearly half past eight, and he had let the front door in for the air.

He had the W-4 on the bench with the second sheet under it, and the fee in a tin on the corner — four shillings and sixpence, in coin, counted twice in nineteen days — and the grey book open on the end of the bench with a prop against it.

The four things at the head of the form took him four minutes, and the first eight lines of the table took him fifty, because he does nine hundred of these a year and a line is a machine and a fault and a piece of work and he can write one in the time it takes to look up a thing in his own book.

He was not dawdling. He had done a fair copy of a passage of a labour book correctly once, in a year when he was seventeen, for a man who could not read it, and he had done nine hundred a year since for his own shop, and the whole of the skill is in not writing down what the page says but what the page *establishes*. The trouble was that this was not a copy. This was a selection, and a selection is a judgement, and a judgement written in a form with three columns is a judgement that a stranger will read in about two years with an interest in his own good name.

The form had four things printed at the head of it — holder, class, bench, term — and beneath them a ruled table of three columns, and the columns were *fault observed*, *work performed*, and *recommendation*, and the recommendation column was a third of an inch wider than the other two because the guild had found out in about 1908 that men will say anything at all if the box is big enough.

There were forty lines. He counted them twice, because that is the sort of thing he does, and then he looked at the clock on the wall of his own shop, which is a good movement and four days from being a week, and worked out that at the rate he had just gone at he could have all forty in about four hours, and that it did not matter, because two of them were going to take the whole morning and he knew which two before he began them.

---

The grey book was two thousand pages. He knew the figure because he had counted the leaves in the spring and had not seen the number change much in a year.

Two thousand pages, and a hundred and nine of them in the last four weeks, and thirty-eight of those hundred and nine about other people's hours and not about machines at all.

Forty lines.

That is the whole of it and he knew it by about the second cup of tea, and it is worth writing down what it felt like to sit with it, because the feeling is the finding and not the arithmetic: it did not feel like being overwhelmed. It felt like being asked to choose, which is a different thing, and which is much worse, because a man can be right about everything in the book and still choose the wrong forty lines and there is nobody in the building who is ever going to tell him that he chose.

He did what any competent man would do. He took the faults on civic machines for the year, in date order, one line each, and he wrote the first eight inside fifty minutes because they were the ordinary work of a shop — a regulator at the Market Gardens, a school clock at Northglass, a landing light in an arcade, a pump at the ropeworks, the parish clock at Chapel Weir, a signal box at the junction of Weir Lane and Carrow Street, a chandler's regulator, and the pillar clock on Halloway Street.

The eighth line was the Halloway Street pillar clock, and it took a line and a half, and it is the truest thing in the book this year.

*Halloway Street pillar clock. Within tolerance on four readings, the district file being three minutes short of the truth, as this maintainer certified in the first month. I corrected two minutes by hand, with a bar, on a Sunday night some days afterwards, in about eleven minutes, on the tail of the works' visit. No receipt, no form, charged to no district. Hand correction, not a fault. Nothing further recommended.*
>
*Second hand correction entered in this book in nineteen years. The other is a bracket clock at the Ferrywright corner, eight minutes, the same week. Neither is on a form and neither is on a receipt, and this form has no column for it, which is why it is written here, and this is the only copy of either of them in any office in this city.*

That was the last of the forty that was going to be easy. Everything after it on that form was going to be about other people's hours and there was nothing on the form for that either.

---

The ninth line took until half past eleven and then stopped, because it was not a line, it was three.

*Green Span hospital, third-floor landing. Auxiliary rising main, vertical spindle. Gland packing renewed and spindle drawn and reseated by E. Reeve, hospital engineer, second month, 7th, at my direction, my hand, at my request, and on the record, the packing having been renewed by her the previous summer in a manner she states was wrong. Spindle within tolerance. No defect in the certified sense. Work performed: packing renewed, gland drawn, spindle reseated, four courses of hospital white to the gland face.*

And then the recommendation, which he wrote four times, and the fourth time is the one on the form.

*Recommended: that the rising main be isolated for re-survey, and that a working window upon the Lower Weirs pumping main be certified for the purpose. A maintainer of class three may certify the machine and may not certify a district toll, and I have no standing to certify this window, and the pumping ward's reserve has been committed since the diversion. Recommended further: that the main be re-surveyed against a pumping day of four cycles before any further packing is done, the gland having been renewed for a fortnight's duty and not for a year.*

He read it back twice. It was the honest entry. It was the entry that cost the most and helped the least, and he worked out exactly how the cost was made up, on the end of the bench, with a pencil, because he is that kind of man.

*It costs: Ellen Reeve, who will be asked what the maintainer wrote, and who asked for her own work to be on the record and has got it, and who did not ask to be mentioned in a recommendation in a hall in the Clockwork Quarter, and who cannot now be unmentioned.*

*It costs: a document. In three years, in a room, with somebody in it who wants an answer, there will be a piece of paper with my name on it saying that a hospital on the Green Span line could not be repaired because a district could not be got to certify four minutes' opening, and there is no version of that which is not an argument somebody could use against me, and there is also no version of it which is not true, and I could put my name to no other true version of it that is shorter.*

*It helps: nobody, this month. It is a recommendation in a work book to be transcribed by a copyist at fourpence a page into a fair copy that goes on a shelf.*

*It is the correct thing to write.*

He left it in.

---

*Lower Weirs, Weir Two floodgate and second-stage pumping station. Fault observed: hand valve spindle, and second-stage gland packing, both serviceable, both worn at the gland. No fault in the certified sense of the term. Work performed: none by me. The station is maintained nightly by persons of this district whose shift is not entered in the certified shift returns of the district and is not, so far as I can find, entered in any other return of any office in this city.*

*Recommended: that the maintaining hall note that the works' certified shift return for this station is not consistent with the station's own board; that the pumping district be advised that a district's own return is now the only return that records its night work; and that no maintainer of class three be asked to certify any line of it, my licence extending to one civic machine and no further.*

Then he stopped, with the pencil on the end of the word *further*, for about four minutes.

He was aware, in the way you are aware of a sound in a room you had not noticed, of what the sentence was.

It was eleven years' worth of the pumping district's night work entering an institutional record. Not a copy of the hall's book — worse, and better, and a different thing altogether: a licensed maintainer of the Clockwork Quarter, in a form in the hall's form, in his own hand, on a Sunday morning in a cold shop with a lease, stating on paper that the night work exists. It had gone in. It was not going to come out. It would be transcribed at fourpence a page, in a small upright hand, and signed, and bound, and it would sit in the hall's series room in the same brown bindings as everything else, and from this afternoon the pumping district's hours existed in an office in this city, and no vote had been taken, no meeting had been called, no person had been asked, and no one had decided anything.

*A result of mine*, said the rule, *goes out of this room without a person named who can refuse it.*

He looked at the room. Two benches, a vice, a wall of lathes, a sign, a lease, a grey book with a prop against it, a tin with four shillings and sixpence in it, and no person in it at all.

He sat with it for about ten minutes and worked out the hole in his own rule, and the hole was not small and it was not a surprise.

*A fault observed on a civic machine is a job. A form is not a room. And I have just written a result into a third column and there is nobody here who can refuse it, and the rule I wrote four weeks ago does not stop me, because the rule was written by a man who was frightened of speaking and not by a man who understood what a form is.*

He did not tear it out. He read the whole of the entry back from the top, and he added one line at the foot, in the same hand, which is the only honest place to put a thing like that.

*Entered of my own motion and with nobody able to refuse it. I am the source. If this is used against the pumping district I will have put it there.*

And then, because he was still a clockmaker, and because the phrase is the only one that carries the size of the thing:

*I am not going to take it out, because it is true, and because a rule that you only keep when it works is not a rule, and because a woman told me that in a corridor three weeks ago and she was right, and because if I take it out then the only record of what I have found is a book in a shop with a lease, and that was not good enough in the month before last and it is not good enough now.*

He put the entry in.

Then he filled the other thirty lines with faults on machines, because that is what the book is for and the ordinance says so in the standing request, and it took him fifty minutes, because a line is a machine and a fault and a piece of work and he can write one in the time it takes to look up a thing in his own book.

At two o'clock on the afternoon of the nineteenth of the third month of a year in which nothing whatever had happened to him he read the whole thing through from the top, twice, at the speed of a man who has been doing this for nineteen years, and squared it, and put the second sheet under it, and put the tin on the counter by the door, and got his coat, and walked down to Verge Lane with the book in a carrier, and sat down on the bench outside the guild hall at twenty-five to three.

And then sat on it, because the counter took renewals between ten and twelve and it was twenty-five to three, and the light went at about four, and the hall's street lamp came on at a quarter to five, and the porter who had let him in at six o'clock came past twice and looked at the book across his knees and did not ask him anything, because a man sitting on a bench outside a hall with a book is a thing a porter has seen before.

A bench outside a hall is a very good place to fill in a form. Nobody can see your hand, there is no light after four, and a man in a good coat going in and out of a door all day has a reasonable amount of practice at not looking at a bench.

And then, on the front of the sheet, in the space under the four printed things: *Work book, hall form W-4, current issue. Annual term, maintained by the holder, complete to the date of lodgement.*

And on the second sheet, which is the hall's return copy, and which is not asked for and which a man on a counter will hand you over because he can see you have not thought of it: the same, and the date, and his name, and the class, and the fee.

He was on that bench from twenty-five to three until about ten to ten, in the rain, on the last day of his licence, and he was the only one on it, and it was the seventh hour of a bench.

---

The licensing counter opens on Sundays for licensing, and there is no law saying that it does and no law saying that it does not, and it opens at ten, and the notice on the wall of the licensing floor says it takes renewals between ten and twelve on the day of expiry, and the nineteenth of the third month was a Sunday.

There was a porter at the door of the hall on Verge Lane who looked at a clock inside and said, "Renewals are shut," and Orrin said, "It's within the hour," and the porter said, "It is, and there is nobody else here, and I am going to unlock the counter and go home, and you have got until twelve and the master's in by arrangement." And he unlocked the counter and went home, and there was a clerk behind it who was about twenty-five and who had a boiled egg going cold on a saucer beside the inkwell, and no other man in the licensing floor, and no light on in the front of the building at all.

"Vale," said Orrin. "Class three. Renewal."

"Ten to twelve for renewals," said the clerk, "and you're in, go on." He took the fee without counting it in front of him, and put it in a tin, and the tin went into a drawer with a lot of other tins, and then he turned the two sheets over and read the front of the first one the way a clerk reads a form, which is quickly and in the right order.

"Twenty-two blanks is the whole stack," he said. "There's no more coming this month. You've brought the second sheet, which is more than most."

"Second sheet's signed, which is more than most," said the clerk, "and if you have not signed the top of the first, sign it in front of me, because I would rather see a man do it than find out in a year that he didn't." He put a small green slip in front of him. "Sign there, and the master signs under, and that's it done, and you'll have your receipt and it's stamped, and there's no charge for the book and there's no charge for the stamp."

Then the door at the back opened, and it was Hask.

---

He did not mention the interview. Not once, and not by any way of not mentioning it, and Orrin understood that this was not a coincidence, and that Hask had put a date in his own hand on a card five weeks ago and had said that nothing follows from it, and that he would say the same thing if he were asked in a year, and would offer the same twenty minutes in his own hand, and would be glad of him.

"Your hand's better," said Hask.

"It is."

"Not as good as it was. You'll get the rest of it back over a year and don't do anything stupid in the meantime." He looked at the two sheets on the counter without picking them up. "That's a full book."

"Forty lines."

"That's a full book. The men who send me a card and no book are the ones I have had to chase, and the men who send me forty lines and have clearly thought about which forty, they are the ones I have never had to chase once." He took his pen off the chain. "The fee's in. I'll endorse it."

He wrote the endorsement in the space under the four things, in a small neat hand, and it was a signature and a class and a date and nothing else, and it took him about four seconds, and there is no more of it than that, and there has not been any more of it since the twenty-fifth of the first month.

"There you are," said Gerren Hask. "A year from today, and the nineteenth, same as it was. Bench, class, address unchanged, which is more than I can say for two men of my own hall." He put the pen down. "You'll get the book back in a fortnight when the copyist's been through it, and if you want a page back you can have a page back and I'll tell her so, and if you want the whole of it back you are welcome to the whole of it, and the hall will have its copy, and I have thought about that and I am content with it, and I would like it minuted."

"Mine isn't going to the minute, Gerren."

"Then it will not be minuted," said Hask, "and the hall's will, and the difference between those two books is the entire subject of my afternoon and it is not a subject I discuss on a Sunday evening with a man who has brought me forty lines." He squared the second sheet. "That's the receipt. The stamp's the wet one and it takes a minute. Keep it. It is the only copy of the lodgement in the world, and if you lose it you will come back and I shall not be able to help you, and that is not a threat, it is how a receipt works."

---

The hall shut behind him at twenty past twelve and the rain had started again and the street was doing what the Clockwork Quarter's streets do, which is to be exactly on time in a way nobody can prove afterwards.

Orrin stood under the awning opposite the hall with a stamped receipt in his hand and a licence that was good for a year inside his coat, and found that he felt nothing at all.

Not the flatness he had braced for. He had braced for a kind of grief — for the grey book, for the shelf, for the six years of a private record — and the grief did not come, because the truth was that nothing had been taken off him. He had gone down to a counter in a hall and paid four shillings and sixpence and handed over a piece of paper and a second sheet, and had been given a green slip with a wet stamp on it, and had been endorsed by a man who thought about his morning.

The correct thing and the useful thing were two different documents. That was all. The correct thing was two thousand pages in a school exercise book on a shelf behind a bench in Verge Lane, in his own hand, in no office, unreadable by anybody including him at two in the morning.

The useful thing was a two-page form with four things printed at the head and a ruled table of three columns beneath, of which the third was a third of an inch wider than the other two because the guild had found out in 1908 that men will say anything at all if the box is big enough, and it had gone onto a cart that evening to a copyist in the back room of that building, and it would be transcribed at fourpence a page, and bound, and it would be in an office in this city for as long as the hall stands.

And he could not write in it that the front of the Lower Weirs would be running four cycles a day from the fifteenth, or that the Weir One gate's escapement governor was made for two and was thirty years older than the works' standard and would hold all the same and was the only thing in the whole of it that ought to frighten anybody, because that had come out of a brass case in a hall on the eighth with his hand on it, and there is no form in the world for that, and a boy of twenty had refused him permission to say it out loud in a room, and had been right to.

He stood in the rain on Verge Lane and read the wet stamp on the receipt.

*Licence renewed. Third month, 19th. Term: one year from the date of lodgement. Class three. M. Vale.*

There was a reason, he thought, walking up the lane, that the fee was four shillings and sixpence and not a round figure. A round figure would have been a price on a schedule. Four shillings and sixpence was a fee, which is a different thing, and the difference between a fee and a price is that a fee is what a building charges you to let you keep something that is yours, and a price is what it charges you to be allowed to do it.

He had paid a fee. The book was still his. It was in a cart in Verge Lane, and it would come back in a fortnight, and it would come back with a hole in it, and nobody in the hall had decided anything about the hole.
