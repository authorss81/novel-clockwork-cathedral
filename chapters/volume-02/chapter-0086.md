# Chapter 86 — Two Operators and a Common Question

Sabine Kaye came to the Lower Weirs on Wednesday the ninth of the eleventh month because she had been asked, and she had never been to this district, and she spent the morning walking eleven miles of main with a folding rule she had brought with her and did not use, and on the Friday she drew a map on the back of a works' counter sheet and it took four minutes and it is the same map Ruth Ashby read out in a pumping ward in the sixth month, and it has not got a man on it, and the man who sent for two operators he does not trust got a method, a map, a question in the same words, three clocks, and the discovery that a district cannot be drawn.

---

**Wednesday the ninth, about eleven in the morning, in the outer room of the pumping ward, and she is forty-four.**

Sabine Kaye holds the first class, has held it twelve years, is over a shop about nine miles off, does about four certificates a month, has a bench, two watches and a rule that is not hers and never will be. She came on a bus and got it wrong twice and said so twice, and the second time she said, *I have never been to this district and I am not going to pretend I have, and if that stops me being useful then I am not useful, and you should have asked me that question on the telephone in September and you did not and it is your fault and not mine.*

She read the three clocks' figures off the wall. She read the cost table from the fourteenth of the tenth. She read Elsie Cobb's card, which Bevin Sarr had copied out at the ward and put in a folder on the ledge beside the glass case, **beside** the toll ledger and not in it, and she read the third line of it and then read the note in the different pencil under the third line, and she read that twice.

"**This is the only instrument in this city that has ever caught anything,**" she said. "And it is a card in an apron, and it cannot be produced, and whoever holds it can lose it, and the woman who holds it is nineteen and she is paid at the ward rate. **Have you asked her whether she wants it produced?**"

"No."

"**Then do not use her card in this. Not because she says no — you have not asked her and I am not telling you what she would say. Because you have not asked her, and a document in a room that belongs to somebody who is not in the room is a thing you have taken.**"

He wrote that down. It is the only thing written down on the Wednesday.

**What she is, and what she is not.** She is not a student. She said so before he could put it into a shape: *I have been doing this for longer than you have been doing anything and I have never had a lesson and I am not having one this month.* She is not an operator in the Question Hall and has never been one and does not want to be. She is a woman who reads machines honestly.

"**I will do it for nothing and I will write nothing down,**" she said, on the Wednesday, at about half past twelve, standing up, the way people in that room stand up. "Not as a condition and not as a threat and not for a reason you will enjoy. I do four certificates a month and none of them is this. **A thing written down by nobody is worth what it is worth and I am the nobody, and that is a true sentence and I have been saying it for about eleven years and it is the reason I have four certificates and not forty.**"

---

**Thursday the tenth, about half past seven in the evening, and Ivo Pell came on a bus, at a time of his own choosing, and stood at the back with his hands behind his back for about fifty minutes before he said anything at all.**

He read the wall. He read it twice, standing up, from about four feet, which is a thing a man does who has read a great many figures off boards and does not want a chair. He read the copy of the winter rotation with the fifteen minutes on the face of it that has been on the counter of the Water Users' Committee since the seventeenth. He read the item ten line and the 1931 interlock line and the line about the tank and the alarm and the date.

Then he said the thing that was expected, and he said it in about four seconds, and it is the reason he was asked and the reason he came.

"**I am not going to countersign it, and it is not a question of the work.** A piece of paper with nobody's name at the bottom of it is worth what a man expects. I have seen about two hundred of them in twelve years and I have watched every single one of them be worth nothing, and I have watched two of them be worth something, and both of those two had a man's name on them, and one of those two had a man's name on it who was wrong."

"That is not fair," said the chandler, who had come up.

"**It is completely fair and it is not about you.**" Pell did not turn round. "The reason is not that I think Mr Vale is dishonest. The reason is that a piece of paper with nobody's name at the bottom of it cannot be held by anybody, and the second it is used it will be used by somebody who put their name on it afterwards, and the somebody will be a person this district has not got, and I will not be the man who hands it to him. **I will not sign it now and I will not sign it in the twelfth month and I will not sign it on the morning of it, and I am telling you that on the tenth so that you can stop designing things around it.**"

"**And you are right,**" said Sabine Kaye, "and it is the reason I am not going to sign it either, and my reason is not yours and I would like it written down that it is not yours."

"Go on."

"**A thing written down by nobody is worth what it is worth and I am the nobody.** And you are the nobody as well, and that is the difficulty, and neither of us is being careful — we are being clean, which is a different thing and is much more comfortable. **Between the three of us there is not one person in this district whose name could go at the bottom of that sheet and be the right name, and there has not been one for nineteen years, and the form that would make one cost two shillings the head for a year and buys a line in a list.**"

"**That is the whole of it,**" said Pell, "and I have said it in three rooms since July and I am not going to say it a fourth time, and if the two of you are going to keep saying it to each other in front of this man then I am going to go and stand outside."

He did not go outside. He stood at the back for another half hour.

---

**And then, on the Friday the eleventh, at about ten in the morning, in the warden's outer room, a woman of about thirty at a counter said the two words that had to be corrected, and a man of thirty-six corrected them from the doorway in about nine seconds, and it is the second time in nineteen years that those two words have been put into the wrong mouth by a person in this district and it is going to be the last.**

"It is a reading room," said Ambrose, waving at the three clocks and the folder on the ledge. "That is what that is. I said so in September and I was told no, and I have been thinking about it for seven weeks and I have come round."

"**No it is not,**" said Ivo Pell, from the doorway, "and I am going to correct you for the second time and it is the last time and I am going to be shorter about it. **In September I told you what it was not. A room that cannot tell you whether your question was a proper question is not a room, it is three people with watches. It is still three people with watches. Nothing has been added to it since September except a map and a woman's arithmetic, and a map does not make a spindle.**"

"Then what is it now," said Ambrose.

"**It is a rehearsal,**" said Pell. "It was a rehearsal in September. It is a rehearsal now. It has no spindle, it has no seal, it certifies nothing, it can stop nothing, and it is the safest thing anybody in this district has produced in a year, and the reason it is safe is that there is no question in it. **If you ever get a real question into it, it stops being safe, and at that point you will want a room, and there is no such thing, and that is the entire finding of this morning and it took me twelve years to be able to say it in one sentence.**"

**And that is the whole of the disagreement, and both halves of it are right, and neither of them is a teacher.**

---

**And the map, on the Friday, at about half past ten, on the back of a works' counter sheet, in four minutes and eleven seconds, and it is Ruth Ashby's map and has been in this book since the thirteenth of the sixth month.**

> *Lower Weirs, for the purposes of a rehearsal of three clocks.*
> *Eleven miles of main. Two stations, Weir One and Weir Two, with their gates. Four stopcocks. **Six clocks**, of which three are on the line and two are public and one is in an outer room. One hospital branch, entered as a draw and not as a supply, which is the auditor's own distinction and which is the only reason the ward was ever a ward.*
> *Six hundred standpipe brackets, which are fittings on services, and are not on this map because they are not an instrument, and are on the district works' own sheet at one thousand eight hundred and ninety pounds, and are on a wall in a pumping ward in chalk with the price on it.*
> *The low main, Ferrywright Row to Weir Lane, relined 1951, four stopcocks, **one of which was shut for six years and is item one on a folded piece of paper in a man's pocket and is not on this map.***
> *The four standpipes on the canal front. Twenty-seven street clocks on the front, hand-wound if they ever have to be.*
> *The boundary sluice at the Northglass works' gate: a 1911 clock-driven valve, four feet of reach, about eleven minutes cold and nine warm, with a square-headed override on the side of it. **A works' asset. On the map because a rehearsal cannot avoid it and not because this district can ask anybody about it.***
> *Sluice Row, off Weir Lane, twenty-two houses, eleven clocks, two standpipes, **fed from a brick cistern under the row itself, put in in 1889 off the canal and not off this main at all.***

**And there is no man on it, and there is no woman, and there is no house except Sluice Row, which is on it because it has its own water.**

"That is the auditor's list," said Sabine Kaye. "I have her minute. **And it is the correct list for a rehearsal, and it is the only list in this city that anybody could hand to a machine, and it is eleven miles of iron and six clocks and it has not got a person on it anywhere, and a man is not an instrument and a man's shift is not an instrument, and there is no form in this city on which either of those two sentences can be entered.**"

"And it took four minutes," said Pell. "**A district cannot be drawn. A thing that cannot be drawn is a thing that is not in the model, and a thing that is not in the model is a thing the model cannot be wrong about, and that sentence belongs to a woman in a good coat with a brass weight and it is true, and I have been on the other side of it for twelve years and I do not say it lightly.**"

"**Then what is the map for,**" said Ambrose.

"**It is for telling three people where to stand,**" said Kaye. "That is all a rehearsal map is. It is a list of places. It is not a picture of a district and it was never going to be, and the man who asked for it has been asking the wrong office for seven months, and I say that as a woman who has drawn about two hundred of them for men who did not know what they wanted."

---

**And on the Saturday the twelfth, at about eleven in the morning, the common question was agreed in the same words for the third time, and the third time was the first time anybody had checked that all three copies said the same thing, and it took nine minutes and found one word different.**

> *At the minute stated, read each clock named below and write down, in figures, what it says, against the district reference of the Cathedral public clock; and state the time of reading, and state who read it.*
> *Pumping ward, canal road, outer room. Weir Two pump house, station clock. Quarter hill, south public clock.*

**One copy said *write down* and one copy said *write*, and that is the whole of the difference, and the writer of the shorter one is not on the page and will not be, and the thing the disagreement proved is not that anybody had done anything wrong. It is that a question in the same words is not the same thing as a question in the same words, and that the only instrument in this business for finding out is two people with the two copies in their hands.**

And then the four things that were settled, and they are four lines, and none of them is a signature.

> **No toll is taken and none is owed. No keeper's toll has been taken in this district since the sixth month and the count is four and it is not moving.**
> **Nothing is entered on any rail. The station on the rail takes a person of the affected district, and there is none, and there has not been one for nineteen years, and the form that would have one is two shillings the head for a year, and **nothing about this rehearsal is going onto that rail by any other road, and the three of us know exactly what the other two roads are and neither of us is going to take them.** That is what Kaye means by a thing written down by nobody and it is why she will not sign.**
> **Nothing is filed, nothing is certified, nothing is admissible, and there is no form on which any of it could be made admissible, and no character in this book may treat one of those as a technicality that could be got round.**
> **And nothing is corrected. The count of Mr Vale's own hand corrections is two and it is two this morning; he had an opportunity on Friday with a station clock in a pump house and did not take it and wrote down that he did not, and that is the fourth time this year and he is not going to be a man who takes a third.**

---

**And on the Saturday the twelfth, at about half past seven in the evening, in a room over a chandler's shop, six people decided one thing, and it was decided by a show of hands because there is no minute book in that room newer than 1948, and it is a decision and not a plan, and here is all of it.**

> *That the three-clock rehearsal be attempted, in the eleventh month, on a date to be fixed, with those two operators, those three clocks, and that question, and that **nobody be asked to put their name to it**, and that the whole of it be done in daylight in public, with the sheet on the ledge under the glass case beside the ledger and **not in it.***
> *Cost, as chalked on the wall in the pumping ward in October: about four hours a week for five people for a month, of which nobody can be paid for any part of, because there is no ratepayer and no rate can be levied and there is no body in this district to be billed.*

"**The date is the twenty-fourth of the eleventh,**" said Pell, "and I have not chosen it and I want it written on the sheet that I did not choose it, and I am going to be there at about half past seven in the morning because that is the minute I chose, and the minute was written down in advance in September by a man who is not a teacher and has never been one."

He put his coat on.

"**And I am going to say the last thing now and then I am going, and it is for the man who asked me.**" He did not look at him. "A rehearsal cannot stop one single thing in this city. It cannot stop a schedule and it cannot stop a rate and it cannot stop a standpipe going dry in 1951 and it cannot get a person into a room. **And it is going to cost somebody something, and the somebody is a street, and there is going to come a morning when you have to stand in that street and say the number out loud, and there is nobody in this district who can be asked to do that for you and nobody in this district who can refuse you either, which is the first time all year that those two things have been the same shape.**

"**You are the man who cannot be refused. That is not a gift. It is the reason nobody is going to help you.**"

Nobody thanked him. Sabine Kaye walked out with him to the top of the stairs and they were gone for about nine minutes and came back separately and neither of them said what had been said.

**And on the Sunday the thirteenth nothing happened at all, and that is the last beat of it and it is not a small one. Nothing in this city is scheduled for a Sunday. No Assembly and no committee and no guild counter and no water committee and no mutual sits on a Sunday, and the reason is not constitutional, it is that the people who do the sitting do not turn up on a Sunday, and that is the same reason there is no counter in this city that has ever refused anybody anything. Six people in a room over a chandler's shop found a date in November and a working method and two operators who will not sign, and on the Sunday the thirteenth of the eleventh month, which is a Sunday in the month in which the rehearsal is set to be run, there was nothing to do and nowhere to go and no form on which to say so, and three people who had been in a room over a chandler's shop two nights earlier went to bed, and one of them did not sleep well, and none of the three of them has said why to anybody.**
