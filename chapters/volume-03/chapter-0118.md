# Chapter 118 — Sunday, Porter and Clerk

On Friday the seventeenth of the third month Orrin read the council's answer where it had already been read, in the room over the chandler's shop in Bellweather Road, standing up, at about ten in the morning, on a Friday, because the minute was on a shelf with the others and the shelf was not locked, and a book on an open shelf is a thing anybody may read and nobody has to be in the room for.

Corvin Hale had been in that room at ten that morning and had not stayed ten minutes, and Orrin knew that because the cup on the table was still warm, and because Hale is a man who reads a thing standing up and then goes and does something with it, and the something he did with this one was to be in the Weirs tenant council's rooms before nine in the morning on the day the council sat, alone, asking nobody anything and being asked nothing, which is what happens to a man who is refused in public and finds out that a refusal can be read off a shelf by anybody at all.

Then Orrin walked down to the Weigh Lane end and along the arcade, which was open because it trades on a Tuesday and a Friday, and where there were fourteen tokens out on the tray and the rate card was fresh with the same four figures on it in chalk that had been on it in January, and a man and two women were standing at the end of the row reading the board, which is a thing that did not happen in January and now happened on most days, and nobody at the end of the row had been told about a room in Hobden Street with a hole in its window and eleven pounds of second-hand clocks in it, and Orrin had not told them and was not going to.

Hale had the council's minute face up on the counter and he squared it with two fingers of his left hand, and then he turned it round so the shelf it had come off could not be seen, and neither of those things was sentiment and both of them were the same man.

"She has put a man's whole trade in three lines and told me there is nobody to carry it to," he said, "and in eleven months I am going to put a question to Bellweather Road out of my own room, and she will not be able to show me a person, and neither will I be able to show her one, and that is a thing I did not have in January and I have been able to do business with it every day since, and you should understand that a broker who is told there is no procedure is not a broker who is stopped. He is a broker who knows exactly what there is."

And then he walked up Weigh Lane to the guild hall and went up the steps to the licensing floor, which is the same steps in every direction from the Cathedral and is not the same building, and he had been on them in the first year with a determination in four true lines in his coat and had come away with a licence he could not use and a term nobody had shortened.

The licensing floor was the same: a counter, a queue of two, the die drawer in its case behind the counter, the plate with the cut figures on it, and the monthly list on the wall with about nineteen names on it of men who had certified something in the last month. His own name was not on the monthly list and had not been since the first of the sixth and could not be, and looking at it was the only thing on that floor he had wanted to do and he did not do it.

He asked for the renewal of a class-three licence whose term ends on the nineteenth of the third month, which was the day after tomorrow, and the clerk behind the counter was a man of about thirty-four called Hask's opposite number in every respect and had a Monday-to-Friday face and did not look up until Orrin had finished.

"Suspended the first of the sixth," the clerk said. "Term ends the nineteenth. So you want to lodge."

"I want to lodge."

"You are suspended, and a suspended man may lodge for determination, and the determination is not a renewal, and I am obliged to say the word determination out loud because the last man who had this counter said *renewal* and I would rather it was not in the room." He turned a sheet round. "Fee, four shillings and sixpence. Date, here. The endorsement of one master, and it has to be a master and it has to be in the box marked *master*, and it does not have to be in this hall, and it does not have to be the master who suspended you, and there is a man in Bellweather Road over a shop who has been a first class for twelve years and who is not going to be delighted."

"I know. I've got the book."

"The book is hall form W-4?"

"I've got the book."

The clerk looked at him for slightly longer than a counter requires and said, "Three columns."

"Fault observed, work performed, recommendation."

"The third one's wider."

"The third one's wider," said Orrin, "and there is no column for *I am not sure* and there never will be, and I have been putting *I am not sure* in it four times in two months and it is the only column in this city that has anything in it, and I would like that noted while I am standing at this counter, and you may refuse to note it and I would think no worse of you."

"Noted nowhere," said the clerk, "and refused, and here is your receipt for the fee, and here is the date, and here is what you must understand about it: your term ends on the nineteenth, and on the twentieth you have no licence, and there is no arrangement in this building by which you have one on the nineteenth, and I have told a man in this city that in a fortnight and he did not believe me and he was not being rude, he was not able to hold the two things at once."

"I understand."

"Everybody understands it," said the clerk. "It is understanding it in a room in two days' time that is the difficult part, and I am not going to pretend I can help you with that, and there is nobody in this building who can."

He put a date card in front of him and it said the twentieth of the third month, and under it, in a different hand, in a different pen, on a slip of paper that was not part of the form: three lines.

**That panel will sit and its determination will be provisional, and these are the three conditions proposed, and they are proposed and not settled, and a man may be heard on them.** *1. He may not certify a district toll. 2. He may not enter a sealed room without a person named who may refuse it. 3. He may not be alone with a machine he has not been asked about.*

Orrin read the three lines four times in the queue behind him and put the slip in his coat and went out into Weigh Lane and stood on the step of the hall.

The third condition was the hall's. The first was the hall's: it was the reason he was standing at that counter at all, and the hall had every right to put it back on him for ever, and any man who said otherwise was a man who had never been suspended.

The second was not the hall's.

The third was not the hall's.

Nobody in Verge Lane had ever heard of it, and he was the only man in the city who had heard it, and it had been said in a corridor on the twenty-fourth of the second month by a woman of fifty-eight standing with her back against a door, and she had said it to him and not to the hall and not to a form, and he had put it in his own book, and the hall had somehow got it out of a box in about three weeks.

The first thing he did on the Saturday the eighteenth was go and tell her.

He went up Cathedral Rise at eleven in the morning with no lamp and let the porter let him in, and Miss Cray was on the landing outside the north door with the key ring in her hand, as though she had been waiting there and had a reason for it, and she was not surprised, which told him a good deal about the building.

"You have come to tell me something," she said.

"I have come to tell you that two of the three conditions a hall has put on me are yours, and they are the second and the third, and they are in a slip of paper dated the seventeenth of March, and I did not send them to them, and I have not spoken to any of them in about a year."

"Then they have got them from a box, and they will have got them from a form, and a form is a way of asking without the rule, and you have just told me that I have been put in a form without being asked, which is correct, and I would like to be left out of it."

"They have named you?"

"They have not named me and they may not name me," said Miss Cray. "If my name goes into a condition then I am the mechanism, and a mechanism is a thing a safeguard is written to stop, and I am not a safeguard and I am not an instrument and I am a person of fifty-eight in a brown coat who can refuse you. Those are two different jobs and they pay differently and only one of them has ever been any use to anybody. If I am named, then one day somebody will say *the registrar was satisfied*, and I will have been satisfied once, in a corridor, about one man, on one morning, and it will be a condition, and a condition is a sentence with a door in it and nobody is ever asked to come through the door."

"I could say on the record that they are yours and not theirs."

"You will not," said Miss Cray, "because as soon as they are in a hall's conditions they are the hall's, and then in about four years somebody will change the wording to *he shall not be left alone with a machine* and drop the *that he has not been asked about*, and the day they drop those seven words is the day a man in a uniform is standing in a room with a woman in a bed and neither of them can make anybody leave, and the six words will be gone, and I will be a woman who once said something in a corridor to a clockmaker."

"That is a very good argument for a woman I am not permitted to make."

"It is not an argument," said Miss Cray. "It is a reason. I have made four arguments in nineteen years in this building and I have lost three of them and I do not make them any more. Do not tell them anything. Tell them the conditions are theirs and let them be theirs, and if you are half a decent tradesman you will be able to live inside a condition you did not write, because you have been living inside one written by a hall since the first of June and you have not once asked to be let out of it."

He walked back down Cathedral Rise with the slip in his coat and the four feet of stone worn into a bowl in the middle of the fourth tread, and he stood at the bottom of it for a while and then walked up Bellweather Road as far as the printing shop in the middle of it, where a woman of about thirty-eight was stacking two hundred and forty identical sheets into a bundle with a strap, and he carried the last eleven of them up a stair with her and was not thanked and did not look for it, and the minute she was carrying said that a refusal stops nothing, and he had read it on Wednesday.

On Saturday the notice run went out, two hundred and forty copies on a Saturday afternoon, of a minute that had cost a body with four entitled members three days and had produced nothing anybody could use, which Wenna Pruitt said was the correct ratio and which nobody in Bellweather Road said anything about at all.

---

The nineteenth of the third month was a Sunday, and a licensing counter in this city takes lodgements on a Sunday, and has for as long as anybody can remember, and the reason is not generosity. It is that a counter which can be found on a Sunday is a counter that cannot be shut, and a counter that cannot be shut is a counter that has to be found open every other day as well, which is a burden, and the burden is the point.

The porter unlocked it at a quarter to nine. He was about sixty and had been a porter in that building for twenty-two years and he unlocked the licensing door and the outer door and nothing else, and the corridor between the two doors was dark, and the counter had one lamp on it and the lamp was the clerk's own and had come out of his own house in a van.

There was nobody else in the building. Orrin had thought about that on Saturday night and had not been able to make it mean anything, and on Sunday morning at a quarter to nine he stood in a corridor with a porter who had opened two doors and gone back down, and the only sound in the building was a gas lamp, and he understood that the sentence about a door a man can walk through meant this, and had not meant a corridor.

The clerk was a woman of about forty. She said good morning and asked for the fee and the date and the endorsement and the book, and he gave her all four, and she counted the four shillings and sixpence into a tin and gave him a receipt with a date on it, and then she said the thing that made the whole morning worth the walk.

"You have lodged for a determination and not for a renewal," she said, "and I have written *determination* on the card, and I want you to know that I have written it that way on purpose and that if I had written *renewal* then in about four years there would be a card in this building with your name on it and a word on it that nobody in it can explain, and there would be no way of taking the word off, because words on cards in this building have never been taken off anything."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me, I am the counter and I am on a Sunday because the counter is on a Sunday," said the clerk, "and there is a porter in this building and the two of us and that is the whole of the establishment, and I have done this about two hundred times and I have never once been asked anything and I have never once been thanked and I have never once wanted to be, and I am telling you that because a man who thinks Sunday at a counter is a favour will come on a Monday and expect something, and this is not a favour, it is a door, and you should be able to tell the difference by now."

He could tell the difference by now, and he had written an answer about it in a letter a month ago, and it had come back in four lines with one sentence in it that was better than the answer he wanted, and he did not say any of that, and she did not want to hear it.

"Nothing is certified today," she said. "Nothing can be. There is no panel in this building today and there is no officer in it but me. Your term ends at midnight tonight and not at four o'clock this afternoon, and if you were to walk into a ward tomorrow morning and certify a bracket clock you would be doing it without one, and there is a man in this city who did exactly that in the first year and it is why there is a form with a determination on it in this building at all."

"I have certified nothing since the first of June."

"I know you have not," said the clerk. "It is on the monthly list that you have not and the list is public and it is a wall with nineteen names on it and yours is not one of them, and I have looked at it every month of the year and I have never said anything to you about it and I am not going to, because saying something would be a favour and it is not a favour, it is a wall."

He walked out into Weigh Lane at twenty past nine on a Sunday morning with a receipt for four shillings and sixpence and a slip of paper in his coat with three conditions on it that he had not written and could not now disown.

It was not a licence. It was a card with a date on it and a word on it, and the word was *determination*, and the date was the twentieth, and the three conditions were the hall's, and he had one day and one night of the whole of his standing in this city left, and he had spent it on a form at a counter on a Sunday morning with a clerk and a porter and nobody else, and the Weir Two branch was three hours and five minutes out behind him, and there were nineteen names on a wall on the third floor and his was not one of them.
