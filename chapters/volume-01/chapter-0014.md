# Chapter 14 — Behind a Closed Door

The hall's back office was a corridor with a fire bucket in it and a door at the end that said, in the guild's own type, *certification series — no entry without the secretary*, and the door was shut, and a light was burning under it.

The guild's secretary was sixty-one years old and had a face like a shut drawer and a stack of spectacles on a chain and a black binder held under her arm that she did not put down, and she looked at the notice of renewal in his hand for about four seconds and then went past him into the corridor.

"Mr Vale," she said, without slowing. "The work book is not in that office. You want the series room."

"I want a number looked up," Orrin said.

"Then you want the series room, and I have a fire bucket on my corridor, and you may carry the bucket out yourself and come back in."

The series room was eight feet by eleven and there were four sets of shelves in it and a single lamp and no window, and the shelves went up past the lamp, and everything in the room was in the same brown binding. It smelled of sizing and mouse and forty years of a room that is opened on request and shut again.

"I am going to shut the door," said Della Marsh, "and I am going to ask you a question, and if you lie to me I shall stop, and you will not know that I have stopped, and you will go away and think that a woman of my age is slow. Do you understand what I have just told you?"

"Yes."

"Good." She shut it. "Now. Which number."

"GH-9. Standing withdrawal, maintenance-deferred class. Eighteen years ago, the storm month, batch twenty-one slash four, Weirs series, forty-one entries, countersigned for the affected district by a deputy pumping warden."

Della Marsh put the binder down on the desk and took her spectacles off and cleaned them, which took longer than it needed to.

"You have had a very good month," she said.

---

She did not make him pay first. She made him stand there while she found it, which turned out to be a different kind of cost, because it meant watching a competent woman do careful work in a room he was not permitted to help in.

The series was kept by form number, and the form numbers ran in decades, and she went to the middle of the fourth shelf and took down a volume and opened it on the desk and turned it round to face him, and the entry was four lines long, and it was the same entry, in the same four lines, that Sindri Lake had put a finger on in a warm room on Cathedral Rise ten days ago.

*Eighteen years past, the storm month. Batch 21/4, Weirs series, forty-one entries. Maintenance-deferred class, form GH-9. Requesting hall. Standing withdrawal, priced at the hall's schedule for the class. Countersigned for the affected district.*

"Forty-one entries," she said. "You will have the price. Two shillings the head for a year is the class rate, and one shilling where a man has already been paid once, and it has not moved in nine years, and I could tell you the derivation of the class rate out of the price book if you had a long afternoon and a reason to want it."

"I'd like it."

"You would not." She turned a page back, one leaf. "That is the transfer. What you have come for is the copybook, and everybody who comes for a number ends up at the copybook eventually, and I would rather you got there by yourself than that I walked you."

The copybook was a working duplicate, kept because the series volumes were the hall's record and the copybook was the hall's working: a foolscap book with a limp cover, a thousand pages, filled in a small upright hand that got smaller as it went, the way a hand does when the person using it has been at the same page a long time.

It was not a list of numbers. It was a list of sites.

"Show me the Weirs."

She turned to a marker, and there it was, in the same hand, a ruled line per site per year, four columns: the site, the class, the number of the batch, and the head count, and nothing else. No names. That was the point of it, and it was the most frightening thing Orrin had found in a month.

*Weir Two, second stage, night pumping, maintenance-deferred, batch 21/4, 1 crew, 3 nights.*

*Weir Two, second stage, night pumping, maintenance-deferred, batch 21/4, 1 crew, 3 nights.*

The same line twice, in two years. And under it, with a different marker, a thin gap, and then nine more years of the same line.

"Eleven times," Orrin said.

"It is the same crew. It is a standing arrangement. When the hall withdraws a standing, the request comes up for renewal each year, because a standing withdrawal is only a withdrawal for the year of the request, and a crew that is not certified in year two is a crew that has never been certified." She turned the page. "And before you ask: yes, that is what it means. It means a man on a night pump at Weir Two has not been inside the model since the year before the Long Nine, and the reason is a line in a book in this room, and the line has been copied eleven times by a clerk who did not know what it meant, because it is a form number and a batch and a head count and there is no column on this page for a man and it was never intended to have one."

She turned two pages and put her finger on another one.

"Northglass. A boardinghouse on the Kestrel side, a stair and a landing light. Maintenance-deferred, batch 22/1, one batch of nine, six years ago." Her finger moved. "The Sloughs. A pumping station at the road end, out past the last house, maintenance-deferred, batch 22/1, two batches, six and eleven. And a hospital generator, and I am not going to tell you which hospital, because I have written that page out about nine hundred times and I have never once been asked, and if I am asked I will say that the hall holds a record of machines and not a record of the buildings the machines stand in."

"Nine districts in this book?"

"Nine series, and I will tell you something for nothing, because you have been decent about waiting." She closed the cover with one finger keeping the place. "Eight of the nine are a pumping station or a stair or a landing light or a night gate. One of the nine is not. That is why you are frightened, and you are right to be, and I have watched three men in this room be frightened for the wrong reason and one of them be frightened for the right one, and you are the fourth, and you are the first one to come in here without wanting to catch anybody."

Orrin stood very still.

"Why am I frightened of that?"

"Because you came in with a form number for a dead man in a well." She took her spectacles off. "You want it to be one shop and one family and one bad year. And it is one form, and one price schedule, and one hall, and it is nine districts, and a hall with a form number and a price schedule and nine districts is not a crime, Mr Vale, it is a *practice*, and practices do not get exposed. They get found out by somebody with a licence, and then they get a column, and then they get priced differently, and then everybody in the nine does the arithmetic and notices that they are all on the same page." She put the spectacles in her apron. "You have just made eight districts' worth of people into people who have been on the same page as a man who drowned. And the person who drowned has a daughter on the six o'clock tram."

That was the sentence, and it was the whole of the chapter, and Orrin did not have an answer to it then or afterwards.

---

"You did not ask me for anything yet," Della Marsh said. "I have noticed. I have been doing this for thirty-one years and I have a fairly exact sense of the shape of a transaction and you have been standing in my room for forty minutes admiring my handwriting."

"I have no authority to pay you for anything," Orrin said. "And anything you showed me is hall property and I can't use it."

"You can use it in this room. That is what a room with a shut door is for. It is the only use it has." She sat down on the edge of the desk, which was not a thing a guild secretary did. "Now. You want a fee, because you are right that you cannot pay me in a receipt, and a receipt would be a document, and a document is something somebody could find, and what you want is the opposite of a document."

"Four shillings," Orrin said. "In coin. And no receipt, and I do not write it down, and if anybody asks me where the number came from I say the public Archive room on Cathedral Rise on the twentieth, which is true, and which is where a man with a stamped transfer entry could have got it, and I will not be lying when I say it."

Della Marsh looked at him for a while.

"That is the first sensible thing anybody has said to me in this corridor in about nine years," she said. "Four shillings. Not now — I have a rule and the rule is a week, and the day is named, and it is a week today, because a thing paid for the same day is a thing bought in a mood, and you will go home tonight and be a better man about this tomorrow and cheaper. And I will tell you why a week, and then I will not explain it: a week is long enough for you to decide whether you actually want it and short enough that you cannot decide it is somebody else's business." She got off the desk. "You will come at the same time and you will put the coin on the table and you will not say a word, and I will not say a word, and neither of us will know the other's face by the end of the year, and that is the correct arrangement and I have had it eleven times."

"Then I will come at half past three."

"A week today. Half past three, because the licensing floor clears at three and I do not like a man in my corridor when there are forty of them on it." She opened the door on the fire bucket. "Mr Vale. One thing first, because you have not asked it and you will ask it, and I would rather answer it than watch you work it out badly.

"The book. The one on the top shelf, the big one, the one with the columns." She had not gone through the door. "You have got a form number out of me in a shut room for four shillings and you think the next step is the other one. So: the labour book is a different animal and it lives on the licensing floor on an open shelf, and it is a book of machines, and the hall's position is that anybody in the public may walk up and read it, and the hall's position is also that forty years of form says *presented to the licensing desk* and there is no form that says *photocopied*, because there is no such thing as a photocopy and there is a man eleven doors down with a lantern and a wet sheet who will copy anything you like for a penny a side and is not a part of this building.

"Three doors to it. The first: the investigator requisitions it. It is a guild record, it is requisitionable, and it will come in about fourteen months, and I am telling you fourteen and not a year because I have watched two of them come in at eleven and I do not want to hear you say you were told a year.

"The second: a licensed maintainer has had access to it in the course of his work and copies it for his own records. There is no rule against that either. There is, however, a line on the inside cover of the book for a maintainer's name, and it is a line about a copy made in a hall and kept, and it goes in a file, and it will be found in about eleven weeks by a man who is fond of you, and it will be found either in the hall or in your own shop, and I cannot tell you which is worse and neither can anybody else in this building.

"The third." She looked at him. "The third is a person with no licence, because there is no form to fill in and no name to put on a line, and who goes into that hall in the afternoon like any other person in the public and reads a book off a shelf, and who is therefore not declaring anything, because declaring requires a form and a form requires a person the hall can send a letter to. Do you see how that one works?"

"Yes."

"Then do not be shocked when somebody does it, because it is the most obvious thing in the world and it is the only one of the three that nobody can stop by writing a rule. It also happens in about four days, because they have been talking about it since the seventeenth and I have been in this corridor for all of it. You did not tell them to and you will not be able to say that you did. Now: two things, and the second one is free, and I would like you to take it because you have been decent and because I do not have anybody else to give it to.

"One. The guild publishes a liability annex. It is upstairs, in the press, and it is about the night of the Long Nine, and the hours that stopped, and there is a figure in it that I have stamped the top sheet of since the year I came in here. I have never been asked about it, not by one person in thirty-one years, and I am not going to tell you the figure, and if you ask me again I will tell a worse answer than this one. I am telling you it exists because you have found the copybook and it is better that you know there is one more piece of paper in this building than that you find out in a worse room.

"Two." She stood in the doorway. "You have not slept. You have been to the Weirs and to the Assembly and to this room on six days out of seven and you did not take your coat off in the investigator's office on Friday, which everybody notices and nobody mentions. Your ear is doing that thing where you turn your head further than you need to. And I am not your friend and I am not your master and I have known you since you were twenty-two, and I am going to say one thing about it and then I am not going to say anything else about it ever, and I would like it noted that nobody has ever thanked me for anything, not once, in thirty-one years, and that this is not a complaint, it is a measurement, and I would like you to measure it before you go."

He waited.

"The book you are keeping," she said, "is the only copy of about half of what has happened in your district this year, and it is in a shop with a lease, and if anything happens to you it goes in a bin with the shop, and then the whole of it will be a thing a woman in the Weirs says to a committee and a woman on a rota says she did not understand." She shut the door on him. "There is a kitchen in Green Span with a lamp in it until about six, and there is a woman in it who works nights, and I have watched you for two weeks and you have not once gone and been told a true thing by somebody who is not paid to tell you one."
