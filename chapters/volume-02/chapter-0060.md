# Chapter 60 — Ten Days, Four Places, and Two Dates He Set Himself

The wave did not arrive as a wave. It arrived as four things on four days in a district of a hundred and forty thousand people, and by the fourth day there were four words on everybody's mouth in the Weirs, and the four words were *outside the reliable model*, and everybody in the district had got them from a piece of paper on a wall, and a wall is a slow instrument and it is the best one anybody in this city owns.

---

What the ten days were, taken in order, with the times:

**The twenty-seventh of the sixth, night.** Weir Two, second stage started twice, the gland at about ten to twelve, the front at about thirty-one pounds, nine Weir Lane four feet by half past three. That is the twenty-seventh and it has a night book in it with nine names in it.

**The second of the seventh, Saturday, about four in the afternoon.** The gate at Weir One is clock-driven off the branch and it opened at about ten to seven instead of about five, and the front of Weir One was short for two hours and ten minutes, and there are nineteen households off that gate and about half of them are on the second court where the standpipe is. Ivy Pike signs for that gate. She did not report it because she has never in eleven years reported a thing that came back on its own, and this one came back on its own at about ten to nine, and she wrote the two hours and ten minutes in her own book at the time because she writes everything in her own book, and she has never shown that book to anybody and she is sixty-one.

**The fifth of the seventh, Tuesday, about eleven at night.** The standpipe at the corner of the second court ran dry for about four hours and came back on, and the woman who has a bucket and a shawl over her head was there for all of it because she was there for the water on the ninth of the sixth, and this time somebody had come. Nobody could say why it had come back. The district works' return for the fifth of the seventh says *no defect found*, which is a true sentence and the most expensive four words in the district's file.

**The sixth of the seventh, Wednesday, about seven in the morning.** A timer on the new circuit on the third floor of the Green Span hospital, eleven feet from the lift shaft, skipped and lost eleven seconds and did not recover and did not know. It was found at about four in the afternoon by a woman of forty-four who has been the hospital's engineer for twenty-two years, and it was eleven seconds slow, and it is on a wall.

Nobody died. Nobody was hurt. **That is not a mercy. It is a bad fortnight with nobody hurt in it**, and a ward's whole slack is forty minutes and the branch is an hour and twelve minutes and forty seconds out, and a man of sixty-eight with a bucket is the only reason there is nothing else to say.

---

**The addendum went up on the two boards on the Friday the eighth of the seventh month at about four in the afternoon**, and it is one paragraph, and it is in the Cathedral's own type, and it is the shortest public document in Morrowfen.

> **ADDENDUM TO THE STATEMENT OF DISTRICT RELIABILITY, LOWER WEIRS PUMPING DISTRICT, tenth day of the seventh month.**
> That three occasions of clock and draw irregularity are recorded in the district between the twenty-seventh day of the last month and the seventh day inst.; that all three are consistent with the condition described in the statement of the thirteenth of the last month; that the condition is the condition; and that no answer returned in respect of this district is a forecast of conditions in it, and none is to be treated as one.
> *Signed for the Directorate: A. Voss, Director of the Cathedral.*

Nobody in the Lower Weirs had ever seen the name before. It is on a return and it is a signature, and there is no offer in it, and nobody in the Lower Weirs expects one and none is coming for a long time, and the whole of what it means to a man standing at a ward board on the Friday afternoon of the eighth of the seventh month is that a woman he has never met has read a paragraph about his district and agreed with the district's own account of itself.

Bevin Sarr read it twice and then said one thing to about six people, and it is in the night book that night because somebody wrote it down.

"Right," said Bevin Sarr. "Then that is the best that has ever happened. She has not told us we are wrong. **She has agreed that the numbers on the wall are real, in writing, under her own hand, and put them on a board in a public street.** I have wanted that for eleven years and I have never once asked for it because I did not know it could be had.

"And she has also told us that being real is no use to us, in the same breath, on the same sheet, in the same afternoon. And both of those are her job. And I want it noticed, in this ward, tonight, that those two things arrived in one paragraph and it took nine years and a pump house wall to arrange it, and that whoever finally gets a better answer than this will be standing on top of a road we built, and if they ever cut the road we will not have a district."

---

**And then on the Tuesday the eleventh of the seventh month, at about ten in the morning, a clockmaker with no licence went up Cathedral Rise and asked at the Question Hall counter what a district standing would have to be.**

He had a piece of paper in his coat with six figures on it and a second piece with a form number on it, and he had got up at half past six and been on the hill since nine, and the counter is a public counter and it takes a question in about four minutes.

The clerk was about twenty-six and had been there two years and was extremely good, and she did not know anything about pumps or cellars or hourlines, and she took him through it in a completely level voice in about eleven minutes, and at no point did she treat him as a small man with a large opinion, which is the single professional courtesy this building extends and which he had been told in his first year he could not expect.

"A district standing under the water act," she said, "is created by an instrument, lodged by a body, and entered by the clerk of the water authority. There are three requirements and I am going to give them to you because you have asked the right question and nobody has asked me the right question in about two years.

"**One:** a body. A standing is a written claim and a written claim has to be made by something a room will accept as the party. A district is not a body. A district is a list of properties, and the water act does not know a district exists.

"**Two:** an instrument. That is a standing withdrawal and reinstatement, the guild's series, and it is priced at two shillings the head for a year and one shilling where a man is the occupier, and I know the price because it is printed on the counter card and because half my work is that card.

"**Three:** the person. A station on the rail takes a person of the affected district, or a person named in a district standing. **If there is no standing, the station is filled by somebody from anywhere, and it has been filled from Green Span and Northglass for about twenty years, and every one of those Readings was lawful.**"

"That is a great deal of help," said Orrin Vale, "and I have got to tell you that I have already paid the two shillings the head and I did it in May and I did not know what it was for, and I would like to know whether it was for this."

---

And she asked him one question, and it is the only question anybody asked that day which had an answer, and she asked it because the answer was on the counter card in front of her and because she is good at her job.

"Sir, whose heads did you enter?"

"The Weir Two night crew. Forty-one of them. On the first of the fifth month. Eighty-two shillings, my own money, at the guild's counter, in four minutes, on a standing withdrawal and reinstatement, series GH-9."

She wrote that down, and then she said: "Then that is a return, and I am very sorry, and I have got to tell you what the difference is and I am going to tell you the way I would say it to a clerk.

"That form puts a person's hours into the district's **return**, and a return is made on for rating and for levying the water rate, and it is a list of properties and of the persons chargeable in respect of them, and it is what your ward's rates are made on, and it is what your sister's rent went up on in October.

"It is not a party to a question. It cannot be. A return is a document and a document cannot be a party, and if a return could stand on that rail then every rate return in this city could and I would not have a job and neither would the water authority. **A form is not a body. That is the whole of it.**"

---

He stood on the step of the Question Hall for about six minutes.

**A form is not a body.**

**A form is not a room.** He has written that down four times in a grey book in six months, and it has cost him a fault report, a question, a district and a rate, and he wrote it in the second month of the year about a copyist, and in the fourth month about a fault observed on a civic machine going into a hall's own form with nobody able to refuse it, and in the twenty-seventh of May about a sheet of foolscap on four boards with his own name at the bottom of it, and in every one of those four the form was full of true information and there was nobody in the room who could say no.

And the thing he has spent five months believing about the eighty-two shillings — that it was the one instrument he had that nobody could take back, the one private act in a city of forms — is a document.

A document is worth nothing at a rail. A document is worth nothing in a room. A document is what you have when you have nothing else, and a man who has put eighty-two shillings of his own money into one and told nobody has got a receipt and a secret.

He got his book out on the step of the Question Hall and did the sum, because he is that sort of man and because he had it in his head already and wanted it written down in a hand.

> *Eleventh of the seventh. Two dates, and I have been doing them as one for a fortnight, and they are three months apart and they are both mine, and only one of them is the day anybody could use against me.*
>
> *The licence. Suspended on the first of the sixth, term unaltered, expires the nineteenth of the third month next year. From tonight, two hundred and fifty-one days. Eight months and eight days, and thirty-five weeks and six days. I have written that date down four times already and it is the date I do not need, because on that day I may go back to a counter and sign my name for a fee, which is not a thing I want, and there is no version of me that is glad of it and I do not know what I am working for.*
>
> *The form. GH-9, forty-one heads, lodged the first of the fifth, a year from the date of lodgement. It runs out on the first of the fifth next year. From tonight, two hundred and ninety-four days. On that day those forty-one men fall out of the ward's standing again with no hearing and no vote, and the hall's four lines become true again about them, and nobody in that hall will ever have noticed that they were not true for a year, and I will be the only person in Morrowfen who knows, and I have already been that person once, and it was not a privilege.*

And then, in the same book, immediately, in the same pen:

> *And the thing I have not put down because I did not want to, and I am putting it down now because Bevin Sarr is right and I have had five months of being right at a counter and it was worth nothing each time.*
> *The eighty-two shillings was never the instrument. It never was. It is a return, and it is a rate, and it is a woman of twenty-six's name on a list of properties, and it is not a party to anything.*
> *The instrument is a **body**. And there is one in the Lower Weirs and it has been there the whole time, and it had four pounds ten in a year and spent every shilling of it on an appendix in the fourth month, and it has a room over a chandler's shop and a minute book that nobody can produce in a court, and it has never once applied for anything, and the reason is that a thing which applies becomes a party's legal personality, and that council has spent two years refusing to be a single thing on purpose, and I would have said the same thing in February.*

He stood on the step for a while longer with the book shut.

---

**At about one o'clock on the same day he went to the district works' counter on the canal road and asked a man of about thirty, who has been at that counter eleven years, a question with one word in it.**

"Ambrose. If I wanted a body to lodge an instrument, and I am not lodging one, and I am asking, who would have to say yes."

Ambrose did not look up from the counter for about four seconds and then he said, "The council, or a mutual, and there are three mutuals on this side and one of them has about nine members and meets when there is a drain, and the answer to your question is that the council could and would not and one of the mutuals could and would not either and the reason is the same in both cases, and the reason is that whichever one of them lodges the first instrument becomes the district's representative in a room where the district is not allowed to speak, and that is a trade and they know the price of it and none of them has got it, and I have watched three offices try to get one of them to sign something in nine years and I have watched all three of them go away.

"You are the first man in nine years who has asked me that question without a form in his hand."

"I have got a notebook," said Orrin Vale.

"Aye," said Ambrose. "Well. I have got a counter, and I have never once been asked what I thought about any of it, and there is a sheet in front of me with your name not on it, and I am going to say one thing and then I am going to go back to work. **If anybody in that council ever says yes, it will be on a day when the water has already gone wrong and not before, and there is no version of this where they say yes in advance, and any man who tells you they will is selling something.**"

---

**He went to find Ivo Pell on the Tuesday the eleventh, at about half past three in the afternoon, and he walked the whole way, and it is about four and a half miles, and it took him until about five.**

The four clauses they agreed at the top of the steps at one o'clock on the nineteenth of the sixth said that Mr Pell would come and look at what Mr Vale had written down, at a time of his own choosing, about once in a fortnight. It is eight days into a fortnight, and a fortnight is the space the four clauses were written for, and the arrangement does not require a reason, and the entire mechanism is a piece of paper with a date on it and no signature on it.

He found him at the Northglass end of the Green Span line, on a platform, with a case of instruments that is not his and a coat that is.

"I have not come to be taught," said Orrin Vale, on the platform, in the cold, with people going past. "I want to say that first because you said it once in a pump room and I have thought about it for four weeks and I am not here to be taught."

"You never are," said Ivo Pell. "That is not the difficulty. What is it."

"I need a person who can stand on a rail," said Orrin Vale. "That is all. That is the whole of it. I have got six figures on a wall in a pump house and a form number in a drawer and a card in a nurses' room and I cannot get a question put about any of it, and the reason I cannot get a question put is that the rail takes a person of the district and the district has never had a standing and there is nobody to put there, and the one thing I did about it in May cost eighty-two shillings and it was a return and it is not a party to anything, and I have found that out on a step this morning from a girl of twenty-six at a counter who did not know me and was extremely kind about it.

"And there are two operators in this city I can think of. One of them has countersigned nine certificates this month in a hall in the Sloughs and does not know a single one of them. And one of them has spent four weeks watching a man put things in a book and has said he is not going to teach him anything and has not said why, and I have got about eight months and twelve days and I have not got a second person."

Pell looked at him for a while. There was a train coming and neither of them looked at it.

"You have not asked me for anything," said Ivo Pell.

"No."

"Then I am going to tell you what I would have to do, and then you can go away and think about it, and I am not going to say yes to any of it this afternoon and I would take it as a kindness if you did not ask me again this week.

"To put a question about a district that has no standing, somebody with standing has to put it, and the somebody is an operator with a licence, and the operator has to be able to stand on a rail, and I can stand on a rail. That is not a favour. I am paid a hundred and forty pounds a year to be able to stand on a rail and it is the only thing in this city I am actually good at, and if you asked me tomorrow I would say yes, and in four months I would say yes, and I would be extremely good at it, and I would be extremely good at it in a way that you would not be able to see inside.

"**That is what an operator is.** We are the people a room believes. And a room believes us because we have spent twelve years not being wrong in public, and if I stand on a rail for a district that has not asked to be stood on, then there is a Reading, and a Reading has four parts, and every one of them is true, and the four of them together is a thing that happened to a district without anybody in the district having asked for it.

"And I would do it for you. I want that on the step. I would probably do it for you in about a fortnight if I had not made myself say all that, and the reason I would do it is that I am tired of being right about things in a building nobody elects, and you are the only man I have met who has put a number on a wall that he was not paid to put there. So I am not going to refuse you, and I am also not going to help you, and I would like you to be able to hold both of those in your hand at once, because in about three years you are going to need to."

He picked the case up.

"Give me a fortnight," he said. "And do the other thing first. **The other thing is a body. Get a body before you get an operator, and do not go and ask the council, go and ask a mutual, and take the worst one, and do not go back to it twice in a fortnight.** A body is slow and cheap and it can be refused and you have never once in your life had anybody refuse you, and if you get the operator first you will never be refused again as long as you live, and that is the condition this city is in and it is the whole of what is wrong with it."

He got on the train. He did not say whether it was the far-forgotten platform, and Orrin Vale stood on the platform for about four minutes afterwards with a notebook and a form number and eight months and twelve days, and the bell went and the doors shut on a rule about a mutual that he had not thought of, in a book, from a man who said he would not help him.
