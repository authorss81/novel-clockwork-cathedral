# Chapter 107 — A Letter With a Date On It

On Friday the third of the second month Corvin Hale wrote to the Weirs tenant council, in writing and with a date on it, asking whether he might ask the district about the drift of its clocks.

Orrin did not see the letter until Saturday the fourth, when Wenna Pruitt showed it to him in the tenant council's rooms over the chandler's shop in Bellweather Road, with Sel Kade at the table and Ivy Pike at the window and nobody else, because a letter with a date on it is a thing a body answers and not a thing a man carries round.

The letter was short, in a broker's plain hand, on arcade paper.

*To the Weirs tenant council, 3rd second. May I ask the district, on Pindar Row and elsewhere, what the drift of its clocks may be, for the purpose of my rate card, and may I put the answer on my board? C. Hale, No. 14.*

"It is polite," Wenna said. She was thirty-eight, organiser for nine years, not a member and never had been, because if she was a member she was inside the decision. "It names an act. It has a date. It asks a body that exists. Half the letters in this city do none of those."

Sel Kade read it twice. She was forty-four, a tenant-council representative since the seventh of the first month, on the body that held the new right, not chairing it. She had no water and no hours and was no longer the person who had to be asked, and she was the only person in the Weirs who could be asked about a market, because a tenant council can be asked about a room and a light on a stair and it cannot be asked one question about the water.

"What does the right say?" Ivy Pike said from the window. She was sixty-one, signed for a gate, chaired because a body that cannot be refused is not a body.

Wenna turned the minute book to the five clauses. The fourth was the one that made it a right and nobody had argued about it: the council might not put a question to the row either, the row got seven days, the row might refuse the council, and there was nowhere to carry either refusal.

"He is asking the council whether he may ask the district," Sel said. "The council may refuse. A refusal binds the person who asked and nobody else. It is not a form, not a resolution, not a vote. It is entered in the row book and published with the minutes."

"And if we refuse, will he stop?" Orrin said from the corner, where he was not a member and had not asked to be.

"He will stop asking us," Wenna said. "Whether he stops asking other people is not in our power and never was. A right to refuse is neither a safeguard nor a shortcut. A safeguard is a rule about what a question has to be. A shortcut is a way of asking it without the rule. Clause four is what makes it a right."

They gave him the seven days that a schedule gets, although the letter asked for nothing that needed seven days, because the terms said seven days and terms the district could be held to were the only terms worth having.

On Saturday the fourth Orrin walked Hale's pitch and found it shut, because the arcade trades on a Tuesday and a Friday and the Saturday pitch was shuttered and the rate card covered. He wrote that in the book with satisfaction: a market that shuts is a market that can be believed about closing.

Sunday the sixth he did nothing that needed a counter or a board, because no market sits on a Sunday. A market which runs on a Sunday has no day to close on, and a day to close on is the only thing a market has that a criminal market does not.

The council met on Friday the tenth, in daylight, with the stove lit, and refused, in its own words, and the refusal bound Hale and nobody else.

The minute, published where anybody might read it, ran:

*3rd second asked, 10th second answered. C. Hale may not put to this body, nor to any person of Sluice Row, any question about the clocks of that row, nor the row's water, nor any hour belonging to a person of the row. Refusal binds C. Hale and nobody else. No person thanked.*

Eleven days later, by the book, a token would be in a clock on that row. Nothing in February anticipated that and nothing in December was contradicted by it. Orrin wrote the refusal in the grey ledger with both dates on it and put nothing at the bottom, because a refusal that binds one man is the whole of the instrument, and the instrument was new in January and this was the first time anybody with money had tested it.

Teo came in that evening and read the entry over Orrin's shoulder and said the sentence about a list of addresses.

"A broker's day book with a buyer's street in it is a list of doors," he said, and went out, and nobody thanked him, and the bell rang twice in the cold.
